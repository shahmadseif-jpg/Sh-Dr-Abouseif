*A study of a word that joins worship and petition, and of the Qur'an's pausing at "he called out" (nādā) where we expect "he supplicated" (daʿā). The article follows the root d-ʿ-w through the lexicons and the commentaries, from Zechariah's whispered call to Jonah's confession in the darkness.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

An old man, whose bones have grown frail and whose head is ablaze with white, stands before his Lord with no one beside him. He does not raise his voice or prolong it; he lowers it until none but God can hear: ﴿إِذْ نَادَىٰ رَبَّهُ نِدَاءً خَفِيًّا﴾ "when he called upon his Lord with a hidden call" (19:3). Then the Qur'an unveils what he said: ﴿قَالَ رَبِّ إِنِّي وَهَنَ الْعَظْمُ مِنِّي وَاشْتَعَلَ الرَّأْسُ شَيْبًا وَلَمْ أَكُن بِدُعَائِكَ رَبِّ شَقِيًّا﴾ "He said, 'My Lord, my bones have grown frail and my head is ablaze with white, and I have never been unfortunate in calling upon You, my Lord'" (19:4). Consider the courtesy in it: he began with his weakness, not with his request, and closed with a testimony to his Lord, not to himself, as though saying: You have never accustomed me, my Lord, to anything but that I call upon You and You answer. This is the same Zechariah, peace be upon him, of whom the Qur'an says elsewhere: ﴿هُنَالِكَ دَعَا زَكَرِيَّا رَبَّهُ ۖ قَالَ رَبِّ هَبْ لِي مِن لَّدُنكَ ذُرِّيَّةً طَيِّبَةً ۖ إِنَّكَ سَمِيعُ الدُّعَاءِ﴾ "There did Zechariah call upon his Lord. He said, 'My Lord, grant me from Yourself good offspring. Indeed, You are the Hearer of supplication'" (3:38). One scene, then, and three words: "he called out" (nādā), "he supplicated" (daʿā) and "supplication" (duʿāʾ). In that lies a secret we shall approach in this study, once we have understood what we mean by the word in the first place.

### A Root That Recurs Two Hundred and Twelve Times

According to the Quranic Arabic Corpus (corpus.quran.com), the root d-ʿ-w («د ع و») occurs two hundred and twelve times in the Qur'an, in seven forms[^1]: the verb of Form I (*daʿā* and its conjugations) one hundred and seventy times, and the verb of Form VIII (*yaddaʿī, taddaʿūn*) three times, so that the verbs together number one hundred and seventy-three. As for the nouns, *duʿāʾ* occurs twenty-two times, *daʿwa* six, *daʿwā* four, and the active participle *al-dāʿ* three times and *al-dāʿī* four. In the twenty-two there is a caveat that must not be lost: the Corpus list includes within it *adʿiyāʾikum* (33:4) and *adʿiyāʾihim* (33:37), which leaves *al-duʿāʾ* in the sense of the pure verbal noun at twenty, by my reckoning from the list.

This root runs in four directions in the Qur'an. It is the creature's supplication to God, as in "he called upon his Lord". It is his calling upon something other than God, as in "they call upon others besides God". It is the invitation of God and His messengers to humankind, as in ﴿ادْعُ إِلَىٰ سَبِيلِ رَبِّكَ بِالْحِكْمَةِ وَالْمَوْعِظَةِ الْحَسَنَةِ﴾ "Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good instruction" (16:125). And it is calling by naming, as in ﴿ادْعُوهُمْ لِآبَائِهِمْ﴾ "Call them by their fathers' names" (33:5). This diversity is no confusion; it is precisely what the people of the lexicon have said, so let us look at it.

### What the People of the Language Say

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī says: "*Al-duʿāʾ* is like *al-nidāʾ*, except that *al-nidāʾ* may be said with 'yā' or 'ayā' and the like without a name joined to it, whereas *al-duʿāʾ* is hardly ever said unless a name accompanies it, as in 'O so-and-so'; and each of the two may be used in place of the other." He then adds that it is used for naming; that when you have "called" someone (*daʿawtahu*) you have "asked him" or "sought his aid"; and that "*al-duʿāʾ ilā al-shayʾ* is urging toward aiming for it"[^2]. For him, then, *duʿāʾ* is a call joined to a name, that is, a call whose speaker knows whom he addresses. Ibn Fāris, for his part, returns the whole chapter to a single origin: "that you incline a thing toward yourself by a voice and speech that come from you"[^3]. It is as though the one who supplicates draws the one called toward himself; so when the one called is God, it is the drawing of His mercy by one's neediness, and when God is the one who calls, it is the drawing of the servant toward faith and obedience.

Note that al-Rāghib himself says that either of *al-duʿāʾ* and *al-nidāʾ* may be put in the place of the other. We should not, therefore, set a sharp boundary between them. On that footing we read what follows: an observation on particular passages, not a law of the language.

### When the Qur'an Says "He Called Out" and Not "He Supplicated"

Let us pause at three prophets who were tried with a particular distress and raised their complaint to their Lord, and we find that the Qur'an used for them the verb *nādā* ("he called out"). Zechariah: ﴿إِذْ نَادَىٰ رَبَّهُ نِدَاءً خَفِيًّا﴾ "when he called upon his Lord with a hidden call" (19:3). Job: ﴿وَأَيُّوبَ إِذْ نَادَىٰ رَبَّهُ أَنِّي مَسَّنِيَ الضُّرُّ وَأَنتَ أَرْحَمُ الرَّاحِمِينَ﴾ "And Job, when he called upon his Lord, 'Indeed, adversity has touched me, and You are the Most Merciful of the merciful'" (21:83). Jonah: ﴿فَنَادَىٰ فِي الظُّلُمَاتِ أَن لَّا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا أَنتَ سُبْحَانَكَ إِنِّي كُنتُ مِنَ الظَّالِمِينَ﴾ "And he called out within the darknesses, 'There is no god but You; exalted are You. Indeed, I have been of the wrongdoers'" (21:87). The root d-ʿ-w does not occur in any of these three passages[^4]. Rather, the root used is n-d-w, which the Corpus lists fifty-three times, forty-four of them in Form III (*nādā*).

Yet the picture does not run on a single pattern, for in Zechariah's own case both roots occur: "Zechariah called upon his Lord" in Āl ʿImrān (3:38), and in Maryam "he called out" and then "in calling upon You". The Qur'an, then, does not confine either word to one prophet over another. What I propose here is a reading of these three passages alone, not a verdict on the Arabic tongue nor on the whole Qur'an: it appears to me that *nādā* in these scenes brings out direct address, a man crying out his Lord's name from the depths of his distress, while the Qur'an afterwards sums up the scene under the name of *duʿāʾ* for the sense of petition and worship it carries. Perhaps what Ibn ʿĀshūr said about the verse in Maryam lends this understanding some support: "There is no contradiction between its being a call and its being hidden, for it is a call to the One who hears what is hidden"[^5]. The call here comes close to a whisper, because the One called is near. It is a hypothesis on which I have not run an exhaustive count of all the places of the two roots; let whoever wishes put it to the test.

### "Call Upon Me": Worship and Petition in One Verse

When we move to the pivotal verse of this chapter, the matter seems deeper: ﴿وَقَالَ رَبُّكُمُ ادْعُونِي أَسْتَجِبْ لَكُمْ ۚ إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَسْتَكْبِرُونَ عَنْ عِبَادَتِي سَيَدْخُلُونَ جَهَنَّمَ دَاخِرِينَ﴾ "And your Lord says, 'Call upon Me; I will respond to you. Indeed, those who disdain My worship will enter Hell in humiliation'" (40:60). A command to supplicate and a promise of response, then a threat against whoever "disdains My worship". The question that poses itself is: how did the verse make abandoning supplication a disdain of worship?

The commentators differed on this, and each opinion has a face. Al-Ṭabarī explains "call upon Me" as: "worship Me and be sincere to Me in worship", and reports from Ibn ʿAbbās: "affirm My oneness and I will forgive you"[^6]. Ibn Kathīr holds that God "urged His servants to call upon Him and took it upon Himself to answer them", and explains "My worship" as: "calling upon Me and affirming My oneness"[^7]. Al-Qurṭubī says: "This shows that supplication is worship, and so said most of the commentators", then relates a second view: "And it was said: it is remembrance, supplication and petition"[^8]. The views thus revolve between supplication as affirmation of oneness and worship, and supplication as petition.

Then Ibn ʿĀshūr joins the two meanings, saying: "Since the mention of supplication had preceded in both its senses, the sense of worship and the sense of asking for what is sought, this all-embracing command was added, covering both meanings", and he says that "call upon Me" is used "in both its senses by way of the generality of the homonym"[^9]. This view is the closest to what I incline toward, because it preserves the richness of the word: whoever asks his Lord for his need has worshipped Him, and whoever worships Him has asked Him, since worship in its essence is to display need and neediness. This is what the Prophet ﷺ made plain in the hadith of al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr: «الدعاء هو العبادة» "Supplication is worship", and then he recited the verse. It was narrated by Abū Dāwūd (1479) and al-Tirmidhī (3372), and al-Tirmidhī said: "a good, sound hadith"; it is also in his collection under tafsīr (2969)[^10].

Al-Qurṭubī has a subtle remark on the unrestricted promise in "I will respond to you": he relates from Khālid al-Rabʿī that it carries no condition, and relates that some took it as a case of the unrestricted read in light of the restricted, in the sense of "I will respond to you if I will", like His saying in Sūrat al-Anʿām: "and He removes that for which you call upon Him, if He wills"[^11]. We shall see this very disagreement return in another verse.

### "Indeed I Am Near": And the Word "Say" Is Absent

In Sūrat al-Baqara there is a verse that differs from its sisters in its construction: ﴿وَإِذَا سَأَلَكَ عِبَادِي عَنِّي فَإِنِّي قَرِيبٌ ۖ أُجِيبُ دَعْوَةَ الدَّاعِ إِذَا دَعَانِ ۖ فَلْيَسْتَجِيبُوا لِي وَلْيُؤْمِنُوا بِي لَعَلَّهُمْ يَرْشُدُونَ﴾ "And when My servants ask you about Me, indeed I am near. I respond to the call of the caller when he calls upon Me. So let them respond to Me and believe in Me, that they may be guided" (2:186). Look at the question: "My servants ask you about Me", then the answer at once: "indeed I am near". It did not say "say: indeed I am near". Ibn ʿĀshūr noticed this and said that it contains "the stripping of the answer of the word 'say', which is mentioned in the places of questioning in the Qur'an, such as 'They ask you about the new moons; say, they are measures of time'", and called it "a Qur'anic subtlety": "it conveys the impression that God Most High took upon Himself to answer their question Himself, since He omitted from the wording what indicates the mediation of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, to point to how close the servant is to his Lord in the station of supplication"[^12]. This does not require that the omission occurs nowhere else; what remains is that the commentator sees in this place a rhetorical secret, and that others whom I have read did not address it.

In lexical terms, the verse brings the root together three times, as I count from the Corpus list: *daʿwa*, *al-dāʿ* and *daʿāni*. It is as though the verse says: everything in it is supplication, from the one who calls to the One who answers. On its meaning al-Zamakhsharī says that it is "a likeness for His state in the ease of His answering whoever calls upon Him and the speed of His granting the need of whoever asks Him, by the state of one whose place is near"[^13]. Al-Ṭabarī holds that the meaning of "let them respond to Me" is: with obedience[^14], and this ties supplication to obedience: it is not enough for us to seek God's answer; we must answer Him when He calls us. Perhaps for this reason "and let them believe in Me" follows it.

In interpreting it, Ibn Kathīr relays the report of Abū Mūsā, which includes the saying: «إنكم لا تدعون أصم ولا غائبا، إنما تدعون سميعا بصيرا، إن الذي تدعون أقرب إلى أحدكم من عنق راحلته» "You are not calling upon one who is deaf or absent. You are calling upon One who hears and sees. Truly, the One you call upon is nearer to any of you than the neck of his mount", and then comments: "What is meant by this is that He, Most High, never disappoints the supplication of a supplicant, and nothing distracts Him from it"[^15].

### "In Humility and in Secret": The Etiquette of Voice and Heart

Next comes the etiquette of supplication in Sūrat al-Aʿrāf: ﴿ادْعُوا رَبَّكُمْ تَضَرُّعًا وَخُفْيَةً ۚ إِنَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ الْمُعْتَدِينَ﴾ "Call upon your Lord in humility and in secret. Indeed, He does not like transgressors" (7:55), and then: ﴿وَادْعُوهُ خَوْفًا وَطَمَعًا ۚ إِنَّ رَحْمَتَ اللَّهِ قَرِيبٌ مِّنَ الْمُحْسِنِينَ﴾ "And call upon Him in fear and hope. Indeed, the mercy of God is near to the doers of good" (7:56). We have here two pairs: outward and inward, fear and hope.

The commentators differ over "in humility". Al-Ṭabarī says that "in humility" means "in abasement and submission to His obedience", and "in secret": "with the humility of your hearts and the soundness of your certainty of His oneness… not openly, for show", and he relates from Ibn Jurayj that among the transgression in supplication is "raising the voice, calling out and shouting in supplication"[^16]. Al-Qurṭubī agrees with him that "in secret" means "privately within the soul, to keep away from showing off", and cites God's praise of Zechariah[^17]. Ibn ʿĀshūr, however, differs on the meaning of "in humility", saying: "*Taḍarruʿ* is also applied to supplicating aloud… and this is what we choose because it is more fitting by contrast with 'in secret'", and he rebuts whoever thought that supplicating openly is forbidden: "This is a mistake, for the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, supplicated openly more than once"[^18].

On this same question there is a pause at the verse in Maryam with which we began: is there in "a hidden call" praise for concealing supplication? Al-Qurṭubī holds that it is praise of Zechariah, whereas Ibn ʿĀshūr holds that "the verse merely recounts the description of Zechariah's supplication as it occurred, so there is no suggestion in it of praise for concealing supplication"[^19]. I stand where the text stands: concealment was a state that was true for Zechariah, humility and reverence are a spirit required in every supplicant, and as for speaking aloud and speaking in secret, that is a wide door which the verse does not narrow.

### The Scene of the Ship: When the Supplicant Becomes Sincere

Then the Qur'an sets out a scene known to everyone who has sailed the sea: ﴿فَإِذَا رَكِبُوا فِي الْفُلْكِ دَعَوُا اللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ فَلَمَّا نَجَّاهُمْ إِلَى الْبَرِّ إِذَا هُمْ يُشْرِكُونَ﴾ "And when they board the ship, they call upon God, sincere to Him in religion; but when He delivers them to the land, at once they associate others with Him" (29:65). Al-Ṭabarī says that in hardship they were sincere in affirming His oneness, "and did not call for help upon their gods and their equals", and Ibn ʿĀshūr says: "*Ikhlāṣ* is purification and singling out. And *al-dīn* is dealing. What is meant by it here is supplication"[^20]. Hardship strips the heart of everything besides God, and this is what al-Qurṭubī says on the verse of al-Naml: ﴿أَمَّن يُجِيبُ الْمُضْطَرَّ إِذَا دَعَاهُ وَيَكْشِفُ السُّوءَ﴾ "Is He [not best] who responds to the desperate one when he calls upon Him and removes evil" (27:62), where he says that God "guaranteed… the answering of the desperate one when he calls upon Him", and that the reason is "that the necessity of fleeing to Him arises from sincerity and from the heart's severance from all else", so that this occurs "from a believer or a disbeliever, an obedient one or a wicked one". Ibn ʿĀshūr then differs from him, saying: "The apparent sense of the restriction by the adverbial clause requires a guarantee of response. The fact is that response depends on the will of God Most High, according to what the state of the supplicant requires"[^21].

Between the two views the secret in supplication becomes clear: it is not a mechanical key but a relationship between one in desperate need and a wise Lord. Al-Qurṭubī observes sincerity, Ibn ʿĀshūr observes wisdom, and the verse is wide enough for both. In the verse of al-ʿAnkabūt, moreover, there is a bitter hint that the purity born in hardship may not remain in ease: "at once they associate others with Him". This is what we ought to fear for our own supplications: that they be the call of an hour and not the call of a lifetime.

### Jonah in the Darknesses: A Supplication That Is a Confession

As for the scene in which supplication reaches its summit, it is Jonah's: ﴿فَنَادَىٰ فِي الظُّلُمَاتِ أَن لَّا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا أَنتَ سُبْحَانَكَ إِنِّي كُنتُ مِنَ الظَّالِمِينَ﴾ "And he called out within the darknesses, 'There is no god but You; exalted are You. Indeed, I have been of the wrongdoers'" (21:87). He asked for nothing. No deliverance and no relief, but affirmation of oneness, glorification and confession. And right after that: ﴿فَاسْتَجَبْنَا لَهُ وَنَجَّيْنَاهُ مِنَ الْغَمِّ ۚ وَكَذَٰلِكَ نُنجِي الْمُؤْمِنِينَ﴾ "So We responded to him and saved him from the distress. And thus do We save the believers" (21:88). Al-Zamakhsharī relates from al-Ḥasan: "By God, nothing saved him but his confessing against himself of wrongdoing"[^22]. Ibn ʿĀshūr interpreted the response as "an answer to his repentance for what he had neglected"[^23]. This is a supplication with nothing of "petition" in it but an allusion, and with enough of "worship" in it to fill the darknesses. It supports Ibn ʿĀshūr's reading of the verse of Ghāfir: the supplication that is worship and the supplication that is petition may come together in a few words, and the first may come so far forward that it nearly swallows the second.

### The Response That Is Delayed

An objector may say: how many a supplicant has not been answered? Here the Prophet ﷺ lays down a measure of etiquette: «يُستجاب لأحدكم ما لم يعجل، يقول: دعوتُ فلم يُستجب لي» "The prayer of any of you is answered so long as he does not grow hasty, saying: 'I called, and I was not answered.'" It was narrated by al-Bukhārī (6340) from the hadith of Abū Hurayra, and by Muslim (2735) in a close wording[^24]. The problem, then, is not the closed door but the hand that tires of knocking. And the response remains, as we saw in Ibn ʿĀshūr on the verse of al-Naml, "according to what the state of the supplicant requires", not according to what his haste desires.

In this sense there is a hadith reported from Salmān on modesty: «إن ربكم حيي كريم، يستحيي من عبده إذا رفع يديه إليه أن يردهما صفرًا» "Your Lord is Modest and Generous; He is shy before His servant, when he raises his hands to Him, to return them empty." It was narrated by Abū Dāwūd (1488), and it is presented with the ruling "sound" by al-Albānī, whereas al-Tirmidhī narrated it (3556) and said: "good, strange", and the editors of Dār al-Salām graded it weak; so I do not state its soundness categorically, and content myself with pointing to the disagreement[^25].

### From One in Need to One in Need

In the books of tafsīr there is a story that carries the whole lesson, which Ibn Kathīr relates in his commentary on the verse of al-Naml: someone asked Ṭāwūs to supplicate for him, and he said: "Supplicate for yourself, for He answers the desperate one when he calls upon Him"[^26]. I do not affirm the incident historically; I relate it as the commentator reported it, but its meaning is clear: there is no intermediary between the desperate one and his Lord. Supplication is not a profession in which some people specialize, but the concern of every servant who knows his need.

Applying this in upbringing begins with teaching our children and students that supplication is not a list of demands: it is first an affirmation of lordship, as Jonah did. We teach them that it is joined to sincerity, not to a passing surge of feeling, as in the scene of the ship. That it has an etiquette of voice and heart: humility and reverence, fear and hope. That answering God, by obedience, is a practical prelude to awaiting His answer, as in the verse of al-Baqara. And that the believer supplicates and does not rush, trusting that his Lord is nearer to him than the neck of his mount. Whoever understands this no longer sees in supplication only a means of obtaining what is sought, but sees in it a place of meeting.

### Conclusion

The word has grown so wide in the Qur'an that it gathers in a single root the caller's call and the Answerer's answer, the asking of need and the worship of neediness, Zechariah's whisper, Jonah's silence and the desperation of the passenger in the ship. And in the Qur'an God says, "Call upon Me", making the door of petition open to the servant, and then making its abandonment disdain. O God, we call upon You as You have commanded us, so answer us as You have promised us, and teach us how to call upon You: with humble hearts and truthful tongues, without haste and without weariness; and do not make us of those who, when You have delivered them, associate others with You, but of those who said in their darknesses: There is no god but You, exalted are You; indeed we have been of the wrongdoers.
