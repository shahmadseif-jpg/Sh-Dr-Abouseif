*Ikhlāṣ is not merely a good intention but an intention sifted free of every admixture, as pure milk comes forth from between half-digested matter and blood: a secret between the servant and his Lord, easily spoiled and therefore always to be guarded.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

In Sūrat al-Naḥl the Qurʾan paints a precise physiological scene:

﴿وَإِنَّ لَكُمْ فِي الْأَنْعَامِ لَعِبْرَةً نُسْقِيكُمْ مِمَّا فِي بُطُونِهِ مِنْ بَيْنِ فَرْثٍ وَدَمٍ لَبَنًا خَالِصًا سَائِغًا لِلشَّارِبِينَ﴾ (16:66)

*“And indeed, in livestock there is a lesson for you: We give you to drink from what is in their bellies, from between half-digested food and blood, milk pure and pleasant to those who drink.”*

Milk comes forth from between *farth* (the residue of digested food) and blood, clear and pure, touched by nothing of what lay beside its source. This very tangible picture, the drawing out of the pure from amid what is mixed around it without itself being mingled, is the meaning on which the Qurʾan builds elsewhere one of its subtlest concepts among the deeds of the heart: *ikhlāṣ*, sincere purity.

### Delimiting the Word and Counting Its Occurrences

The root kh-l-ṣ occurs in the Qurʾan thirty-one times, in seven forms. Two usages lie far from the axis of this article. The first is the verb *khalaṣū*, “they withdrew and set themselves apart,” which occurs once (12:80), in the story of Joseph's brothers when they withdrew to confer in private. The second is the verb *astakhliṣhu*, “I shall choose him for my own service,” which occurs once (12:54), on the lips of the king of Egypt concerning Joseph. Both share with the root's origin the sense of “setting apart and drawing out,” yet both are remote from the context of sincerity in worship.

The field that concerns us comprises three forms. First, the adjective *khāliṣ/khāliṣah*, which occurs seven times in the sense of purity and exclusiveness, as in the pure milk of 16:66 and the pure religion of 39:3. Second, the active participle of Form IV, *mukhliṣ/mukhliṣīn*, which occurs eleven times and is the direct verbal witness to one who makes his deed sincere for God by his own will. Third, the passive participle *mukhlaṣ/mukhlaṣīn*, which occurs nine times and describes those whom God has chosen and purified, not those who made themselves sincere. The first is an act the servant performs; the second is an act God performs in His servant, though both share root and word.

### The Linguistic Root: From the Purity of Milk to the Purity of Intention

In the language, the root *khalaṣa* denotes the clarity and purity that result from separation from what mingles with a thing. Milk comes forth from between *farth* and blood without blending with them. Pure gold is gold that has parted from the dust and impurities that mingled with it in the raw ore. And when the Qurʾan says of Joseph's brothers that they *khalaṣū najiyyan* (12:80), it means they separated from the company to confer alone. The unifying meaning in all these usages is one: the coming forth of what is pure or set apart from amid what mingled with it or lay beside it. Applied to the intention of the heart, the same meaning becomes this: the aim of worship comes forth pure for God alone, separated from whatever might have mingled with it, whether ostentation, the quest for praise, or some other purpose.

### Every Sincere Person Has an Intention, but Not Every Intention Is Sincere

Here it is worth pausing at a distinction often overlooked when *ikhlāṣ* is rendered as “good intention.” In the root of the language, intention (*niyyah*) is no more than aim. It is a general and neutral notion, covering every orientation of the heart toward some end, praiseworthy or mixed, pure or tainted. A person may have an intention in his worship that is nonetheless blended with a desire for people's praise, and it remains an intention in the general linguistic sense.

*Ikhlāṣ*, by contrast, does not describe the mere existence of an aim. It describes the condition of that aim after it has been sifted of everything that mingled with it, just as milk is strained of *farth* and blood without ceasing to be milk in either case. Every sincere person has an intention, but not every intention is sincere. That is the distinction that makes *ikhlāṣ* more specific than intention and a degree above it, not a synonym for it.

### The Central Structure: Purifying the Deed of Admixture

This is the heart of what the Qurʾan calls *ikhlāṣ*: not the bare existence of a good intention, but the cleansing of the act of every other purpose that mixes with it, until it comes forth for God alone, as milk comes forth pure from between *farth* and blood. The Qurʾan states this meaning outright when it speaks of the pure religion:

﴿أَلَا لِلَّهِ الدِّينُ الْخَالِصُ﴾ (39:3)

*“Unquestionably, to God belongs the pure religion.”*

It uses the very adjective with which it described the milk, to say that worship, like milk, is not accepted unless it comes forth pure, unblended with any trace of association, hidden or open.

### A Scene That Reveals the True Moment of Sincerity: The Prayer of Distress

The Qurʾan repeats one scene in three places in almost identical words. People board a ship at sea; waves close over them from every side until they believe they are lost; and what do they do?

﴿دَعَوُا اللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ﴾ (10:22)

*“They call upon God, making their religion sincerely His.”*

The same scene recurs at 29:65 and 31:32. In the moment of extreme peril, when the heart is stripped to its reality and every social pretense falls away, these people call on none but God alone, with no partner named beside Him, not even out of imitation or habit.

This recurring scene shows that *ikhlāṣ* is clearest when every motive for ostentation has vanished. When there is no one left to impress, and no profit in professing faith, natural sincerity appears in its purest form. The Qurʾan then reproaches these same people, in the continuation of the verses, because once God delivers them to land they return to associating partners with Him, as though the sincerity drawn out by hardship did not endure in ease.

### Another Pattern: The Servants Whom God Chose and Selected

Over against this sincerity that a servant exerts by his own will, the Qurʾan describes a company of the prophets as *mukhlaṣūn*, in the passive participle and not the active. It is God who purified them and chose them:

﴿إِلَّا عِبَادَ اللَّهِ الْمُخْلَصِينَ﴾

*“Except the chosen servants of God.”*

This phrase recurs verbatim five times in Sūrat al-Ṣāffāt alone (37:40, 74, 128, 160, 169), and elsewhere as well (12:24, 15:40, 38:83). In the story of Joseph in particular, when he inclined toward the woman who sought to seduce him, the Qurʾan accounts for his deliverance from indecency in these words:

﴿كَذَٰلِكَ لِنَصْرِفَ عَنْهُ السُّوءَ وَالْفَحْشَاءَ إِنَّهُ مِنْ عِبَادِنَا الْمُخْلَصِينَ﴾ (12:24)

*“Thus [it was], that We might avert from him evil and immorality. Indeed, he was of Our chosen servants.”*

Here sincerity is the cause of protection, not its result: because God had purified and chosen him, evil was turned away from him. This adds an important layer to the concept. *Ikhlāṣ* has two complementary faces: a sincerity the servant offers by his own will in his worship, and a choosing that God grants to whom He wills, guarding him from stumbling.

### When *Ikhlāṣ* Joins *Ḥanīfiyyah*

It is worth dwelling on the fact that the Qurʾan, in what are said to be nearly the last of its verses to be revealed, describes the divine command that gathers up all earlier laws in these words:

﴿وَمَا أُمِرُوا إِلَّا لِيَعْبُدُوا اللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ حُنَفَاءَ﴾ (98:5)

*“And they were not commanded except to worship God, making the religion sincerely His, inclining to the truth.”*

*Ikhlāṣ* is not mentioned here alone but joined to *ḥanīfiyyah*, whose meaning was set out in the first article of this series as an innate inclination away from idolatry toward pure monotheism. The verse gathers the two descriptions because they are two faces of one coin. *Ḥanīfiyyah* is an inclination of belief that refuses to associate any partner with God in creed; *ikhlāṣ* is a purity of practice that refuses to associate any partner with Him in aim and intention. Each is a cleansing of the servant's relationship with his Lord from whatever mingles with it or contends against it.

### “So That His Left Hand Does Not Know What His Right Hand Spends”

If the scene of the people on the ship reveals sincerity in a moment of overwhelming distress, the Prophetic Sunnah offers another picture of it in a moment of free choice, when a person could display his deed and chooses to hide it. In the hadith of the seven whom God will shade in His shade on the day when there is no shade but His, the Prophet ﷺ names among them:

«ورجلٌ تصدّق بصدقةٍ فأخفاها، حتى لا تعلم شماله ما تُنفق يمينه»

*“…and a man who gave charity and concealed it, so that his left hand did not know what his right hand spent.”*[^1]

The image is a rhetorical hyperbole: the left hand, nearest of all things to the right, is imagined to be veiled from knowing what its sister has spent, to express concealment from every possible eye, even that of one's own body. It is a literal application of the standard of “the people of the ship” reached in this article. As the drowning were sincere when every observer was absent, this giver of charity was sincere when he himself chose that every observer should be absent from his deed, though nothing compelled him to.

### The Prophetic Witness

ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb (may God be pleased with him) reported, in a hadith recorded by both al-Bukhārī and Muslim in their *Ṣaḥīḥs*, that the Prophet ﷺ said:

«إنما الأعمال بالنيّات، وإنما لكلّ امرئٍ ما نوى»

*“Deeds are only by intentions, and every person will have only what he intended.”*[^2]

Imām Aḥmad is reported to have said of this hadith that the foundations of Islam turn upon three hadiths, and that this is one of them.

In contrast, Abū Hurayrah (may God be pleased with him) reported, in a hadith recorded by Muslim in his *Ṣaḥīḥ*, the account of those “with whom the Fire will first be kindled on the Day of Resurrection”: a scholar, a fighter and a man of charity. They performed deeds that were outwardly great, but these did not come forth purely for God; they were done in search of praise. To each of them it was said:

«كذبتَ، ولكنّك فعلتَ ليُقال…»

*“You have lied; rather, you did it so that it would be said…”*[^3]

Imām al-Nawawī said in his commentary that this hadith is proof of the gravity of the prohibition of ostentation (*riyāʾ*) and the severity of its punishment, and of the obligation of sincerity in deeds. Together, the two hadiths draw the two pans of the scale. Whoever made his intention sincere is reckoned by it, though his deed be small. Whoever spoiled his intention by seeking praise has his deed thrown back at him, though it be great.

### A Reading in Light of the Higher Objectives (*Maqāṣid*)

Scholars note that the Qurʾan did not make *ikhlāṣ* a condition of the purely devotional acts alone, but extended it to the whole of religion. “Unquestionably, to God belongs the pure religion” is a general expression that embraces creed, worship and dealings together, not prayer and fasting only.

Some commentators connect this generality with the fact that *ikhlāṣ* is a condition of acceptance, not a merely additional recommendation. A deed of corrupted intention, however perfect in outward form, is not counted by God as a righteous deed, as the hadith of the scholar, the fighter and the man of charity makes plain. This sets *ikhlāṣ* apart from many other virtues, whose lack diminishes the reward; when *ikhlāṣ* is absent, the whole deed vanishes with it, for when enough impurity mingles with what is pure, it is the very substance that is spoiled, not just its measure.

For this reason many masters of the spiritual path counted *ikhlāṣ* the hardest of all the deeds of the heart. It is hard not because it is difficult to attain at the outset but because it is difficult to remain firm in it. The taint of ostentation may enter a deed that began pure and spoil it along the way, as an impurity may fall into pure milk after it has been drawn and ruin its clarity.

Perhaps no one expressed this difficulty more eloquently than Sufyān al-Thawrī, of whom it is related that he said:

«ما عالجتُ شيئًا أشدَّ عليَّ من نيّتي؛ لأنها تتقلَّب عليَّ»

*“I have never wrestled with anything harder than my intention, for it keeps turning over on me.”*[^4]

*Ikhlāṣ*, then, is not a resolution tied once and left behind, but a striving renewed in every deed, because the intention, as he describes it, never settles in one state.

Of the same kind is what is related from al-Junayd ibn Muḥammad, the master of the Sufis of his age, who defined *ikhlāṣ* thus:

«سرٌّ بين الله وبين العبد، لا يعلمه ملَكٌ فيكتبه، ولا شيطانٌ فيُفسده»

*“A secret between God and the servant, which no angel knows so as to record it, and no devil knows so as to corrupt it.”*[^5]

This definition meets the hadith “he concealed it so that his left hand did not know.” So long as *ikhlāṣ* is a secret known to none but God, its greatest protection is that it remain so, hidden even from the knowledge of the recording angels, who record the outward form of a deed and not the hidden things of the heart.

### The Contemporary Application

In an age when every act can be displayed, documented and shared, many find it hard to separate the worth of a deed in itself from its echo in the eyes of those who see it. The picture of milk with which this article opened offers a practical measure. As the slightest real admixture of *farth* and blood spoils milk, so the slightest real admixture of the desire to be seen in the intention behind a deed spoils its worth with God, though the deed remain sound in outward form.

What is asked is not that we withhold the sharing of good or hide every righteous act; there may be an example for others in displaying it. What is asked is that the first motive of the deed remain pure for God, so that if everyone who sees it were to vanish, neither the doer's purpose nor his effort would change. This is the fine distinction that the scene of the people on the ship disclosed: true *ikhlāṣ* is what remains when no one is left to see it.

It also yields a simple daily test by which anyone may examine his intention. Before any deed he means to do, let him ask himself: would I do this if I knew for certain that no one would ever know of it but myself and God? If the answer is yes, that is a sign of purity in the aim. If the answer shifts with the presence or absence of people, there lies the admixture that must be strained out, just as milk is purified of whatever may mingle with it.

### Conclusion

From milk that comes forth pure from between *farth* and blood, to a prayer offered for God alone amid the surging waves, to a charity the right hand hides from the left, to servants whom God chose and cleansed of indecency, the Qurʾan and the Sunnah draw one unchanging meaning for *ikhlāṣ*: that the aim come forth pure from everything that might mingle with it, a secret known to none but God, as the pure comes forth from amid what surrounded it. And God, the Exalted, knows best; He is the Guardian of success.
