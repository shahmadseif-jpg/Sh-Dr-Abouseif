*A study of a word that Joseph's brothers spoke to their father and with which the Qur'an then answered the Bedouin: what is faith when it is ascribed to the heart, when it increases and decreases, and when deeds are called by its name? The study follows the root ʾ-m-n from Jacob's chamber to the mosque.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

The brothers of Joseph came to their father at nightfall: ﴿وَجَاءُوا أَبَاهُمْ عِشَاءً يَبْكُونَ﴾ "And they came to their father at night, weeping" (12:16). There were abundant tears, a choked voice, and a well-arranged story: we went off racing one another, we left Joseph with our belongings, and the wolf devoured him. Then they said a word as though they knew that no one would credit it: ﴿وَمَا أَنتَ بِمُؤْمِنٍ لَّنَا وَلَوْ كُنَّا صَادِقِينَ﴾ "But you would not believe us, even if we were truthful" (12:17). And Jacob turned to the shirt, and the blood upon it was the blood of a lie: ﴿قَالَ بَلْ سَوَّلَتْ لَكُمْ أَنفُسُكُمْ أَمْرًا ۖ فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ﴾ "He said, 'Rather, your souls have enticed you to something, so patience is most fitting'" (12:18). The meaning of *bi-muʾminin lanā* is "you are not one who credits us"; yet al-Zamakhsharī catches in the sentence something that lies beyond mere assent, and says of its meaning: "even if we were, in your eyes, people of truthfulness and trustworthiness, because of your great love for Joseph; how much more so when you think ill of us and do not trust our word"[^1]. Faith in this scene is therefore not information received but trust in a person, by which the soul is set at rest; and the father who has lost that trust is persuaded neither by tears nor by a shirt.

When the Qur'an carries this very word from Jacob's chamber to the mosque, it takes on a different weight. It is what the Bedouin say to the Messenger of God ﷺ and the Qur'an turns back upon them; it is what grows in the heart and what God writes into it; and it is the name given to a prayer offered toward Bayt al-Maqdis (Jerusalem). Let us follow, then, what the verses do with it.

### Eight Hundred Twelve Occurrences from a Root of Eight Hundred Seventy-Nine

The root ʾ-m-n («أ م ن») occurs in the Qur'an 879 times, distributed over seventeen forms, according to the Quranic Arabic Corpus (corpus.quran.com)[^2]. This root carries three branches: security (*amn*); trustworthiness or trust (*amāna*), to which we devoted an earlier article, "Amāna in the Qur'an: When the Mountain Refuses and the Human Being Accepts"; and faith (*īmān*), which is our subject. The branch of faith predominates by a wide margin: the verb *āmana* 537 times, the active participle *muʾmin* 202, the verbal noun *īmān* 45, *muʾmināt* 22, and *muʾmina* 6. Together these make 812 occurrences, so that more than nine-tenths of the root belongs to this branch, leaving sixty-seven occurrences for security, trust and entrusting. This sum is my own arithmetic on the figures of the page cited, and I am assured that the reading is sound because the rows of the page total 879, as the page itself announces.

Among the forms there is something that draws the eye: the form *āmanū* alone occurs 253 times, nearly half the occurrences of the verb. According to my own count from the texts of the verses on the same page, the address "O you who believe" occurs in seventy verses; *āmanū* is joined with "and did righteous deeds" in forty-four verses; while the singular form "believed and did righteousness" occurs in only five verses[^3]. What appears to me from this is that the Qur'an speaks of faith, for the most part, as the speech of a community that is addressed and called, not of an isolated individual, and that it almost never leaves "faith" alone in a place of promise without joining deeds to it. This is an observation about the structure of the words, not a ruling on the disputed questions that follow.

### Two Closely Related Origins: Stillness of the Heart and Assent

Ibn Fāris opens the entry *amn* with these words: "The hamza, the mīm and the nūn are two closely related origins: one is trustworthiness (*amāna*), which is the opposite of betrayal, and its meaning is stillness of the heart; the other is assent (*taṣdīq*). The two meanings, as we have said, are near to each other." He then cites for assent the words of the Most High "and you are not a *muʾmin* to us", that is, one who assents to us[^4]. On this point the commentators agree concerning the verse: al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī and al-Zamakhsharī all read "one who assents"; and al-Qurṭubī adds that the verb "takes both *bi-* and *li-*"[^5].

When we reach al-Rāghib we find the two origins joined in a single statement. He mentions the view that the meaning is "one who assents to us", and then says: "except that faith is assent accompanied by security"[^6]. That is, faith is an assent by which the soul comes to rest and fear departs, not a mere intellectual acknowledgement. He then elaborates: when the word is used in praise, it is "the submission of the soul to the truth by way of assent, and that takes place through the coming together of three things: verification by the heart, avowal by the tongue, and action in accordance with it by the limbs"[^7]. Joining the statements of Ibn Fāris and al-Rāghib, the two formulations meet at what we shall see in the verses: that in the Qur'an faith is a movement within the heart before it is a word upon the tongue.

Al-Rāghib has a subtle point in this chapter. When it is said "they believe in *al-jibt* and *al-ṭāghūt*" (4:51), the word is used by way of censure, "and that security has come to them through that by which security does not come about, since it is not in the nature of the heart, so long as it has not been sealed, to find rest in falsehood"[^6]. Whoever has found rest in falsehood has "believed" in the sense of the language, but the Qur'an censures him for what he has lodged in his heart.

### Bedouin at the Door of Prophethood: Between Islām and Īmān

The clearest thing the Qur'an does with this word is in Sūrat al-Ḥujurāt. The Bedouin came saying: we have believed. In al-Qurṭubī it is reported that they arrived in a year of drought saying: we have come to you with our burdens and our families, and they began to reproach the Prophet ﷺ with their favour to him, whereupon the verses came down. The answer came at once firm and gentle: ﴿قَالَتِ الْأَعْرَابُ آمَنَّا ۖ قُل لَّمْ تُؤْمِنُوا وَلَٰكِن قُولُوا أَسْلَمْنَا وَلَمَّا يَدْخُلِ الْإِيمَانُ فِي قُلُوبِكُمْ ۖ وَإِن تُطِيعُوا اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ لَا يَلِتْكُم مِّنْ أَعْمَالِكُمْ شَيْئًا ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ﴾ "The Bedouins say, 'We have believed.' Say, 'You have not believed; but say [instead], "We have submitted," for faith has not yet entered your hearts. And if you obey God and His Messenger, He will not deprive you of anything of your deeds. Indeed, God is Forgiving and Merciful'" (49:14). Then the Qur'an sketches what must be realised after the claim: ﴿إِنَّمَا الْمُؤْمِنُونَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا بِاللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَرْتَابُوا وَجَاهَدُوا بِأَمْوَالِهِمْ وَأَنفُسِهِمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ ۚ أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الصَّادِقُونَ﴾ "The believers are only those who have believed in God and His Messenger and then doubt not but strive with their wealth and their lives in the cause of God. It is those who are the truthful" (49:15). The two verses set the stages in order: a claim upon the tongue, then entry into the heart, then the absence of doubt, then the outlay of wealth and self. And in the same verse there is a door of hope: "and if you obey God and His Messenger, He will not deprive you of anything of your deeds"; the Qur'an did not close the door on those who said "we have submitted".

Here the commentators differed in a beneficial way, and we present it as it is. Al-Ṭabarī held that God had admonished these Bedouin, who had entered the community by avowal in word and had not verified their word by their deed, not to say "we have believed" unreservedly and without qualification, and he reported the saying of al-Zuhrī: "Islām is the word, and īmān is the deed"[^8]. Ibn Kathīr held that the verse shows "that faith is more specific than islām, as is the position of Ahl al-Sunna wa'l-Jamāʿa", and that the Bedouin "were not hypocrites; rather they were Muslims in whose hearts faith had not yet become firmly established, so they claimed for themselves a station higher than the one they had reached, and were disciplined for it". He attributed this to Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī and Qatāda, and noted that Ibn Jarīr chose it[^9]. Al-Qurṭubī takes the opposite view: "the meaning of 'but say, We have submitted' is: we have surrendered out of fear of being killed or enslaved, and this is the description of the hypocrites". For him the reality of faith is "assent by the heart", and islām is "acceptance, in outward form, of what the Prophet ﷺ brought, and this protects blood"[^10]. And al-Zamakhsharī's formulation gathers both sides of the disagreement into one definition: "Faith is assent together with trust and the tranquillity of the soul. Islām is entry into peace (*salm*)"[^11].

The disagreement thus concerns two matters: are the Bedouin hypocrites, or Muslims weak in faith? And is faith a deed that verifies speech, or an assent of the heart by which the soul comes to rest? Ibn Kathīr transmitted from the Sunna what supports the distinction between the two names, and we pause at two hadiths. The first is the hadith of Saʿd, in which he testified for a certain man: «فوالله إني لأراه مؤمنا» "By God, I see him as a believer"; the Prophet ﷺ interjected: «أو مسلما» "Or a Muslim", and then said: «يا سعد، إني لأعطي الرجل وغيره أحب إلي منه، خشية أن يكبه الله في النار» "O Saʿd, I give to one man while another is dearer to me than he, for fear that God would cast him on his face into the Fire"[^12]. The second is the hadith of Jibrīl, in which faith is «أن تؤمن بالله وملائكته وكتبه ورسله واليوم الآخر وتؤمن بالقدر خيره وشره» "that you believe in God, His angels, His books, His messengers and the Last Day, and that you believe in the decree, its good and its evil", and iḥsān is «أن تعبد الله كأنك تراه فإن لم تكن تراه فإنه يراك» "that you worship God as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, He sees you"[^13]. In the narration of Abū Hurayra in al-Bukhārī, faith comes in a wording that includes «وبلقائه» "and His meeting" and «وتؤمن بالبعث» "and that you believe in the resurrection", so the fundamentals are constant even though the narrators differ in the wording with which they list them[^13].

I see in these verses a courtesy of address: the Qur'an did not say to the Bedouin "you have lied" but "you have not believed", and then gave them the name that is rightfully theirs: "we have submitted". The matter is thus a fine balance with no contempt in it. Then the scene closes by setting the direction right: ﴿بَلِ اللَّهُ يَمُنُّ عَلَيْكُمْ أَنْ هَدَاكُمْ لِلْإِيمَانِ إِن كُنتُمْ صَادِقِينَ﴾ "Rather, God confers favour upon you that He has guided you to the faith, if you should be truthful" (49:17). Faith is a favour from God to the servant, not a favour from the servant to God.

### A Faith That Enters, Is Made Beloved, and Increases

When we gather the verses containing the verbal noun *īmān* (which occurs in 45 places), we see that the Qur'an portrays it as something that moves in the heart: "and faith has not yet entered your hearts"; it is something that is made beloved and made beautiful: ﴿وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ حَبَّبَ إِلَيْكُمُ الْإِيمَانَ وَزَيَّنَهُ فِي قُلُوبِكُمْ﴾ "But God has endeared to you the faith and has made it pleasing in your hearts" (49:7); and something that increases: ﴿إِنَّمَا الْمُؤْمِنُونَ الَّذِينَ إِذَا ذُكِرَ اللَّهُ وَجِلَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ وَإِذَا تُلِيَتْ عَلَيْهِمْ آيَاتُهُ زَادَتْهُمْ إِيمَانًا وَعَلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يَتَوَكَّلُونَ﴾ "The believers are only those who, when God is mentioned, their hearts become fearful, and when His verses are recited to them, it increases them in faith; and upon their Lord they rely" (8:2). Likewise in hardship: ﴿فَزَادَهُمْ إِيمَانًا وَقَالُوا حَسْبُنَا اللَّهُ وَنِعْمَ الْوَكِيلُ﴾ "and it increased them in faith, and they said, 'Sufficient for us is God, and [He is] the best Disposer of affairs'" (3:173), and in tranquillity: ﴿هُوَ الَّذِي أَنزَلَ السَّكِينَةَ فِي قُلُوبِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ لِيَزْدَادُوا إِيمَانًا مَّعَ إِيمَانِهِمْ﴾ "It is He who sent down tranquillity into the hearts of the believers that they would increase in faith along with their [present] faith" (48:4). In these places there come together the trembling at remembrance, recitation, trial, tranquillity and reliance; and all of them are states of the heart, not sayings of the tongue.

Here too there is a disagreement worth presenting. Al-Ṭabarī says, on the verse of al-Anfāl, that the believer "is the one who, when God is mentioned, his heart trembles and he submits to His command… and when the verses of His Book are recited to him, he assents to them and is certain that they are from God, and so he increases through his assent to that"[^14]. Ibn Kathīr says that al-Bukhārī and others drew on this verse and its like as evidence "for the increase of faith and its gradation in hearts, as is the doctrine of the majority of the community; indeed, more than one of the imams has reported consensus on that, such as al-Shāfiʿī, Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal and Abū ʿUbayd"[^15]. As for al-Rāzī, he directs the increase to another aspect, in what I have read of his words: since obligations followed one after another in the time of the Messenger ﷺ, "at the occurrence of each obligation they would increase in assent and avowal", and whenever they heard a new verse "they brought a new avowal, and that was an increase in faith and assent". He then mentions a third aspect, namely that the perfection of God's power and wisdom is known through its traces in His creatures[^16]. His explanation of the increase therefore turns on the multiplicity of what is believed and the growth of knowledge, and I have not come upon the whole of his position on the question, so I attribute to him no more than he said here. What remains common is that the verse speaks of a faith that moves upon hearing the words of God and does not stand rigid.

### When Deed Is Called Faith

In al-Baqara there is a passage that widens the word astonishingly. When the qibla was changed and people asked about their brethren who had died while praying toward Bayt al-Maqdis, there came down: ﴿وَمَا كَانَ اللَّهُ لِيُضِيعَ إِيمَانَكُمْ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ بِالنَّاسِ لَرَءُوفٌ رَّحِيمٌ﴾ "And never would God have caused you to lose your faith. Indeed, God is, to the people, Kind and Merciful" (2:143). Al-Ṭabarī said: "By faith in this place is meant prayer"; Ibn Kathīr said: "that is, your prayer toward Bayt al-Maqdis before that; its reward will not be lost with God", and he reported from Ibn ʿAbbās that the meaning is: "the first qibla, and your assent to your Prophet, and your following him to the other qibla"[^17]. Here, then, the Qur'an named bowing and prostrating toward a particular direction "faith", and upon this al-Rāghib built his statement: "Each of belief, truthful speech and righteous deed is called faith"[^18].

The Sunna has what agrees with this breadth: «الإيمان بضع وستون شعبة، والحياء شعبة من الإيمان» "Faith has some sixty branches, and modesty is a branch of faith", in al-Bukhārī, and in Muslim's narration "some seventy branches"[^19]. The two narrations differ in the number, and this changes nothing of the meaning: faith is a house with many doors. Consider how the verse and the hadith meet: prayer toward the qibla is faith, modesty is faith, and between the two words a whole life is built, by the heart and the limbs together.

### A Believer in the Tyrant's Palace

If we want a human model in whom what has preceded takes shape, the verse stands before us in Sūrat Ghāfir: ﴿وَقَالَ رَجُلٌ مُّؤْمِنٌ مِّنْ آلِ فِرْعَوْنَ يَكْتُمُ إِيمَانَهُ أَتَقْتُلُونَ رَجُلًا أَن يَقُولَ رَبِّيَ اللَّهُ وَقَدْ جَاءَكُم بِالْبَيِّنَاتِ مِن رَّبِّكُمْ﴾ "And a believing man from the family of Pharaoh who concealed his faith said, 'Do you kill a man because he says, "My Lord is God" while he has brought you clear proofs from your Lord?'" (40:28). A man in the house of the tyrant, whom the Qur'an called "a believer" though he had not yet declared his faith: faith here stands in the heart, settled, and is ascribed to its owner even though he concealed it. Then we see him speak and argue with a calm argument before a people bent on killing a man because he says "my Lord is God". We content ourselves here with what is in the verse itself, and it is enough: a heart that outran the tongue, then a tongue that caught up with the heart when matters grew severe. In a single stance the man brought together the meaning of "the faith that enters the heart" and the meaning of "the faith in which speech is made true by deed".

### Where Do These Threads Meet?

If we gather what has preceded, it appears that the Qur'an's work upon this word rests on three pillars. The first is linguistic: the origin is assent and stillness of the heart, as Ibn Fāris and al-Rāghib said. The second concerns usage: the verb takes *bi-* when the object is God, His books and His messengers, and *li-* when the object is a person to whom we respond or whom we trust, as in *bi-muʾminin lanā*, in al-Qurṭubī's statement that it "takes both *bi-* and *li-*", and in his proof-text *fa-mā āmana li-Mūsā* ("none believed Moses except a few of his people", 10:83). This distinction in transitivity is an editorial observation of my own; I have not counted all the places of *bi-* in the Qur'an, and so I build on it nothing more than the pointer. The third is structural: faith in the Qur'an is a door open onto degrees; it is claimed, then enters, then increases, then is translated into deed, then is tested by doubt and striving.

It appears to me that in the verses there is an educational gradualness: the Bedouin is not asked to move from "we have submitted" to "we have believed" by a decision of the tongue, nor is he humiliated because he has not yet arrived; he is told that the way is to "obey God and His Messenger", and that faith is a "favour" to be sought. It is as though the verse says that faith is not wrested by the force of argument alone, but enters when God wills and makes it beloved to the heart, and that the servant must open the door to it by obedience, remembrance and recitation.

### Faith in Our Homes and Mosques

The application of this in our lives is wide. The educator who hears his son say "I am a believer" learns from al-Ḥujurāt to distinguish between the claim and the entry: he neither despises him nor is deceived by his word, but leads him toward what increases faith in the heart. And the verses show him the tools: a remembrance that makes the heart tremble, a recitation that increases, a tranquillity that descends, and a companionship like that of the believing man when he stands alone.

The imam and the caller to God have another lesson in the courtesy of address. When someone comes to you saying "I have believed" or "I have repented", do not call him a liar and do not inflate his station; say to him as the Qur'an said to the Bedouin: what is valid for you today is what you have said sincerely, and before you is a road that God does not close against you so long as you obey. So too in the family: the father who accuses his son at every incident learns from Jacob's scene that "faith" in people is a trust built through a long life, and it appears to me that Jacob was not set at rest by the tears because trust does not arise in a single hour. If we want those around us to believe us, let us be among those who are credited when they speak, and among those whose word puts people at rest before its proof does.

And in the practical field: modesty is a branch of faith, and al-Rāghib mentioned modesty and the removal of harm among what is called faith, so the picture of the believer is not confined to the one who argues well about belief, but includes character and righteous deed. This brings us back to what we began with, that the root itself joins assent and tranquillity.

### Conclusion: A Trust That God Places in the Heart

We began with a father telling his sons that he could not rest in their word, and we have ended with a Lord who confers favour upon His servants by having "endeared the faith to them and made it pleasing in their hearts". Between the two scenes lies a long road: a word on the tongue, then stillness in the heart, then an increase at remembrance and recitation, then a deed that bears witness, then steadfastness when the test arrives. O God, as You have endeared faith to us, make it pleasing in our hearts, and let it enter them as the entry of one who does not leave; increase us in it at every remembrance, every verse and every trial; send down tranquillity upon us that we may increase in faith along with our faith; make us among those who do not doubt and do not forsake; grant us the truthfulness of "Sufficient for us is God, and [He is] the best Disposer of affairs"; and do not let our faith be lost, for You are, to the people, Kind and Merciful.
