*Birr is the Qurʾan's most spacious name for goodness, a breadth the Book itself guards against being narrowed to a single direction, rite, or outward form. From the debate over the qiblah to the Prophet's two-word summary, "good character," this article traces how faith, generosity, patience, and filial devotion converge in one comprehensive virtue.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

The Muslims used to pray toward Jerusalem, until the command came to turn the qiblah toward the Kaʿbah while they were in the midst of their prayer. Those standing in a row behind the imam made a complete turn, from one direction to its very opposite, without breaking their prayer. Scarcely had the matter settled when a broad dispute arose around it: what did God intend by this change? And what has religion to do with such directions? The verses that were then revealed did not answer the question "which direction is better?" They uprooted the question itself:

﴿لَيْسَ الْبِرَّ أَنْ تُوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ قِبَلَ الْمَشْرِقِ وَالْمَغْرِبِ وَلَٰكِنَّ الْبِرَّ مَنْ آمَنَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَالْمَلَائِكَةِ وَالْكِتَابِ وَالنَّبِيِّينَ﴾ (2:177)
*"Righteousness (birr) is not that you turn your faces toward the east or the west, but righteousness is that of one who believes in God, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets."*

The dispute over the direction of the qiblah was only an instance of a graver illusion: that religion may be reduced to an outward movement, and that birr is as narrow a thing as the one direction a person faces.

### Twenty Occurrences Concerning Goodness, and Twelve Concerning Dry Land

The root b-r-r recurs in the Qurʾan thirty-two times, but nearly half of these bear no relation to our subject. Al-barr, with a fatḥah on the first letter, means dry land as opposed to the sea, and in this purely geographical sense it occurs twelve times (for example 6:59, 63, 97; 17:67, 68, 70; 30:41). These are places that have no true verbal kinship with the moral quality, despite the identity of the letters. The circle that concerns us comprises the verb tabarrū (twice: 2:224, 60:8); the noun al-birr (eight times: 2:44, 177 [twice], 189 [twice], 3:92, 5:2, 58:9); the plural al-abrār (six times: 3:193, 198; 76:5; 82:13; 83:18, 22); the adjective barran, describing the son devoted to his parents (19:14, 32); the divine name al-Barr (52:28); and the adjective bararah, describing the angels (80:16). The direct verbal witness thus totals twenty places.[^1]

### A Breadth That Answers to a Breadth

Before tracing this circle of twenty through its occurrences, it is worth pausing at what joins it, in language, to its distant sister, "dry land." The lexicons agree that birr, in its origin, is goodness wide and all-embracing, and not any one particular good deed. The philologists have observed that this meaning meets al-barr, dry land, at a single point of kinship: breadth and expanse. As dry land spreads before the beholder with no limit to confine it, so birr is a wide goodness that is not shut up within any one door of obedience. This linguistic meeting, though not a verbal witness to the moral usage but a distant derivational link, casts light on the meaning, and the light is confirmed by the text of the verse itself. When the Qurʾan wished to refute the illusion that birr could be reduced to a single direction, it brought one of the broadest definitions of virtue in the whole Book, as though the breadth of the word in language were the very breadth of its content in the law.

### Two Verses That Refute the Reduction Twice

This is not the only time the Qurʾan uses the pattern "birr is not… but birr is…" to overturn a superficial understanding. In the same sura, twelve verses further on, the pattern recurs on an entirely different occasion. Some of the Arabs in the Age of Ignorance, once they had entered the state of iḥrām for pilgrimage, would not go into their houses by the door, but would pierce an opening in the back wall and enter by that, in a spurious show of piety. Then was revealed:

﴿وَلَيْسَ الْبِرُّ بِأَنْ تَأْتُوا الْبُيُوتَ مِنْ ظُهُورِهَا وَلَٰكِنَّ الْبِرَّ مَنِ اتَّقَىٰ﴾ (2:189)
*"Righteousness is not that you enter houses from the back, but righteousness is that of one who is God-fearing."*

The central structure that this repetition speaks is this: whenever people invent a formal rite and take it for the essence of religion, whether the direction of prayer or the door of entry, the Qurʾan returns them to one unchanging criterion: faith, God-consciousness, and righteous action. Birr, then, is not one more act added to the list of obediences. It is the comprehensive name by which every act that its doer takes for obedience is tested: does it belong to the essence of religion, or to its invented husks?

### A List, Not a Direction

The remainder of 2:177 deserves to be read in full, for it is the most comprehensive list of the constituents of birr in the Qurʾan: faith in God, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets; then

﴿وَآتَى الْمَالَ عَلَىٰ حُبِّهِ ذَوِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينَ وَابْنَ السَّبِيلِ وَالسَّائِلِينَ وَفِي الرِّقَابِ﴾
*"…and gives wealth, in spite of love for it, to relatives, orphans, the needy, the wayfarer, those who ask, and for the freeing of slaves,"*

then the establishment of prayer and the giving of zakāh; then the keeping of covenants; then patience

﴿فِي الْبَأْسَاءِ وَالضَّرَّاءِ وَحِينَ الْبَأْسِ﴾
*"…in poverty and hardship and in the time of battle."*

In a single breath the verse gathers creed, worship, giving, social conduct, and patience, and leaves no door of religion outside its definition. It is striking that the giving is qualified by "in spite of love for it," which another verse states plainly:

﴿لَنْ تَنَالُوا الْبِرَّ حَتَّىٰ تُنْفِقُوا مِمَّا تُحِبُّونَ﴾ (3:92)
*"You will never attain righteousness until you spend from that which you love."*

Birr is not attained through the surplus of wealth that is never missed, but through what its owner loves and yet prefers to give to another. In a further dimension, the Qurʾan ties birr to parents in particular, describing Yaḥyā and ʿĪsā (peace be upon them) as "dutiful to his parents" and "dutiful to my mother" (19:14, 32). It thus makes this quality one with which God Himself described His prophets, and not the general body of the righteous alone.

The word al-abrār itself deserves an independent pause, for it does not appear as a passing description but as the name of a lofty rank among the people of Paradise, marked out by a special bliss:

﴿إِنَّ الْأَبْرَارَ لَفِي نَعِيمٍ﴾ (82:13، 83:22)
*"Indeed, the righteous will be in bliss."*

They are given to drink from a cup

﴿كَانَ مِزَاجُهَا كَافُورًا﴾ (76:5)
*"…whose mixture is of camphor,"*

and their record is said to be

﴿لَفِي عِلِّيِّينَ﴾ (83:18)
*"…in ʿIlliyyūn,"*

the highest of the stations of the records, not the lowest. Birr in the Qurʾan is therefore not one virtue among many of equal weight. It is the name given to one who has reached in obedience a degree that sets him apart with a standing of his own among the people of Paradise, and not merely among those delivered from the Fire.

### A Word That Abridges the List

After this Qurʾanic breadth came a prophetic hadith that gathered the whole meaning into two words. Al-Nawwās ibn Samʿān (may God be pleased with him) asked the Prophet ﷺ about righteousness and sin, and he said: «الْبِرُّ حُسْنُ الْخُلُقِ، وَالْإِثْمُ مَا حَاكَ فِي نَفْسِكَ وَكَرِهْتَ أَنْ يَطَّلِعَ عَلَيْهِ النَّاسُ» — "Righteousness is good character, and sin is what stirs in your soul and you dislike that people should come to know of it." Muslim recorded it.[^2] After the Qurʾan had set out a long list of creed, worship, giving, and patience, the hadith brought all of it back to a single essence that shows itself in daily conduct: good character. It is as if every item in the list of al-Baqarah were one manifestation of this one essence.

In another hadith, ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd (may God be pleased with him) asked the Prophet ﷺ: «أَيُّ الْعَمَلِ أَحَبُّ إِلَى اللَّهِ؟» — "Which deed is most beloved to God?" He said: «الصَّلَاةُ عَلَى وَقْتِهَا» — "Prayer at its proper time." He asked: «ثُمَّ أَيٌّ؟» — "Then which?" He said: «ثُمَّ بِرُّ الْوَالِدَيْنِ» — "Then dutifulness to parents (birr al-wālidayn)." He asked: «ثُمَّ أَيٌّ؟» — "Then which?" He said: «الْجِهَادُ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ» — "Striving in the path of God." It is agreed upon.[^3] The Prophet ﷺ thus ranked dutifulness to parents second, after prayer, and before jihad, the summit of religion's hump. He gave it a priority that may escape the person who supposes that striving and calling to God are worthier than caring for an aged parent at home.

### When the Rite Becomes a Veil

In their commentary on the verse "Righteousness is not that you turn your faces," many exegetes agree that its purpose is not to prefer one direction over another, but to free religion from the illusion that rites, stripped of the faith that should accompany them in the heart and the deeds that should accompany them in life, can achieve birr or bring forth good. A rite may become a veil over the essence of religion if it is taken to be that essence in itself, and not a means to it. This explains why the Qurʾan, at the moment when people might have been absorbed in the dispute over "which direction is more correct," chose to widen the whole question rather than answer it in its narrowness. The aim of religion lies not in a direction one faces but in a heart that is set right, and a life established upon truthfulness, generosity, and fidelity.

### When We Too Reduce

The very pattern that the two verses of al-Baqarah corrected recurs today, though its guise has changed. Some suppose that religiosity is confined to a single outward form, such as the manner of dress, the tone of speech, or severity in some secondary matter of dispute, while they are careless in good character toward those nearest to them, in their homes or at work. Such a person sets up his own private "qiblah" and takes it for the whole of religion, exactly as some of the Arabs of the Age of Ignorance took entering a house from the back for piety.

The list in the verse of al-Baqarah itself serves today as a practical measure. Is there truthfulness in my faith? Is there, in my wealth, preference for others and not mere surplus? Is there fidelity in my promise? Is there patience in my hardship? If one of these pillars is missing and its place has been taken by strictness in some formal matter, that is precisely the reduction the two verses were revealed to refute.

The same applies to one who supposes birr confined to the relationship with God alone, so that he busies himself with abundant voluntary devotions while falling short in the rights of his parents, his neighbor, or his colleague. The hadith of Ibn Masʿūd places dutifulness to parents in a rank that precedes even jihad, and it is a reminder that the breadth of birr reaches those nearest to us before those farthest away, and not the reverse. In an age when priorities contend among the pursuit of religious knowledge, the call to God, and public work, this hadith remains an unforgettable reminder: let a person ask himself where dutifulness to his parents stands on the ladder of his priorities, before he busies himself with other fields he has judged to be better.

### Conclusion

From the dispute over the direction of the qiblah, to the piercing of walls as a show of piety, to a list that gathers faith, giving, and patience, the Qurʾan draws for birr a breadth that refuses every attempt to confine it to a single rite. And when all this breadth was abridged into two words on the tongue of the Prophet ﷺ, nothing was chosen for it but "good character," an essence that shows itself in every detail of daily life, and not in one direction a person faces and then forgets. And God, Exalted is He, knows best; He is the Guardian of success.
