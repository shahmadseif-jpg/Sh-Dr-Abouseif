*A study of the word hawā ("desire"), which occurs thirty-eight times in the Qur'an, and of its singular usage there, where it is followed, taken as a god, forbidden, and denied any place in the speech of revelation. The Qur'an sets desire in one pan of the scale and truth, knowledge and revelation in the other, then asks which of them a person will follow.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

A seat of judgment on earth; two litigants standing before a man whom God has made a successor; a truth waiting for someone to speak it; and a hidden inclination in the heart that neither litigant sees and no one present hears. At that very moment the call comes down: ﴿يَا دَاوُودُ إِنَّا جَعَلْنَاكَ خَلِيفَةً فِي الْأَرْضِ فَاحْكُم بَيْنَ النَّاسِ بِالْحَقِّ وَلَا تَتَّبِعِ الْهَوَىٰ فَيُضِلَّكَ عَن سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ﴾ "O David, indeed We have made you a successor upon the earth, so judge between the people in truth and do not follow desire, for it will lead you astray from the way of God" (38:26). What has been reported about the occasion of this address does not concern us here; what concerns us is the call itself. It is addressed to David, peace be upon him, a man of kingship and prophethood, and in this place he is warned against one thing only, whose name is "desire" (*hawā*). It is as though the Qur'an were saying that the gravest danger to one who judges is not that the opponent stands before him, but that his own self leans while he sits. This word, at which the divine call stops, holds a place in the Qur'an unlike that of the other words for the self. For the most part it is mentioned only together with following, taking, or prohibition, as though it were a standing adversary set against truth and knowledge. What, then, is *hawā* in the Qur'an? And how did its usage come to take this singular form?

### Thirty-Eight Times, Twenty-Seven of Them in a Single Sense

The root h-w-y («ه و ي») occurs in the Qur'an thirty-eight times[^1], in five forms: eight times as a triliteral verb (*hawā*, *tahwā*, *tahwī*); once on the pattern of the tenth form, *istahwathu* (6:71); seventeen times as *ahwāʾ*; eleven times as *hawā* or *hawāʾ*; and once as *hāwiya* (101:9). Yet this figure conceals a division of meaning, which we have worked out from the lists of occurrences themselves[^2]. Twenty-seven occurrences (the seventeen *ahwāʾ* and ten instances of *hawā*) carry the sense of the self's inclination, which is the subject of this article. Three times the verb *tahwā* is joined to the self (2:87, 5:70, 53:23). Six times the root means falling, descending or being drawn (14:37, 20:81, 22:31, 53:1, 53:53, 101:9). *Istahwathu* occurs once. And *hawāʾ* in the sense of emptiness occurs once (14:43). The total is thirty-eight.

Within the twenty-seven there is an observation worth pausing over: twenty-two of them are joined to the verb of following. Sixteen of the seventeen occurrences of *ahwāʾ* are so joined (the exception is 6:119, "they lead astray by their desires"), and so are six of the ten occurrences of *hawā* (4:135, 7:176, 18:28, 20:16, 28:50, 38:26). The remaining four are the ones that carry the strangest constructions: "he has taken his god to be his desire" (25:43, 45:23), "he does not speak out of desire" (53:3), and "he restrained the self from desire" (79:40). It is at these passages that we stop.

### One Root Heading Downward

The dictionary sets before us a single root with two faces. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī says: "*Hawā* is the inclination of the self toward appetite. The term is also used for the self that inclines toward appetite. It has been said that it is so named because it casts its possessor down, in this world into every calamity and in the next into the abyss (*al-hāwiya*); and *al-huwiyy* is falling from a height to a depth."[^3] Under one root he gathers the inclination of the self and the fall from a height, and he makes the name (on one view that he reports) derive from the second, so that the possessor of *hawā* is one whom his own *hawā* casts down. He explains *istahwathu* (6:71) as "it led him to follow desire." *Lisān al-ʿArab* is broader: "*Hawā* is passionate love, and it enters both the paths of good and the paths of evil." Among the statements it transmits from the people of the language: "*Hawā* is a person's love of a thing and its mastery over his heart."[^4]

Language, then, says that *hawā* is a love that overcomes the heart, which may fall in the domain of good or in the domain of evil. The Qur'an chose only one face of it. In our survey of the twenty-seven occurrences of the noun we found none in which *hawā* is praised. Al-Qurṭubī reports from Ibn ʿAbbās the saying, "God never mentions *hawā* in the Qur'an except to condemn it," without a chain of transmission[^5], so we cite it as a witness and not as proof. It remains that the root itself is not wholly condemned. In the prayer of Abraham, peace be upon him, that God make the hearts of some people "incline" (*tahwī*) toward them (14:37), al-Ṭabarī explains the word, on the authority of Qatāda, as "yearn toward them,"[^6] an inclination that is praiseworthy in the setting of a supplication. In our reading, then, the condemnation is not a linguistic necessity of the word; it is a Qur'anic usage for the desire that is followed without guidance.

### "Have You Seen the One Who Has Taken His Desire as His God?": When Desire Becomes the Object of Worship

﴿أَفَرَأَيْتَ مَنِ اتَّخَذَ إِلَٰهَهُ هَوَاهُ وَأَضَلَّهُ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ عِلْمٍ وَخَتَمَ عَلَىٰ سَمْعِهِ وَقَلْبِهِ وَجَعَلَ عَلَىٰ بَصَرِهِ غِشَاوَةً﴾ "Have you seen him who has taken his own desire as his god, and God has led him astray with knowledge, and has set a seal upon his hearing and his heart, and put a covering over his sight?" (45:23). It does not say "he followed his desire," which is the prevailing construction, but "he took it as a god," and so it lifts desire from the rank of a motive to the rank of the one worshipped. The commentators differed on the sense of this. Al-Ṭabarī reports that some said: "Have you seen him who has made his religion according to his desire, so that he desires nothing but he indulges in it," while others said: "Rather the meaning is: have you seen him who has taken as the object of his worship whatever thing his own self desires to worship."[^7] Al-Qurṭubī relays the first view from Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḥasan and Qatāda, and from ʿIkrima that he is the one who "made his god, whom he worships, whatever he desires or finds good," and from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr: "One of them would worship a stone, and when he saw something better than it he threw it away and worshipped the other."[^8] These are two readings: desire as a source of reference by which religion is legislated, and desire as a taste by which the object of worship is changed. Al-Zamakhsharī gathers the meaning in a comparison: "He is obedient to the desire of his self and follows whatever it calls him to, so that it is as though he worshipped it as a man worships his god."[^9]

The verse also contains a point which we offer as our own reading and not as a transmitted statement: ﴿وَأَضَلَّهُ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ عِلْمٍ﴾ ("and God has led him astray with knowledge") draws attention to the fact that *hawā* is not merely ignorance. It can coexist with knowledge that is present, but knowledge that does not lead its owner, because the god being followed in the heart is another. The rest of the verse then descends like a series of closings: a seal upon the hearing and the heart, a covering over the sight, as if the one who has made his desire his object of worship had shut upon himself the three gateways of guidance. Then comes the question that has no answer except confession: who will guide him after God? What is meant here is that the desire that is worshipped is not seen as an object of worship, so its owner thinks he is a free man choosing, when he is the captive of what he craves.

### "And Do Not Follow Desire": Desire in the Scales of Judgment

We return to the seat of David, peace be upon him. Al-Ṭabarī says in his commentary on the verse: "Do not prefer your desire, in your judgment between them, over the truth and justice in it, and so deviate from the truth." Al-Qurṭubī says: "Do not take as your guide your desire that is contrary to the command of God," and "so that it leads you astray from the way of God" means "from the path of Paradise."[^10] So the *hawā* here is an inclination in judgment, neither unbelief nor shirk, and it is a prophet and successor who is addressed with it. To the same sense returns God's word to the believers about testimony: ﴿فَلَا تَتَّبِعُوا الْهَوَىٰ أَن تَعْدِلُوا﴾ "So do not follow desire, lest you fail to act justly" (4:135). In both places desire stands in the way of justice: in judging, and in bearing witness, even against oneself, one's parents and one's kin. On this reading the prohibition is not a general admonition but a guard set over the gravest places of trust.

### "He Does Not Speak Out of Desire": Revelation Cleared of Inclination

Then the picture is reversed in Sūrat al-Najm: ﴿وَمَا يَنطِقُ عَنِ الْهَوَىٰ إِنْ هُوَ إِلَّا وَحْيٌ يُوحَىٰ﴾ "Nor does he speak out of desire. It is nothing but a revelation revealed" (53:3-4). Here desire is denied any place in the highest station from which a human being can speak, and the denial is the proof of truthfulness. The commentators paused at the preposition. Al-Qurṭubī relates from Qatāda: "He does not recite the Qur'an out of his own desire," and from Abū ʿUbayda that *ʿan al-hawā* means *bi-l-hawā* ("by desire"), and then quotes al-Naḥḥās: "The view of Qatāda is the better one, and *ʿan* remains in its ordinary sense: his speech does not proceed from his own opinion; it is by revelation from God, the Mighty and Majestic."[^11] There is a benefit in this disagreement, subtle as it is: whether we say *ʿan* or *bi-*, the Qur'an makes desire the very opposite of revelation at the origin of speech. It is either a revelation that is revealed or a desire that is spoken.

### "And Restrained the Self from Desire": Restraint, Not Eradication

In Sūrat al-Nāziʿāt is the scene of the final weighing, between two men: ﴿فَأَمَّا مَن طَغَىٰ وَآثَرَ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا فَإِنَّ الْجَحِيمَ هِيَ الْمَأْوَىٰ وَأَمَّا مَنْ خَافَ مَقَامَ رَبِّهِ وَنَهَى النَّفْسَ عَنِ الْهَوَىٰ فَإِنَّ الْجَنَّةَ هِيَ الْمَأْوَىٰ﴾ "As for him who transgressed and preferred the life of this world, then indeed Hell will be [his] refuge. But as for him who feared the standing before his Lord and restrained the self from desire, then indeed Paradise will be [his] refuge" (79:37-41). The contrast is exact: on the one side transgression and a preference for this world; on the other, fear of the standing before the Lord and restraint of the self from desire. Al-Ṭabarī explains: "He restrained his self from its desire in what God dislikes and does not approve of in it, so he rebuked it for that and went against its desire toward what his Lord commanded him." Al-Qurṭubī says: "He rebuked it from acts of disobedience and from the forbidden," and relays from Sahl: "To leave desire is the key to Paradise."[^12]

The verb "restrained" (*nahā*) deserves reflection. It does not say "removed the self from desire" or "killed its desire," but "restrained it." *Hawā* is a movement that issues from the self and remains as long as the self remains, and what is required is that it be restrained, rebuked and opposed. This is how we read the verse, and it is consistent with the fact that the Qur'an never commanded the abolition of the self; it joined *hawā* to the self in four verses (2:87, 5:70, 53:23, 79:40). In an earlier article we dealt with the description of the self in ﴿إِنَّ النَّفْسَ لَأَمَّارَةٌ بِالسُّوءِ إِلَّا مَا رَحِمَ رَبِّي﴾ "Indeed, the self is a persistent enjoiner of evil, except those upon whom my Lord has mercy" (12:53), in "The Self That Enjoins Evil in the Qur'an."[^13] In our reading, the *ammāra* is the origin that commands, and *hawā* is the movement through which its command appears. Al-Rāghib has defined it as "the inclination of the self toward appetite." The self commands and *hawā* inclines, and between them stands the human being.

### A Man to Whom God Gave His Signs, and He Clung to the Earth

The concept has a human portrait in the Qur'an: ﴿وَاتْلُ عَلَيْهِمْ نَبَأَ الَّذِي آتَيْنَاهُ آيَاتِنَا فَانسَلَخَ مِنْهَا﴾ "And recite to them the news of him to whom We gave Our signs, but he detached himself from them." Then it says: ﴿وَلَوْ شِئْنَا لَرَفَعْنَاهُ بِهَا وَلَٰكِنَّهُ أَخْلَدَ إِلَى الْأَرْضِ وَاتَّبَعَ هَوَاهُ ۚ فَمَثَلُهُ كَمَثَلِ الْكَلْبِ إِن تَحْمِلْ عَلَيْهِ يَلْهَثْ أَوْ تَتْرُكْهُ يَلْهَث﴾ "And had We willed, We would have raised him by them, but he clung to the earth and followed his own desire. So his parable is like that of the dog: if you attack it, it pants, or if you leave it, it pants" (7:175-176). Al-Ṭabarī says of "he clung to the earth": "he settled into the life of this world on the earth and inclined to it, and preferred its pleasure and its appetites over the Hereafter, and followed his desire." It is reported that the man meant is Balʿam, but al-Ṭabarī points out that there is disagreement among the people of knowledge about his story, and in al-Qurṭubī there are several views on what he followed by his desire: that it was what Satan made attractive to him, or that his desire was with the unbelievers, or that it was the pleasing of his wife.[^14] For this reason we are cautious about naming him and stop at what the Qur'an named: a man who was given the signs and then detached himself from them. The harshest element in the picture is the comparison: he does not cease panting, whether he is attacked or left alone, as though *hawā* were a need that is never satisfied. Note also that the verse says that had God willed He would have raised him by the signs, but he clung to the earth. The meaning is a contrast between rising and clinging, the very image that accords with what al-Rāghib said of the origin of the root: a fall from a height to a depth.

### Had the Truth Followed Their Desires

*Hawā* also has a cosmic dimension in God's saying: ﴿وَلَوِ اتَّبَعَ الْحَقُّ أَهْوَاءَهُمْ لَفَسَدَتِ السَّمَاوَاتُ وَالْأَرْضُ وَمَن فِيهِنَّ﴾ "And if the truth had followed their desires, the heavens and the earth and whoever is in them would have been corrupted" (23:71). The commentators differed over who "the truth" is here. Al-Qurṭubī says: "It is God, glory be to Him, as most of them said," and then mentions a view that it is the Qur'an, and a view that it is whatever people desire, so that the order of the world would be voided "because people's appetites differ and conflict."[^15] On any of these views the meaning is one: were the world to turn on people's desires, it would not stand. The Qur'an repeatedly forbids following the "desires" of others after knowledge has come, among them ﴿وَلَئِنِ اتَّبَعْتَ أَهْوَاءَهُم بَعْدَ الَّذِي جَاءَكَ مِنَ الْعِلْمِ﴾ "And if you were to follow their desires after the knowledge that has come to you" (2:120). In al-Māʾida (5:48) there is a prohibition on following their desires in place of the truth that has come, and in al-Jāthiya (45:18) a command to follow the law together with a prohibition on following the desires of those who do not know. The contrast in these passages is striking, and what appears to us from them is that *hawā* is not set against reason, but against the knowledge that came from revelation and the truth that was sent down. Al-Rāghib has pointed to the significance of the plural in this expression when he said: "He used the plural to alert us that every person has a desire other than the desire of another, and the desire of each is without limit; so following their desires is the utmost of misguidance and bewilderment."[^16] The truth is one, the desires are many and in conflict, and whoever abandons the one is lost in the many.

### When the Passages Are Brought Together

When we bring these strands together, what is peculiar to the Qur'an in its treatment of this word becomes clear. *Hawā* is followed, and it is met by truth and knowledge; it is taken as a god, and it is met by the One God; it enters the seat of judgment, and it is met by justice; it is denied of speech, and it is met by revelation; it is restrained, and it is met by fear of the standing before the Lord. In these passages *hawā* stands on one side and revelation on the other, as though there were no third between them. This is what makes it a distinct Qur'anic concept, set apart from "appetite," "wish" and "inclination" in their general sense: it is inclination at the moment it becomes a point of reference.

### A Hadith That Puts Desire in Its Place

The Sunna contains what confirms this meaning. On the authority of ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAmr, may God be pleased with them both, the Prophet ﷺ said: «لا يؤمن أحدكم حتى يكون هواه تبعًا لما جئت به» "None of you believes until his desire is subordinate to (literally, "a follower of") what I have brought."[^17] Notice the wording: it does not say "until he has no desire" but "a follower" (*tabaʿan*). Desire remains, but it moves from the position of the one followed (as in 45:23) to the position of the follower. This yields a gentle reading, which we see as harmonious with "restraining the self": what is asked is not that inclination die, but that its head be bowed before revelation, so that the beloved becomes beloved because God has loved it.

### What This Means for Us Today

In our time *hawā* looks elegant. It does not come in the form of an idol of stone, but in the form of an opinion we love and so go looking for evidence to support, and a position we defend because it is ours and not because it is the truth. Perhaps the question the verses put to each of us is this: do I seek the evidence in order to follow it, or in order to justify what I have already chosen? The difference between the two is the difference between one who "follows the truth" and one who "has taken his desire as his god." It is a question that applies to debate and dispute, to fatwa and teaching, and to the home as well, when anger, pride or the wish to be seen is what selects the opinion and then summons the texts to testify for it.

For the educator, the imam, the teacher, the judge, the father and the mother, the verses hold a special place, for these are people whose word is heard, and whoever's word is heard has a more dangerous inclination in his heart than others, as it was in the seat of David. The first protection is to train ourselves to judge between those we love and those we dislike by one scale, to speak the truth even against ourselves (4:135), and to accept what revelation brought when it contradicts what we crave. For the teaching of the young there is another meaning: that faith is not the suppression of inclinations but their training, for God did not command cutting off the self but restraining it. So we teach them to call their inclinations by their names, and to ask before a decision: toward what am I leaning now, and why? Whoever knows his inclination is no longer led by it without knowing. We also teach them that fear of the standing before the Lord (79:40) is what gives restraint its strength, for whoever does not bring to mind his standing before God will find it hard to rebuke his self for what it loves. And in a report that al-Qurṭubī relays from Ibn Masʿūd without a chain: "You are in a time when truth leads desire, and a time will come when desire leads truth, so we seek refuge in God from that time."[^18] We do not make it an argument, but it describes the condition of those who reverse the order and make desire the leader and truth the follower.

### Conclusion

The Qur'an places desire in one pan of the scale, and truth, knowledge and revelation in the other, and then asks: which of them will you follow? In the root of the word there is an indication that spares much explanation: it is the root of falling, and falling needs no push; it is enough that we leave ourselves alone. O God, make us among those who feared Your station and restrained the self from desire; do not make our god what our selves desire; make our desires follow what Your Prophet ﷺ brought; keep us firm when desires incline us; return us to You with a beautiful return; and make Paradise our refuge on the day we meet You.
