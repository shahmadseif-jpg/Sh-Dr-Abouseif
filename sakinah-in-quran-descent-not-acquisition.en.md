*Sakīnah occurs only six times in the Qurʾan, and always at a moment of crisis. It is not a calm we cultivate but a tranquility God sends down into a heart that cannot steady itself, one that makes steadfastness possible rather than rewarding it.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

In the hollow of a mountain outside Mecca, two men sit in a cave so narrow it holds only the two of them, while above its mouth the feet of their pursuers draw nearer, step by step. Had one of the pursuers looked down at his own feet, he would have seen the cave directly beneath him. In that confinement, when every space for escape has shrunk to a wholly muffled silence, one of the two whispers to his companion: «لَا تَحْزَنْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَنَا» — “Do not grieve; God is with us.” And at that very moment, neither before nor after, when fear has reached its farthest limit, the Qurʾan says: «فَأَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ سَكِينَتَهُ عَلَيْهِ» — “Then God sent down His tranquility (*sakīnah*) upon him” (9:40).

This is the scene whose image recurs wherever the Qurʾan mentions the word *sakīnah*. It does not describe a gentle repose on an ordinary day. It is something sent down from above, at a moment when the heart cannot of itself grow still. This fine distinction, between a stillness acquired through habit and a tranquility sent down from the unseen, is what a tracing of the word's Qurʾanic occurrences brings to light.

### The Word and Its Count

The root s-k-n occurs in the Qurʾan sixty-nine times, in ten different forms. But this total includes semantic branches far removed from one another, which must not be confused with the concept of *sakīnah*, the subject of this article. Among them are *miskīn/masākīn* (23 times, meaning one who is poor and needy), *al-maskanah* (twice, meaning abasement and destitution), *maskan/masākin* (13 times, meaning a dwelling or home), and *sikkīn* (once, meaning the cutting instrument in the story of Joseph)[^1]. All of these are morphological branches of the same root, but they are words independent in meaning, with no direct verbal link between them and the *sakīnah* with which we are concerned.

The word *sakīnah* itself, in its specific nominal form, occurs in the whole Qurʾan only six times: once in the setting of the Children of Israel and the story of Ṭālūt (2:248); once at the Battle of Ḥunayn (9:26); once at the Emigration and the Cave of Thawr (9:40); and three times in succession in the setting of the Treaty of Ḥudaybiyyah (48:4, 48:18, 48:26). Six places only. This small number, set beside the placement of each, is the key to the whole article[^4].

### The Linguistic Root

In its origin in the language, the root s-k-n turns on the meaning of stillness after motion. A thing *sakana* when it quieted after agitation; a person *sakana* a place when he settled in it after wandering; and *al-sakan* is that in which one finds reassurance and at which one rests. Stillness, in its origin, is thus not an initial absence of movement but a calm that follows an agitation which preceded it. This very linguistic origin (a stillness that comes after, not before) applies literally to every occurrence of *sakīnah* in the Qurʾan, as will become clear.

It is notable that the most frequent branch of the whole root is not *sakīnah* but *miskīn* (23 times). By distant derivation it carries the same root of motion, for the *miskīn* is one whose movement has stilled: his means have dwindled and his avenues of earning have been cut off, so that he is like one at rest who cannot move to seek his livelihood. This is a distant thematic link between the two branches, and not a direct verbal witness joining them in the sense of *sakīnah*, the subject of this article. It is mentioned because it reveals the two poles of the root: a stillness imposed on a person by force when his resources are paralyzed (the *miskīn*), and a stillness sent down upon him as an honor when his fear reaches its limit (the *sakīnah*). They are opposite poles of a single arrested motion, the one a trial and the other a gift.

### The Central Structure: A Descent, Not an Acquisition

What stands out in the six passages is that in five of them the act is explicitly ascribed to God in the verb “sent down”: «فَأَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ سَكِينَتَهُ» — “Then God sent down His tranquility” (9:26, 9:40, 48:18, 48:26), or «الَّذِي أَنْزَلَ السَّكِينَةَ» — “He who sent down tranquility” (48:4). Even the sixth (2:248) describes the *sakīnah* as coming “from your Lord” within the Ark. Each time it appears, then, *sakīnah* is an event that befalls the heart from outside, and not a state the heart produces of itself by training or habit. This sets it apart from ordinary stillness (*sakana, yaskunu*), which the Qurʾan itself elsewhere describes in natural terms, as of the night, or of the soul finding repose in its spouse (7:189, 30:21): a stillness within the order of creation, not an exceptional descent from above it.

The most eloquent witness to this distinction lies in a single verse that sets the two opposing meanings side by side: «إِذْ جَعَلَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا فِي قُلُوبِهِمُ الْحَمِيَّةَ حَمِيَّةَ الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ فَأَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ سَكِينَتَهُ عَلَىٰ رَسُولِهِ وَعَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ» — “When those who disbelieved set in their hearts zealotry, the zealotry of the Age of Ignorance, God sent down His tranquility upon His Messenger and upon the believers” (48:26). Zealotry (pride and tribal passion) is a feeling that sprouts from within the human heart itself, needing no outside supply. *Sakīnah* does not sprout; it is sent down. Here the Qurʾan sets before us two models of psychological steadfastness at the moment of confrontation: a firmness that man manufactures from his tribal zeal and his pride, and a firmness that God bestows on those who rely on Him. The verse portrays the second party as the steadier, because its source does not run dry, as zealotry does when the confrontation is prolonged.

### Qurʾanic Models

In the story of Ṭālūt, *sakīnah* is the sign of legitimate kingship: «إِنَّ آيَةَ مُلْكِهِ أَنْ يَأْتِيَكُمُ التَّابُوتُ فِيهِ سَكِينَةٌ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ» — “The sign of his kingship is that the Ark will come to you, in which is tranquility from your Lord” (2:248). The legitimacy of leadership among the Children of Israel at that moment was measured, that is, by the presence of this divine stillness, and not by lineage or standing. At Ḥunayn, after some of the Muslims had turned and fled in the shock of the surprise, *sakīnah* descended upon the Prophet ﷺ and the believers to restore the cohesion of the broken line (9:26). In the Cave of Thawr it descended upon a single heart at the narrowest imaginable moment, when nothing separated safety from danger but a downward glance (9:40). And at Ḥudaybiyyah, where there was no visible fighting but a long political tension and a treaty that seemed to some unjust, *sakīnah* recurs three times (48:4, 18, 26) to bring the believers to accept what their souls could not at first bear.

One thread binds all five passages: a moment of tension in which the human heart, on its own, cannot grow still. A losing battle, the danger of pursuit, a treaty in which right seems trampled: all these are moments beyond the capacity of natural endurance, so that a descent from above becomes the only possible resolution.

The Treaty of Ḥudaybiyyah deserves a particular pause, since it is the only occasion on which *sakīnah* is repeated three times within a single sura, though it was not a military confrontation in the direct sense. The believers on that journey were barred from entering Mecca although they had set out for the lesser pilgrimage and not for battle, and a treaty was signed in which some saw a plain injustice to the Muslims' right, so that ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb (may God be pleased with him) voiced the well-known objection recorded in the Sīrah. This is another kind of fear, no less sharp than the fear of battle: the fear that the truth will be trampled before the eyes of its people while they are powerless to change the situation. And at that very moment, not a moment of victory but a moment of accepting terms that seemed unjust, *sakīnah* descends three times in succession, keeping hearts steady on the hard decision until, two years later, it was revealed that Ḥudaybiyyah had been a conquest and not a defeat.

### The Prophetic Witness

To what the Qurʾan says of moments of fear and confrontation, the Sunnah adds another dimension: *sakīnah* descends also upon the recitation of the Qurʾan, at a moment when there is no fear at all. On the authority of al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib (may God be pleased with him): a man was reciting Sūrat al-Kahf with a tethered horse beside him, and the horse began to shy and bolt. When he finished his recitation, behold, a cloud or a mist had enveloped him. He mentioned this to the Prophet ﷺ, who said: «اقرأ فلان، فإنها السكينة نزلت للقرآن» — “Recite, So-and-so, for that was the tranquility (*sakīnah*) that descended for the Qurʾan”[^2]. Here *sakīnah* descends not in answer to fear of an enemy but in answer to a confrontation of another kind: the heart's direct encounter with the speech of God, an encounter bearing such awe that it calls for the same steadying as battle.

### Two Maqāṣidī Readings

Ibn al-Qayyim distinguished between *sakīnah* and *ṭumaʾnīnah* (reassurance), two words that are sometimes given a single translation despite the difference in their linguistic origin (for *ṭumaʾnīnah* is from the root ṭ-m-n, as in «أَلَا بِذِكْرِ اللَّهِ تَطْمَئِنُّ الْقُلُوبُ» — “Truly, in the remembrance of God do hearts find reassurance,” 13:28). He said that *sakīnah* “is the reassurance, the dignity, and the stillness which God sends down into the heart of His servant when it is shaken by the intensity of its fears,” and added that there is a difference between the two: that *sakīnah* has a steadfastness resembling a bold advance, which may bring awe, while *ṭumaʾnīnah* is the stillness of intimacy, resembling repose[^3]. This distinction agrees entirely with what the tracing of the passages has shown: *ṭumaʾnīnah* is a general state every believer seeks at all times through the remembrance of God, whereas *sakīnah* is a special succor that descends at the moment of crisis in particular.

A second reading notes, from the context of 48:4, that the function of *sakīnah* is not abstract psychological repose but the service of faith itself: «هُوَ الَّذِي أَنْزَلَ السَّكِينَةَ فِي قُلُوبِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ لِيَزْدَادُوا إِيمَانًا مَعَ إِيمَانِهِمْ» — “It is He who sent down tranquility into the hearts of the believers, that they might increase in faith along with their faith.” The purpose of *sakīnah* is not to calm fear for its own sake, but to enable the heart to grow in certainty at the very moment when its certainty might have wavered. *Sakīnah*, in this sense, is not an escape from confrontation but the instrument of steadfastness within it.

The two readings converge on a single conclusion: contrary to what might be supposed, *sakīnah* is not a reward for steadfastness but a condition of it. The order in the verses is not “be steadfast, and tranquility will descend upon you,” but “tranquility descends, and so they are steadfast.” The divine act precedes the human one and does not follow it. This explains why the Qurʾan insists on ascribing *sakīnah* to God alone in verbal form (“sent down”) in five of six places, without leaving the servant any direct act described as his having “attained” or “acquired” *sakīnah* by his own effort. The most the servant can do is prepare for its descent, through sincerity, reliance, and submission to the command, but not manufacture it.

### The Contemporary Application

Many today confuse the pursuit of “quiet,” understood as the absence of stimulus (seclusion, silence, a holiday), with *sakīnah* in its Qurʾanic sense, which is a steadfastness sought in the very eye of the confrontation and not away from it. Whoever faces a financial crisis, a grave medical diagnosis, or a dispute that cannot be avoided need not flee the situation to find his *sakīnah*. He needs a succor that steadies him while he stands in the midst of it, and this is exactly what the Qurʾan describes: a tranquility that descends upon the one in the cave, not upon the one who fled from it.

This does not mean dispensing with means. The one in the cave took the means of concealment, and the one who faced Ḥunayn steadied the line after reforming it. The difference is that means alone, when fear reaches its limit, cannot of themselves produce steadfastness. Here, turning to what is greater than the means is the act that all the verses describe: remembrance, reliance, and sincere supplication, and not passive waiting.

There is in this a correction of a common notion that strength of faith means the absence of fear altogether. All six passages presuppose a real fear that came before: Companions who turned back in flight at Ḥunayn; a grieving companion in the cave who was told “Do not grieve”; believers whose hearts were troubled by the terms of a treaty they did not welcome. *Sakīnah* is therefore not the sign that fear is absent but the sign that it has been crossed. This is a distinction that consoles anyone who supposes that his first agitation in a crisis is proof of weak faith, when it is rather the beginning of the very ground upon which *sakīnah* is sent down, and not its opposite.

### Conclusion

Only six times does *sakīnah* occur in the Qurʾan, and each time it descends upon a heart that has reached the uttermost limit of fear or awe, not upon a heart at rest. This is what makes Qurʾanic *sakīnah* deeper than its common synonym, “calm”: it is not the absence of the storm but steadfastness in the midst of it, when there is sent down from above what cannot be produced from below. And God Most High knows best; with Him is all success.
