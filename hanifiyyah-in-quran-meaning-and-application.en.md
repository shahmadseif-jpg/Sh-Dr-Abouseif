*Ḥanīf is the Qurʾan's word for one who inclines away from falsehood toward uprightness, and this study follows its twelve occurrences from Abraham to the Prophet to the whole community. Ḥanīfiyyah emerges as an innate disposition, borne out in practice and joined to ease, and as a continuous movement rather than a station at which one comes to rest.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

Ḥanīfiyyah is a term around which commentary and opinion have multiplied: now clarifying its meaning, now unfolding its implications, now tracing it back to its linguistic root, until the reader supposes he has grasped it from sheer repetition, while in truth he still stands at its edges. The word recurs in the Qurʾan twelve times. Eight of these are bound to the name of Abraham alone; two address the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ directly, without mention of Abraham; and two final instances widen to embrace the whole community. This disparity in number and in the one addressed is the first thing worth tracing, before we ask about meaning, evidence, purpose, and the character of those who seek it today.

### The Word in the Qurʾan: Singular and Plural

Whoever examines the Qurʾan finds that the word occurs in two forms only and no third: ḥanīfan in the singular, and ḥunafāʾ in the plural. The noun ḥanīfiyyah itself, as a technical term, does not occur in the Qurʾanic text at all; it is a Prophetic expression later distilled from the Qurʾanic description.

The singular form occurs in ten places, eight of which wind directly around the name of Abraham or his creed. In the disputation with the People of the Book, when it was said,

﴿كُونُوا هُودًا أَوْ نَصَارَى﴾
*"Be Jews or Christians,"*

the reply came decisive:

﴿قُلْ بَلْ مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا﴾ (2:135)
*"Say: Rather, the creed of Abraham, ḥanīfan."*

In settling the dispute over his affiliation:

﴿مَا كَانَ إِبْرَاهِيمُ يَهُودِيًّا وَلَا نَصْرَانِيًّا وَلَكِنْ كَانَ حَنِيفًا مُسْلِمًا﴾ (3:67)
*"Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but he was a ḥanīf, a Muslim."*

The command to follow his creed then recurs in three further places (3:95; 4:125; 16:123). The Qurʾan gives his own pure declaration of monotheism in his own words:

﴿إِنِّي وَجَّهْتُ وَجْهِيَ لِلَّذِي فَطَرَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ حَنِيفًا وَمَا أَنَا مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ﴾ (6:79)
*"Indeed, I have turned my face toward Him who originated the heavens and the earth, ḥanīfan, and I am not of the polytheists."*

It describes his comprehensive state:

﴿إِنَّ إِبْرَاهِيمَ كَانَ أُمَّةً قَانِتًا لِلَّهِ حَنِيفًا﴾ (16:120)
*"Indeed, Abraham was a nation, devoutly obedient to God, ḥanīfan."*

And it commands the Prophet to affiliate himself with his creed:

﴿قُلْ إِنَّنِي هَدَانِي رَبِّي إِلَى صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ دِينًا قِيَمًا مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا﴾ (6:161)
*"Say: Indeed, my Lord has guided me to a straight path, an upright religion, the creed of Abraham, ḥanīfan."*

The remaining two instances of the singular address the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ alone, with no mention of Abraham:

﴿وَأَنْ أَقِمْ وَجْهَكَ لِلدِّينِ حَنِيفًا﴾ (10:105)
*"And set your face toward the religion, ḥanīfan,"*

and

﴿فَأَقِمْ وَجْهَكَ لِلدِّينِ حَنِيفًا فِطْرَتَ اللَّهِ الَّتِي فَطَرَ النَّاسَ عَلَيْهَا﴾ (30:30)
*"So set your face toward the religion, ḥanīfan: the fiṭrah of God upon which He originated mankind."*

The plural, ḥunafāʾ, occurs in two places that name neither Abraham nor Muhammad, but address believers generally, all who are commanded to sincerity:

﴿حُنَفَاءَ لِلَّهِ غَيْرَ مُشْرِكِينَ بِهِ﴾ (22:31)
*"…ḥunafāʾ to God, not associating anything with Him,"*

and

﴿مَا أُمِرُوا إِلَّا لِيَعْبُدُوا اللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ حُنَفَاءَ﴾ (98:5)
*"They were not commanded except to worship God, sincere to Him in religion, as ḥunafāʾ."*

This gradation is no accident but a deliberate architecture: from one particular prophet, to a final prophet commanded to follow him, to an entire community commanded to embody the description. The attribute widens as a circle widens around a stone dropped into water, from a single center to a boundless circumference.

### Who Was Described as Ḥanīf?

At the center of this circle stands one man alone: Abraham (peace be upon him), the original bearer of the description, whom no other prophet shares by name, neither Noah, nor Moses, nor Jesus. This distinction is tied to his standing as the head of the creeds that the religions were later to contest. The circle then widens by a step to include the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, not by name but by command; his following of ḥanīfiyyah is the following of an origin prior to him, and no invention of something new. It widens a last step to include believers in general in the plural form, so that the description passes from the individual prophet to the whole community.

History, too, records that on the eve of the Prophet's mission the Arabian Peninsula knew a handful of seekers whose unease was never stilled by the worship of idols, and who found no rest in Judaism or Christianity. They came to be called "the ḥunafāʾ." Most prominent among them were Quss ibn Sāʿidah al-Iyādī and Zayd ibn ʿAmr ibn Nufayl, who went on searching until he died, years before the mission. Of him the Prophet ﷺ said after his death: «إِنَّهُ يُبْعَثُ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ أُمَّةً وَحْدَهُ» — "He will be raised on the Day of Resurrection as a nation unto himself" (reported by al-Bukhārī, in the chapter on the merits of the Anṣār). These individuals bear witness that a remnant of fiṭrah had not been extinguished in the Peninsula, a remnant that the very root of the word explains before history does.

### The Meaning of the Term, and Why Islam Alone Claimed It

In its linguistic root, ḥanaf is an inclination from misguidance toward uprightness, in contrast to janaf, an inclination from uprightness toward misguidance. This exact contrast between the two letters, ḥāʾ and jīm, reveals that the Arabs distinguished in language between two kinds of inclination and reserved praise for one alone. The word's origin is a tender, sensory image: a man whose foot turns inward is called aḥnaf, whence the Companion Ṣakhr ibn Qays took his epithet. This inclination of the foot was carried over, in a remarkable transfer, to an inclination of the heart. The ḥanīf is one who inclines away from false religions toward monotheism, an inclination that looks like crookedness to anyone accustomed to collective conformity, yet is, in the balance of truth, the straightest of paths.

One may ask here: why did the philologists choose the word "inclination" and not "uprightness" directly, when the ḥanīf's very aim is uprightness itself? The answer, as the exegetes have subtly noted, is that shirk is itself an inclination away from the original fiṭrah. When a person rises to counter that inclination with an opposing inclination toward monotheism, he is, as it were, inclined away from the inclined: two opposing inclinations whose resultant is uprightness. This is what Imam al-Marāghī approached in his commentary on the verse of al-Rūm, when he said: "ḥanīfan, from ḥanaf, which is inclination; so he is one who inclines from misguidance toward uprightness." It is a truer description than another account that makes ḥanaf identical with uprightness without the mediation of inclination. "Uprightness" alone carries in its structure no reference to what came before it, whereas "inclination" carries within it the very history of the deviation it corrects, and so describes a movement of correction and not the state of stillness that follows it. Nor is a human being asked, by this description, to incline against his own nature. That is a question the tongue alone cannot answer, but the Sunnah does, as will follow.

This same inclination is what led Islam to claim the term for itself, as the saying of God declares:

﴿مَا كَانَ إِبْرَاهِيمُ يَهُودِيًّا وَلَا نَصْرَانِيًّا وَلَكِنْ كَانَ حَنِيفًا مُسْلِمًا﴾ (3:67)
*"Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but he was a ḥanīf, a Muslim."*

This verse was revealed while the People of the Book were disputing over Abraham, each side pulling him toward its own tent, and the reply came decisive: he was not merely prior to them in history, but stood outside their very categories in essence. Observe the pairing of "ḥanīfan" with "Muslim" in a single description. The verse does not make ḥanīfiyyah a third creed added to the contested ones; it makes it the very essence of Islam in its first origin, before the laws of the prophets branched from it and were then corrupted.

Here is a point of comparison worth dwelling on at length. The nations that dispute today over "who owns the truth" or "who inherits the great symbols" repeat, without realizing it, the dispute of the People of the Book over Abraham, each faction seeking to annex the great symbol to its own tent in order to gain historical legitimacy. The Qurʾan settles this dispute not by adding a third tent, but by raising Abraham above all the contending tents to a station that cannot be measured by tribal affiliation, but only by purity of heartfelt direction. For this reason the naming of "the Muslims" was likewise bound to the creed of Abraham in the same context:

﴿مِلَّةَ أَبِيكُمْ إِبْرَاهِيمَ هُوَ سَمَّاكُمُ الْمُسْلِمِينَ﴾ (22:78)
*"…the creed of your father Abraham; it is He who named you Muslims,"*

though most exegetes, such as al-Ṭabarī, hold that the pronoun "He" refers to God and not to Abraham. On either reading, the naming remains inseparable from the creed of Abraham, and precedes Muhammad ﷺ in both cases.

### The Witness of the Sunnah to Ḥanīfiyyah, and the Determination of Its Meaning

Nor is a human being asked to incline against his own nature, and this is precisely what the Sunnah discloses when it fixes the meaning through three hadiths, each completing the others, none to be separated from the rest.

The first is the hadith of fiṭrah: «كلُّ مولودٍ يولد على الفطرة، فأبواه يهوّدانه أو يُنصّرانه أو يُمجّسانه» — "Every child is born upon the fiṭrah; then his parents make him a Jew, a Christian, or a Zoroastrian."[^1] It directly explains the verse of al-Rūm, "the fiṭrah of God upon which He originated mankind," and establishes that ḥanīfiyyah is an innate origin prior to upbringing, not something acquired afterward. The inclination the tongue described is not a struggle against nature but agreement with it, since it is shirk that is the intruder, not monotheism.

The second is the hadith of the practices of fiṭrah: «خمسٌ من الفطرة: الختان، والاستحداد، وقصّ الشارب، وتقليم الأظفار، ونتف الإبط» — "Five things belong to the fiṭrah: circumcision, shaving the pubic hair, trimming the mustache, clipping the nails, and plucking the armpit hair."[^2] It brings fiṭrah down from abstraction into tangible practice, so that ḥanīfiyyah does not remain a belief suspended without trace in the body or in habit.

The third is the pair of hadiths on "the tolerant ḥanīfiyyah," which close the circle: «بُعثتُ بالحنيفيّة السمحة» — "I was sent with the tolerant ḥanīfiyyah,"[^3] and «أحبُّ الدين إلى الله الحنيفيّةُ السمحة» — "The religion most beloved to God is the tolerant ḥanīfiyyah."[^4]

Ḥanīfiyyah, then, is an innate origin, it has practical witnesses, and it is bound to tolerance and not to severity. These are three conditions, and the word cannot be understood with fewer than all three together.

### The Sayings of the Scholars in Light of the Objective-Based Vision

Upon these three conditions, fiṭrah, practical witness, and tolerance, the scholars of the higher objectives (maqāṣid) built their reading of ḥanīfiyyah. Foremost among them is Ibn ʿĀshūr, who counted tolerance, in *Maqāṣid al-Sharīʿah al-Islāmiyyah*, as "the foremost attribute of this Sharīʿah and its greatest objective," and defined it as "ease of dealing in matters where people are accustomed to contention": a mean between severity and laxity, favoring neither extreme. He explained the wisdom of tolerance's pairing with ḥanīfiyyah by noting that the Sharīʿah is "the religion of fiṭrah," and that matters of fiṭrah return to an innate disposition already present within souls, so that their acceptance comes easily, unlike a duty foreign to the very nature of creation. The inclination, in his view, as we have seen in both the linguistic root and the hadith, is an inclination that accords with innate disposition and does not oppose it.

Ibn Taymiyyah, in *Iqtiḍāʾ al-Ṣirāṭ al-Mustaqīm li-Mukhālafat Aṣḥāb al-Jaḥīm*, tied ḥanīfiyyah to a different objective, the preservation of identity and distinctiveness. He held that differing from the other religious communities in outward practice is part of the meaning of ḥanīfiyyah, not because difference is sought for its own sake, but because imitation dissolves, step by step, the marks that distinguish pure monotheism from whatever deviation has mingled with it. In the same vein, Ibn al-Qayyim, in *Miftāḥ Dār al-Saʿādah*, treated fiṭrah and ḥanīfiyyah as two faces of a single reality: the soul, so long as it is not diverted from its origin by some external influence, inclines of itself to acknowledge the one Creator. Monotheism, in his view, is the recovery of what already lies within the self and no planting from without. From the standpoint of the objectives, this meets what Ibn ʿĀshūr established regarding tolerance as ease, for it too accords with innate disposition and does not oppose it.

### The Character of Those Who Seek Ḥanīfiyyah

From these readings taken together emerges a portrait of the true seeker of ḥanīfiyyah, no part of which stands apart from the rest. He is, first of all, an active seeker, not a passive claimant of affiliation, for the historical ḥunafāʾ did not wait for the truth; they went out asking after it until they found it. His inclination is sound only through a sincerity that keeps him from taking ḥanīfiyyah as a name without substance, as in

﴿مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ حُنَفَاءَ﴾ (98:5)
*"…sincere to Him in religion, as ḥunafāʾ."*

Nor does his sincerity stop at the boundary of belief. It widens, as Abraham's own direction widened when he said "I have turned my face," to embrace every avenue of life, and no single rite alone. At the same time, he does not confuse the severity owed to the root of belief with the tolerance owed to the branches of dealing, hardening in matters of monotheism while being lax with himself in his dealings, or doing the reverse and losing the balance either way. And what distinguishes him last is steadfastness before the pressure of inherited custom, as Abraham parted from his father and his people in pursuit of truth. Ḥanīfiyyah, for him, is a trial to be faced anew in every generation, and no description inherited automatically.

### Conclusion

After this full circuit it becomes clear that the multiplication of sayings around ḥanīfiyyah was not a proliferation that lost the meaning, but a bringing of it into clearer view from every side. We have seen, in the very root of the language, why this inclination, and not uprightness alone, is the more precise description: it carries within it the history of the deviation it corrects, and not the stillness that follows correction. What remains at the core of it all, however many its facets, is one thing undivided: monotheism is a continuous inclination toward uprightness, never a station reached where its seeker comes to rest.

It is enough for the seeker in this inclination to go on walking in it, and not to halt at a limit he supposes to be its end. Knowledge rests with God alone, and He is the Guide to the straight path.
