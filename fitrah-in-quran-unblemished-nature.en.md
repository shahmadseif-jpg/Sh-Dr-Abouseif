---

*The root f-ṭ-r first means to begin a thing upon no prior model, as God originated the heavens and the earth. Man's fiṭrah is a copy of that first origination: a sound origin that carries no flaw of its own and can be marred only from without. The task of education and daʿwah is therefore to guard what God has deposited, not to plant it from nothing.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

An animal is born whole, sound in its limbs, with no notch in its ear and no severed member; and if someone later comes to cut or slit it, the cutting is something imposed from outside, not a trait within the animal. With this simple and tangible example, familiar to anyone who has watched livestock being born, the Prophet ﷺ chose to bring home to his Companions the most delicate question about the original nature of man: «كُلُّ مَوْلُودٍ يُولَدُ عَلَى الفِطْرَةِ، فَأَبَوَاهُ يُهَوِّدَانِهِ أَوْ يُنَصِّرَانِهِ أَوْ يُمَجِّسَانِهِ، كَمَثَلِ البَهِيمَةِ تُنْتَجُ البَهِيمَةَ، هَلْ تَرَى فِيهَا جَدْعَاءَ؟» — "Every child is born upon the fiṭrah; then his parents make him a Jew, a Christian, or a Magian, just as an animal gives birth to a whole animal: do you perceive any mutilation in it?"[^1] The newborn, as it comes into life, is not a neutral page waiting for the first mark to be drawn upon it. Like the perfectly formed animal, it stands upon a sound origin with no defect in it, and whatever deviation appears afterward is imposed from outside that origin and is no deficiency lying hidden within it.

This sound origin is what a single verse in the Book of God calls *al-fiṭrah*:

﴿فَأَقِمْ وَجْهَكَ لِلدِّينِ حَنِيفًا فِطْرَتَ اللَّهِ الَّتِي فَطَرَ النَّاسَ عَلَيْهَا لَا تَبْدِيلَ لِخَلْقِ اللَّهِ ذَلِكَ الدِّينُ الْقَيِّمُ وَلَٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَ النَّاسِ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ﴾ (30:30)
*"So set your face toward the religion, inclining to truth (ḥanīf): the fiṭrah of God upon which He originated mankind. There is no altering the creation of God. That is the upright religion, but most people do not know."*

Whoever followed our earlier study of *ḥanīfiyyah* will not miss that this very verse brings the two words together, "ḥanīfan" and "the fiṭrah of God." It is as though the steady inclination toward uprightness, which is ḥanīfiyyah, moves upon ground already prepared for it, which is the fiṭrah: ḥanīfiyyah is a motion, and the fiṭrah is the earth on which that motion stands. Yet the word fiṭrah itself occurs in the Qurʾan in this one place only, while its linguistic root f-ṭ-r turns in twenty places in six different forms.[^2] This very distribution, as we shall see, discloses the reality of the fiṭrah more fully than the single verse can do alone.

### Twenty Occurrences and the Three Circles That Enclose the Word

The root f-ṭ-r is distributed in the Qurʾan across three distinct circles. The first is the verb faṭara in its various forms (faṭaranī, faṭaranā, faṭarakum, faṭarahunna). It occurs ten times, most often on the tongues of prophets who argue against their peoples with a single irrefutable proof: the One who originated me at the beginning is alone worthy of worship. Such are the words of Abraham (peace be upon him):

﴿إِنِّي وَجَّهْتُ وَجْهِيَ لِلَّذِي فَطَرَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ﴾ (6:79)
*"Indeed, I have turned my face toward the One who originated the heavens and the earth,"*

and

﴿وَمَا لِيَ لَا أَعْبُدُ الَّذِي فَطَرَنِي﴾ (36:22)
*"And why should I not worship the One who originated me?"*

though the speaker in Yā-Sīn is a believing man from the town, and not Abraham himself. Such too are the magicians' words to Pharaoh:

﴿وَالَّذِي فَطَرَنَا﴾ (20:72)
*"…and the One who originated us."*

The second circle is the noun Fāṭir as one of the most beautiful names of God. It occurs six times, always in the construction "Originator of the heavens and the earth" (6:14, 12:101, 14:10, 35:1, 39:46, 42:11). The third circle describes origination from its reverse side: fuṭūr (67:3), meaning a rift or flaw, and munfaṭir, infaṭarat, and yatafaṭṭarna (73:18, 82:1, 19:90, 42:5), meaning splitting and fracturing. The word fiṭrah, the noun we know today, falls only once, in the very verse in which the verb faṭara falls (30:30), completing the twenty.

A methodological note is due here. These twenty are all direct verbal witnesses to the root f-ṭ-r itself. Verses that speak of man's origin from another angle, such as

﴿لَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا الْإِنْسَانَ فِي أَحْسَنِ تَقْوِيمٍ﴾ (95:4)
*"We have certainly created man in the best of stature,"*

are a near thematic connection that enriches the subject but is not counted among the occurrences of the root f-ṭ-r. It will be given its own place, without being confused with the verbal witness.

### The Splitting That Is Origination

The people of the language say that *al-faṭr* is a lengthwise splitting, and that the root sense of "faṭara a thing" is that he began it and brought it into being upon no prior model, and not that he fashioned it from existing matter as a craftsman works with what lies before him. For this reason "Originator (Fāṭir) of the heavens and the earth" was explained as "the One who began them with no prior model." This is more precise than "Creator" in this particular place, since creating may be a proportioning upon prior matter, whereas originating is a bringing-into-being with no precedent. Ibn Kathīr relates in his commentary, on the authority of Ibn ʿAbbās (may God be pleased with them both): "I did not know what 'Originator of the heavens and the earth' meant, until two bedouins came to me disputing over a well, and one of them said: «أنا فطرتُها» — I began it."[^3] The bedouin did not mean that he dug the well out of nothing, but that he was the first to begin its digging. This is precisely the meaning of *faṭr*: to bring a thing into being at the beginning, and not to continue upon a prior trace.

Once this is understood in "Originator of the heavens and the earth," the secret of the choice of this very root, and no other, for describing man's origin becomes plain. Man's fiṭrah is not, like ḥanīfiyyah, an acquired inclination built up by nurture and choice. It is a copy within man of that first origination itself: an origin deposited in every newborn at its bringing-into-being, neither drawn from a prior exemplar nor gained through instruction. The heart's knowledge of its Lord, as Ibn Taymiyyah will show below, is therefore a necessary, innate knowledge, and not an acquired, theoretical one.

### From Origination to Flaw

Here appears the structure that gathers the three circles into a single fabric. The first circle, the origination of the heavens, describes a cosmic origination with no prior model. The second, man's fiṭrah, describes a trace of that same origination deposited in every human being. The third describes what happens when this origin is contravened. In the description of the sky on the Day of Resurrection:

﴿فَارْجِعِ الْبَصَرَ هَلْ تَرَىٰ مِنْ فُطُورٍ﴾ (67:3)
*"So return your vision: do you see any rifts (fuṭūr)?"*

That is: do you find any split or flaw in its structure? The implied answer is no, for it is perfect in its building, having been originated without defect. Then other verses describe the splitting of that very structure when the matter reaches its utmost:

﴿إِذَا السَّمَاءُ انْفَطَرَتْ﴾ (82:1)
*"When the sky is cleft asunder,"*

﴿السَّمَاءُ مُنْفَطِرٌ بِهِ﴾ (73:18)
*"…the sky will be split apart thereby."*

Indeed, the heavens all but burst asunder in horror at the enormity of associating partners with God:

﴿تَكَادُ السَّمَاوَاتُ يَتَفَطَّرْنَ مِنْهُ﴾ (19:90)
*"The heavens almost rupture from it,"*

﴿يَتَفَطَّرْنَ مِنْ فَوْقِهِنَّ﴾ (42:5)
*"…rupturing from above them."*

The single fabric says: what is originated upon a sound origin has no rift in it, yet it is liable to split if its order is assaulted. The same may be said of man's fiṭrah. On the day it is deposited in the newborn it is a sound origin without defect, "as an animal gives birth to a whole animal" in which no mutilation is seen. Yet, like a sky that almost bursts at the horror of what is said against God, it is liable to suffer an external assault that inflicts a fracture never original to it. This is the structure that the root itself states before any commentator does: a sound origin, the possibility of assault, and a permanent distinction between the two.

### An Animal with No Mutilation in It

The hadith with which this article opened is the widest Prophetic witness to the meaning, and its authenticity is agreed upon. Al-Bukhārī narrated it in the Book of Funerals, and Muslim in the Book of Divine Decree, on the authority of Abū Hurayrah.[^1] It carries three significations. First, that "every child," without exception, is born upon this origin; the fiṭrah is not particular to a nation or a lineage. Second, that deviation from it is ascribed to an external act, "his parents make him a Jew," and not to a deficiency in the newborn; the act is attributed to the parents and not to the child. Third, that the likeness of the sound-limbed animal turns the word fuṭūr (67:3) into a tangible image: as you find no mutilation in the sound animal, so the fiṭrah has, originally, no rift in it.

### Between Two Positions on Interpreting the Hadith

The scholars of old differed over what is meant by "the fiṭrah" in this hadith in particular, and Ibn al-Qayyim summarized the disagreement at length in his *Shifāʾ al-ʿAlīl*.[^4] One group held that what is meant is the constitution upon which the newborn is created, an innate readiness to know its Lord, such that if it were left without external influence it would believe. Another group held it to be more general than this: the original disposition toward acknowledgment and submission, receptive to either belief or disbelief, but not belief itself. Whichever position we favor, both agree that deviation is incidental and not original, and that the origin is a readiness for truth, not a preparedness for falsehood. This is just what Ibn Taymiyyah affirmed when he said: "Acknowledgment and recognition of the Creator is innate and necessary in the souls of people, even though some may meet with what corrupts their fiṭrah, so that they come to need a reflection by which knowledge is attained."[^5] The heart's knowledge of its Lord, for Ibn Taymiyyah, is not an inference acquired by reflection at the outset, but an innate acknowledgment that may need, after it has been corrupted, a reflection to return it to its origin, and not a reflection to produce it from nothing.

From a complementary angle, that of the higher objectives (maqāṣid), al-Shāṭibī built his whole understanding of legal responsibility (taklīf) on the principle that the Sharīʿah did not come to impose upon man a nature foreign to him, but to return him to what accords with his fiṭrah, until obedience becomes a second nature to him and no burden upon him. Taklīf, on this view, is not a building from nothing but a restoration of an origin already standing. This explains a phenomenon that every preacher and educator knows before reading it in a book: that the heaviest religious discourse for the soul is not what calls it to truth, but what is loaded upon it of customs and traditions foreign to it, in the name of a religion to which they do not belong. The fiṭrah is weighed down by what was never imposed upon it, and recoils from religion when it is in truth recoiling from accretions attached to it. With this a single thread is completed, passing from language to hadith to the objectives: the fiṭrah is an origin that is not manufactured; religion's function is to guard it and not to invent it; and everything added to it that is not of it is a burden weighing down the origin, and no part of it.

### Guarding, Not Planting

A fine practical distinction follows between two models in education and daʿwah. The first model treats the heart as barren ground in which every good must be planted from zero, so that conveying the message becomes an attempt to plant something foreign to the soul. The second model, which the hadith and the verse state together, treats the heart as fertile ground in which the seed of knowledge already lies, and the task of the educator and the caller is only to guard this seed from the debris that gathers upon it, and not to plant it from the beginning.

This distinction is not merely theoretical. The father who supposes he must "convince" his son of faith, as he would convince him of some foreign opinion, addresses him differently from the father who knows that within his son lies an innate readiness for truth, and that his first task is to lift the veils from it and not to import a conviction from outside. The caller who addresses the listener's fiṭrah has less need to bind him with intricate arguments than to clear from his heart the heedlessness and noise accumulated upon it. In this there is also protection from two opposite pitfalls: that the role of nurture and human choice be annulled, so that it is said every human being will be guided without any apparent cause; or that innate readiness be denied, so that faith is supposed a mere social habit that could be exchanged for another with no consequence. The fiṭrah, as the Qurʾan has drawn it, keeps both doors open together: a sound origin from God, and a human responsibility to guard it or to assault it.

Among the clearest witnesses to this meaning in our own day is the fact that many who drift from religion in noisy materialist societies do not linger long in agnosticism. At moments of loss, of sincere reflection, or in the face of death, they return to a question they were never taught: who is behind this existence? That very moment is what the hadith described as the fiṭrah not erased but covered over by accumulated dust, veiled and not destroyed. It is what the skilled caller turns to account when he addresses the heart rather than the dispute, clearing away the veils instead of producing out of nothing a proof the heart never needed in the first place.

### Conclusion

The One who originated the heavens and the earth at the beginning, upon no prior model, deposited in every newborn a copy of that origination: a sound origin with no rift in it, like the perfectly formed animal. And as the sky all but bursts in horror when what does not befit Him is said against God, so too man's fiṭrah is liable to suffer an external assault that splits its first purity. Between these two poles, the sound origin and the possibility of assault upon it, falls the whole responsibility of nurture, daʿwah, and choice. There is no altering the creation of God; that is the upright religion, but most people do not know.
