*A study of a term that appears eight times in Sūrat Yūsuf out of its seventeen occurrences, and of the difference between its Qurʾānic meaning and the meaning it acquired among later scholars. By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA).*

In prison, before interpreting the dreams of his two companions, Yūsuf says to them: ﴿لَا يَأْتِيكُمَا طَعَامٌ تُرْزَقَانِهِ إِلَّا نَبَّأْتُكُمَا بِتَأْوِيلِهِ قَبْلَ أَن يَأْتِيَكُمَا﴾ ("No food will come to you as your provision but I shall inform you of its taʾwīl before it comes to you") (Yūsuf 12:37). And at the close of the sūrah, after he sees his parents and brothers prostrating before him, he says: ﴿يَا أَبَتِ هَٰذَا تَأْوِيلُ رُؤْيَايَ مِن قَبْلُ قَدْ جَعَلَهَا رَبِّي حَقًّا﴾ ("O my father, this is the taʾwīl of my vision of long ago; my Lord has made it true") (Yūsuf 12:100). Between the two passages two meanings of taʾwīl appear: the first is the exposition and interpretation of the dream; the second is its occurrence and realization in actual fact. This variety is the key to understanding the term as a whole.

### The Word and Its Count

The root *a-w-l*, in the form *taʾwīl* and its derivatives, occurs seventeen times in the Qurʾān, eight of them in Sūrat Yūsuf alone, that is, nearly half of the occurrences[^1]. This makes the sūrah the first point of reference for understanding the term. Its occurrences there fall into two kinds: "the taʾwīl of narratives" (*taʾwīl al-aḥādīth*) (12:6, 21, 101), and "the taʾwīl of the dream" or what is connected with it (12:36, 37, 44, 45, 100).

Outside the sūrah the word occurs in other important contexts: in the verse on those firmly grounded in knowledge (Āl ʿImrān 3:7); in referring disputes back to God and the Messenger: ﴿ذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ وَأَحْسَنُ تَأْوِيلًا﴾ ("That is better and fairer in outcome [taʾwīl]") (al-Nisāʾ 4:59); in the story of al-Khiḍr and Mūsā: ﴿ذَٰلِكَ تَأْوِيلُ مَا لَمْ تَسْطِعْ عَلَيْهِ صَبْرًا﴾ ("That is the taʾwīl of what you were unable to bear with patience") (al-Kahf 18:82); and in the description of the Book: ﴿هَلْ يَنظُرُونَ إِلَّا تَأْوِيلَهُ ۚ يَوْمَ يَأْتِي تَأْوِيلُهُ﴾ ("Are they waiting for anything but its taʾwīl? On the day its taʾwīl comes") (al-Aʿrāf 7:53).

### The Linguistic Root: Returning to the Origin

The origin of *a-w-l* is return. One says *āla al-amru ilā kadhā*, meaning that the matter returned to such-and-such and became it. *Taʾwīl* is the verbal noun of the form *tafʿīl* from it, that is, bringing a thing back to what it returns to. *Al-maʾāl* is the outcome and the point of return. From this it becomes clear that taʾwīl in the original Arabic is not "turning a word away from its apparent sense," which is the meaning that became common among the later scholars of legal theory (*uṣūl*), but rather "that to which a matter returns and into which it turns."

Ibn Taymiyyah and others have drawn attention to the fact that the word taʾwīl, in usage, has three meanings: interpretation and exposition (*tafsīr* and *bayān*), which is the common sense in al-Ṭabarī's phrase "the discussion of the taʾwīl of His saying, Exalted is He"; the later technical sense of legal theory, which is to turn a word away from its preponderant possibility to a less likely one on the strength of evidence; and the original Qurʾānic meaning, which is the reality to which speech returns, that is, what a report or a command comes to in actual fact[^2].

### Taʾwīl in Sūrat Yūsuf: Between Interpretation and Realization

Yūsuf's usage joins the two meanings that the root can bear. His words "I shall inform you of its taʾwīl" (12:37) convey the exposition of what the food or the dream will come to, that is, a report about the future. His words "this is the taʾwīl of my vision of long ago" (12:100) convey the reality into which the dream has turned. In both cases, then, taʾwīl is what things come to; yet in the first it is a report before the event, and in the second it is witnessing after the event.

The verses show that God taught Yūsuf this as knowledge: ﴿وَكَذَٰلِكَ يَجْتَبِيكَ رَبُّكَ وَيُعَلِّمُكَ مِن تَأْوِيلِ الْأَحَادِيثِ﴾ ("And thus your Lord will choose you and teach you something of the taʾwīl of narratives") (Yūsuf 12:6); ﴿وَلِنُعَلِّمَهُ مِن تَأْوِيلِ الْأَحَادِيثِ﴾ ("and that We might teach him something of the taʾwīl of narratives") (Yūsuf 12:21); ﴿رَبِّ قَدْ آتَيْتَنِي مِنَ الْمُلْكِ وَعَلَّمْتَنِي مِن تَأْوِيلِ الْأَحَادِيثِ﴾ ("My Lord, You have given me of sovereignty and taught me something of the taʾwīl of narratives") (Yūsuf 12:101). Note that the phrase "taʾwīl of narratives" is repeated three times in connection with God's teaching, which indicates that this knowledge is a divine gift and not a purely human craft. Perhaps what is meant by *al-aḥādīth* here is reports and events and what they end in, so that the meaning is that Yūsuf was taught to read events and to grasp their outcomes.

### The Verse of Āl ʿImrān: Who Knows the Taʾwīl?

The term is tied to a question of the greatest importance for understanding the Qurʾān. In the verse of Āl ʿImrān: ﴿وَمَا يَعْلَمُ تَأْوِيلَهُ إِلَّا اللَّهُ ۗ وَالرَّاسِخُونَ فِي الْعِلْمِ يَقُولُونَ آمَنَّا بِهِ﴾ ("And none knows its taʾwīl except God; and those firmly grounded in knowledge say, 'We believe in it'") (Āl ʿImrān 3:7), the scholars differed over where to pause: is it at the Name of Majesty, so that knowledge of the taʾwīl belongs to God alone, or is "those firmly grounded" joined to it, so that they share in His knowledge of it? The disagreement is connected with the meaning of taʾwīl: if what is intended is the reality of what things come to, namely the essence of the attributes and the realities of the unseen, then it belongs to God; and if what is intended is interpretation and exposition, then the firmly grounded do know it. It has been reported from Ibn ʿAbbās that he said: «أنا ممن يعلم تأويله» ("I am among those who know its taʾwīl"), and it is related from him that the Prophet ﷺ prayed for him: «اللهم فقّهه في الدين وعلّمه التأويل» ("O God, give him understanding of the religion and teach him the taʾwīl")[^3].

This makes clear that taʾwīl in the Qurʾānic usage is not mere play with words, but an inquiry into what rulings and reports come to, and it is wider than linguistic interpretation.

### A Purposive Reading

The Qurʾānic sense of the term indicates that events and words have an "outcome" (*maʾāl*) that should be considered just as their surface is considered. This is consistent with the principle of taking outcomes into account (*iʿtibār al-maʾālāt*), on which the jurists built many rulings: the muftī looks at what the fatwā will lead to, not at the apparent text alone. Yūsuf's interpretation of the dream is a model: he did not stop at the image of the dream (cows and ears of grain), but read what it comes to, seven years of plenty and seven of drought, and then built upon it an economic plan: ﴿تَزْرَعُونَ سَبْعَ سِنِينَ دَأَبًا فَمَا حَصَدتُّمْ فَذَرُوهُ فِي سُنبُلِهِ﴾ ("You shall sow for seven years consecutively, and what you harvest leave in its ear") (Yūsuf 12:47). For him taʾwīl was not a static interpretation, but a reading of the outcome that issues in action.

Another of the purposes of Qurʾānic taʾwīl is to teach patience with what is not understood at the time. In the story of al-Khiḍr he said: ﴿سَأُنَبِّئُكَ بِتَأْوِيلِ مَا لَمْ تَسْتَطِع عَّلَيْهِ صَبْرًا﴾ ("I will inform you of the taʾwīl of what you were unable to bear with patience") (al-Kahf 18:78), and Mūsā was unable to be patient only because he did not know its outcome. So too in the story of Yūsuf: his being thrown into the well, his sale, and his imprisonment were all events whose outcome could not be seen, until it was disclosed at the end of the sūrah. Hence the words of Yaʿqūb: ﴿فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ﴾ ("So patience is most fitting") (Yūsuf 12:18).

### The Dream and Its Interpretation: What Is Sound and What Is Not

The Prophet ﷺ said: «الرؤيا الصالحة جزء من ستة وأربعين جزءًا من النبوة» ("The good dream is one part of forty-six parts of prophecy")[^4]. Hence the good dream has its standing, yet no rulings of the Sharīʿah are built upon it, and no established knowledge is abandoned on account of it. The Prophet ﷺ used to ask his Companions after the dawn prayer: «هل رأى أحدٌ منكم الليلة رؤيا؟» ("Did any of you see a dream last night?"), and whoever God willed would relate to him, and he would interpret it[^5].

The etiquette of dreams in Sūrat Yūsuf agrees with this: Yaʿqūb forbade his son to recount his dream to his brothers, and in the ḥadīth: «الرؤيا الحسنة من الله، فإذا رأى أحدكم ما يحب فلا يحدّث به إلا من يحب» ("The good dream is from God, so if one of you sees what he likes, let him tell it only to one whom he loves")[^6]. And in the same sūrah, when the king said: ﴿أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ ۖ وَمَا نَحْنُ بِتَأْوِيلِ الْأَحْلَامِ بِعَالِمِينَ﴾ ("A jumble of dreams, and we are not learned in the taʾwīl of dreams") (Yūsuf 12:44), they distinguished between a dream that carries meaning and a jumble that has no taʾwīl. This teaches that not every dream is open to interpretation, and that interpretation is a knowledge that needs its people, not a conjecture to be tossed out at random.

### The Qualities of the Interpreter in the Model of Yūsuf

When the cupbearer asked him to interpret the king's dream, he did not give a bare answer but added a plan to the interpretation, saying: ﴿ثُمَّ يَأْتِي مِن بَعْدِ ذَٰلِكَ عَامٌ فِيهِ يُغَاثُ النَّاسُ وَفِيهِ يَعْصِرُونَ﴾ ("Then after that will come a year in which the people will be given rain and in which they will press") (Yūsuf 12:49). He went beyond what he was asked about the dream by informing them of the year of rain, which the dream did not apparently contain. This shows that his knowledge was not mere decoding of symbols but knowledge given him by his Lord: ﴿ذَٰلِكُمَا مِمَّا عَلَّمَنِي رَبِّي﴾ ("That is from what my Lord has taught me") (Yūsuf 12:37). The true interpreter ascribes knowledge to its source and does not claim the unseen for himself.

### The Contemporary Practical Dimension

In our lives there are many events we judge by their surface: the loss of a job, a delayed marriage, illness, loss. The Qurʾānic method of taʾwīl teaches us not to close our judgment on an event before we have seen its outcome. This does not mean claiming knowledge of the unseen or asserting that every disliked thing is good; for in the verse: ﴿وَعَسَىٰ أَن تَكْرَهُوا شَيْئًا وَهُوَ خَيْرٌ لَّكُمْ﴾ ("Perhaps you dislike a thing and it is good for you") (al-Baqarah 2:216) there is hope, not certainty about every case. But it teaches forbearance, breadth of vision, and graceful patience.

And in dealing with texts: taʾwīl in its original sense teaches us not to stop at the surface of a verse without looking into its purposes and the outcomes into which it turns in reality, while guarding against taʾwīl by caprice, which turns texts away from their meaning without proof. The praiseworthy taʾwīl is that which rests on evidence, and the blameworthy is that which follows desire, as the Qurʾān blamed those who ﴿فَيَتَّبِعُونَ مَا تَشَابَهَ مِنْهُ ابْتِغَاءَ الْفِتْنَةِ وَابْتِغَاءَ تَأْوِيلِهِ﴾ ("follow what is ambiguous in it, seeking discord and seeking its taʾwīl") (Āl ʿImrān 3:7).

### Taʾwīl and the Higher Objectives of the Sharīʿah: Reading the Outcome in the Fatwā

The jurists recognized that considering outcomes is one of the foundations of *ijtihād*. Imām al-Shāṭibī made the consideration of the outcomes of actions a legislative aim, in the sense that the mujtahid does not judge an act by its surface alone but looks at what the application of the ruling will come to. Among the evidences for this in the Sunnah is the Prophet's ﷺ refraining from killing the hypocrites, which he explained with his words: «لا يتحدث الناس أن محمدًا يقتل أصحابه» ("People must not say that Muḥammad kills his companions")[^7]. He looked at what the matter would come to in the minds of the people and in their relation to his call.

The link between this kind of looking and Yūsuf's taʾwīl is there in the root of the meaning: both look beyond the surface in order to reach what the matter ends in. Yet Yūsuf was given knowledge of the outcome by his Lord, whereas the mujtahid derives the outcome from the indicators of reality and the rulings of the Sharīʿah, and does not claim knowledge of the unseen. Here disciplined *ijtihād* is distinguished from guesswork.

### Blameworthy Taʾwīl: When a Text Is Diverted Without Evidence

The Qurʾān blamed those who pursue the ambiguous out of a desire for discord and distortion of meaning, and this is the rejected taʾwīl: turning the text away from its meaning without proof, to please a whim or to support a school. The scholars have warned against this in the matter of the Names and Attributes and in the matter of rulings, and they laid down conditions for acceptable taʾwīl: that the word be capable of the meaning to which it is turned in the language of the Arabs, that sound evidence support it, and that it not contradict a decisive text. Taʾwīl under these conditions is knowledge and discipline, and without them it is toying with the Book.

### Conclusion

Taʾwīl in the Qurʾān is the answer to the question: to what does this come? It is a knowledge that God gave Yūsuf in dreams, that He gave His servants in jurisprudence and reflection, and whose full reality He kept with Himself. Whoever ponders Sūrat Yūsuf sees that all that seemed evil at its beginning was the taʾwīl of a good that God intended at its end, and that the Muslim is asked to act upon what he knows and to be patient with what has not yet shown its outcome.
