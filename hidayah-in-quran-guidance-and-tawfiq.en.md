*A study of the Qur'anic root h-d-y: guidance as a gentle showing of the way that prophets and teachers may offer, and guidance as divine enabling (tawfīq) that belongs to God alone. The article reads the verb's constructions and the two verses that seem to deny and affirm the Prophet's guiding, 28:56 and 42:52.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

A still night, a changing sky, and a man standing among a people who worship what they can see. The moon rises, and he says: This is my Lord. Then it sets, and the man reaches for no argument and no dispute; he lifts up a request instead: ﴿لَئِن لَّمْ يَهْدِنِي رَبِّي لَأَكُونَنَّ مِنَ الْقَوْمِ الضَّالِّينَ﴾ "If my Lord does not guide me, I shall surely be among the people who go astray" (6:77). Then the sun rises and sets (6:78), and he declares himself free of what they associate with God and turns his face to the One who originated the heavens and the earth (6:79). A few lines later he stands before his people, and the verb in his mouth has changed from the present tense hanging on a condition to the past tense, fulfilled: ﴿أَتُحَاجُّونِّي فِي اللَّهِ وَقَدْ هَدَانِ﴾ "Do you dispute with me about God, when He has guided me?" (6:80). Then the picture closes with a declaration that holds no hesitation: ﴿قُلْ إِنَّنِي هَدَانِي رَبِّي إِلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُّسْتَقِيمٍ﴾ "Say: Indeed, my Lord has guided me to a straight path" (6:161). Between "if my Lord does not guide me" and "my Lord has guided me" lies the whole journey of guidance: a servant who asks while he has not yet arrived, and a Lord who guides, so that he arrives. In this article we pause over what the Qur'an does with this root, and how it made a single particle after the verb change the meaning of the road.

### Three Hundred Sixteen Times in Twelve Forms

According to the Quranic Arabic Corpus dictionary (corpus.quran.com), the root h-d-y («هـ د ي») occurs in the Qur'an 316 times in twelve forms[^1]. The most frequent is the verb *hadā* (to guide), 144 times; then the noun *hudā* (guidance), 85 times; then *ihtadā* (to be guided), 40 times; then *muhtadūn* and *muhtadīn* (the rightly guided), 17 times; *ahdā*, *hād* (guide) and *hady* each come seven times, and the remaining forms are few. The detailed count matches the total. Some of these occurrences lie outside the meaning of pointing out the way: *al-hady* in the sense of sacrificial animals, as in (2:196) and (5:2), and the *hadiyya* (gift) that the Queen of Sheba sent in (27:35-36). The Corpus counts these places under the same root, but they fall outside the meaning of showing the way, which is our concern here.

This is what al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī pointed to: "Guidance (*hidāya*) is a showing of the way with gentleness, and from it come the *hadiyya* (gift) and the *hawādī* of wild animals, that is, the foremost of them, which guide the rest. What is a showing of the way is specified with *hadaytu*, and what is a giving, with *ahdaytu*"[^2]. So guidance is a showing and not a coercion, and it is "with gentleness"; and from the giving of a thing one does not say *hadaytu* but *ahdaytu*. Ibn Fāris' wording agrees with him: "*Hāʾ*, *dāl* and a weak letter: two roots, [one of them] going ahead in order to direct, the other a sending of kindness", after which he gives as example their saying: "*hadaytuhu al-ṭarīq hidāyatan*, that is, I went ahead of him in order to direct him, and everyone who goes ahead for this is a *hādin*", and from the same chapter "*al-hādiya*: the staff, because it goes ahead of the one who holds it as if directing him"[^3]. In the language, then, the guide is the one who walks in front of you and shows you the way, not the one who drags you by your clothes. The blind man's staff in his hand is a guide: it goes ahead of him and tells him what lies before him.

### One Particle That Changes the Road

The first thing that strikes one in the Qur'an's handling of this verb is that it makes it transitive in several ways, each with its own shade. I have collected examples of this from the Corpus lists; they are examples documented with their locations, not an exhaustive count. Some forms of the verb take two objects directly: ﴿اهْدِنَا الصِّرَاطَ الْمُسْتَقِيمَ﴾ "Guide us to the straight path" (1:6), ﴿وَهَدَيْنَاهُ النَّجْدَيْنِ﴾ "And We showed him the two ways" (90:10), ﴿عَسَىٰ رَبِّي أَن يَهْدِيَنِي سَوَاءَ السَّبِيلِ﴾ "Perhaps my Lord will guide me to the right way" (28:22). Others take *ilā* (to): ﴿وَإِنَّكَ لَتَهْدِي إِلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُّسْتَقِيمٍ﴾ "And indeed, you guide to a straight path" (42:52). Others take *lām* (for): ﴿الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ الَّذِي هَدَانَا لِهَٰذَا﴾ "Praise be to God, who has guided us to this" (7:43). And in others the particle *bāʾ* enters to mark the instrument: ﴿نُورًا نَّهْدِي بِهِ مَن نَّشَاءُ﴾ "a light by which We guide whom We will" (42:52).

Ibn Kathīr drew attention to the effect of this variety on the meaning: "Guidance here is direction and enabling (*tawfīq*), and *hidāya* may be made transitive by itself, as it is here… so it includes the meaning of: inspire us, or enable us, or provide us, or grant us". Then, of construction with *ilā*, he said: "that is in the sense of direction and showing the way", and of construction with *lām*, in the words of the people of Paradise: "that is, enable us to this and make us worthy of it"[^4]. The particle after the verb tilts the meaning between showing the way and enabling. Ibn Kathīr does not make this a rule without exception, and so I do not generalize it either; I only record the observation as he made it. As for al-Zamakhsharī, he says: "The origin of *hadā* is that it is made transitive by *lām* or by *ilā*"[^5]; that is, direct transitivity is, in his view, a branch of the origin and not the origin itself. The two agree that direct transitivity in *ihdinā* carries a meaning that mere information about the road does not convey, and they differ in wording and in division.

Yet there is in the Qur'an an unexpected use: ﴿فَاهْدُوهُمْ إِلَىٰ صِرَاطِ الْجَحِيمِ﴾ "and lead them to the path of Hellfire" (37:23). Here the verb is made transitive with *ilā*, but toward Hell! Al-Rāghib reported on this: "It is said that the expression is used here in the manner of mockery, for emphasis of the meaning"[^2]; the Qur'an takes up the very word of guidance in order to scorn it where it does not belong.

### *Ihdinā*: The Request of One Already on the Road

The first thing we ask for in every prayer cycle is guidance: ﴿اهْدِنَا الصِّرَاطَ الْمُسْتَقِيمَ﴾ "Guide us to the straight path" (1:6). And the old question arises: how can a believer ask for what he already has? Al-Zamakhsharī answers: "The meaning of asking for guidance while they are already guided is asking for an increase of guidance by the granting of gracious favours", and he cites the words of the Exalted: ﴿وَالَّذِينَ اهْتَدَوْا زَادَهُمْ هُدًى﴾ "And those who are guided, He increases them in guidance" (47:17), and he reports from ʿAlī and Ubayy, may God be pleased with them: "*Ihdinā* means keep us firm"[^5]. Al-Rāzī looks from another side: "Know that it is an expression for asking for guidance, and there are two ways to attain guidance: one is to seek knowledge through proof and argument, the other through the purifying of the inner self and spiritual discipline"[^6]. For al-Zamakhsharī it is the request of the guided for increase and firmness; for al-Rāzī it is a request that turns toward two paths: the knowledge of proof, and the refining of the heart. In both statements there is confirmation that guidance is not a station that the servant reaches and then rests, but a road on which God is asked for more.

This is what the Sunna says in the plainest words. In the hadith qudsī narrated by Abū Dharr: «يا عبادي كلكم ضالّ إلا من هديته فاستهدوني أهدكم» "O My servants, every one of you is astray except him whom I have guided, so ask Me for guidance and I shall guide you"[^7]. It is a command to seek guidance from one who is, in origin, "astray" unless he is guided. And among his ﷺ supplications was: «اللهم إني أسألك الهدى والتقى والعفاف والغنى» "O God, I ask You for guidance, piety, chastity and self-sufficiency"[^8]; he joined guidance with piety, as the Qur'an joined them in His words: ﴿وَالَّذِينَ اهْتَدَوْا زَادَهُمْ هُدًى وَآتَاهُمْ تَقْوَاهُمْ﴾ "And those who are guided, He increases them in guidance and gives them their piety" (47:17). And he taught ʿAlī, may God be pleased with him, a supplication that calls to mind the linguistic picture we saw in Ibn Fāris: «قل: اللهم اهدني وسدّدني، واذكر بالهدى هدايتك الطريق، والسداد سداد السهم» "Say: O God, guide me and keep me on target, and remember by 'guidance' your guidance along the road, and by 'rectitude' the straightness of the arrow"[^9]. Whoever wishes to remember the meaning of guidance, let him remember a traveller seeking out the road; and whoever wishes to remember the meaning of rectitude, let him remember an arrow seeking its mark.

### A Guidance Denied and a Guidance Affirmed

Here we reach the point of greatest precision in the Qur'an's usage. God says to His Prophet: ﴿إِنَّكَ لَا تَهْدِي مَنْ أَحْبَبْتَ وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ يَهْدِي مَن يَشَاءُ ۚ وَهُوَ أَعْلَمُ بِالْمُهْتَدِينَ﴾ "Indeed, you do not guide whom you like, but God guides whom He wills, and He is most knowing of the guided" (28:56). And in another verse He says to him: ﴿وَإِنَّكَ لَتَهْدِي إِلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُّسْتَقِيمٍ﴾ "And indeed, you guide to a straight path" (42:52). A denial and an affirmation of one act by one agent! How can that be?

The occasion of revelation of the first verse is in the Ṣaḥīḥ: when Abū Ṭālib was dying, the Prophet ﷺ said to him: «أي عم قل لا إله إلا الله، كلمة أحاجّ لك بها عند الله» "O uncle, say 'There is no god but God', a word with which I shall plead for you before God", and God revealed concerning Abū Ṭālib ﴿إِنَّكَ لَا تَهْدِي مَنْ أَحْبَبْتَ﴾ "Indeed, you do not guide whom you like"[^10]. Ibn Kathīr said: "that is, that is not in your hands; yours is only to convey, and God guides whom He wills". Al-Ṭabarī explained that God guides whom He wills "by enabling him to believe in Him and in His Messenger"[^11]. As for the second verse, al-Ṭabarī said of it: "that you guide to a straight path Our servants, by calling to God and by making clear to them", and from al-Suddī: "you call to a straight religion"; and Ibn Kathīr notes that the agent in "by which We guide" is God, and the Qur'an is His instrument[^12].

The answer is that the guidance denied is the guidance of enabling and acceptance, and the guidance affirmed is the guidance of calling and clear statement. Al-Rāghib stated it explicitly: "A human being cannot guide anyone except by calling and making the ways known, and not by the other kinds of guidance; to the first He pointed with His words: 'and indeed, you guide to a straight path'… and to the other kinds of guidance He pointed with the words of the Exalted: 'Indeed, you do not guide whom you like'". Al-Rāghib divides God's guidance into four aspects: first, the general guidance of reason, discernment and the necessary kinds of knowledge; second, the call made through the tongues of the prophets and the sending down of the Qur'an; third, "the enabling (*tawfīq*) which is particular to him who is guided"; and fourth, "the reward in the Hereafter and the entry into Paradise". He says: "These four kinds of guidance are in sequence", so that whoever has not received the first will not receive the second, and so on. Then he adds a rule that governs the whole chapter: "Every guidance that God, Mighty and Majestic, has mentioned as withheld from the wrongdoers and the disbelievers is the third guidance, and it is the enabling that is particular to the guided"[^13].

This rule explains the repetition of His words: ﴿إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الظَّالِمِينَ﴾ "Indeed, God does not guide the wrongdoing people" (28:50). The verb "guides" occurs in the negative with "the wrongdoing people", "the disbelievers" or "the transgressors" in many places; I have counted eighteen from the Corpus list[^14], and this is not the result of an independent survey. The meaning, according to al-Rāghib, is that enabling is withheld, not that showing the way is not offered. The door is open, the clear statement stands, and it is wrongdoing that closes the heart against it. The small disagreement in the verse concerns who is meant by "whom you like": al-Qurṭubī says that al-Zajjāj said "the Muslims are agreed that it was revealed concerning Abū Ṭālib", and al-Qurṭubī corrects the wording by saying, "most of the exegetes are agreed". He relates from Abū Rawq that the words "but God guides whom He wills" point to al-ʿAbbās, and from Mujāhid: "whoever it was decreed for him to be guided"[^15]. In all this there is confirmation that what is meant is the rule, not a particular person.

### "And Found You Lost, and Guided You"

In Sūrat al-Ḍuḥā, God reminds His Prophet of His favour: ﴿وَوَجَدَكَ ضَالًّا فَهَدَىٰ﴾ "And He found you lost and guided you" (93:7). The verb *fahadā* comes with no stated complement, so it is not said to what. The exegetes differed over the meaning of *ḍāll* (lost). For al-Ṭabarī: "and He found you on other than what you are on today", and from al-Suddī: "he was on the way of his people for forty years", and it was said: "and He found you among a people gone astray and guided you". For al-Qurṭubī: "that is, unaware of what was intended for you of the matter of prophethood", and he said: "and *ḍalāl* here means heedlessness". And Ibn Kathīr related from al-Baghawī a third view: that he lost his way in the mountain paths of Mecca as a child and then returned[^16].

These are three readings: a going astray in religion, according to those who take it as his state before revelation; a heedlessness of what awaited him; and a going astray in the physical road. What appears to me, by way of reflection and not certainty, is that the omission after *fahadā* leaves the guidance unrestricted, so that the favour extends to all that God gave His Prophet of direction and enabling, and is not narrowed by a specification the text does not state. Whichever view one takes, the act is God's act, and the Prophet ﷺ was the object of care before he was the bearer of the call.

### "Then He Is Guided": What Comes After Repentance and Deed

The subtlest of these places comes in His words: ﴿وَإِنِّي لَغَفَّارٌ لِّمَن تَابَ وَآمَنَ وَعَمِلَ صَالِحًا ثُمَّ اهْتَدَىٰ﴾ "And indeed, I am Most Forgiving to whoever repents, believes and does righteousness, and then is guided" (20:82). What is there after repentance, faith and righteous deeds, that it should be said "then is guided"? Al-Ṭabarī says: "then he held fast to that, stayed upright and lost nothing of it". In Ibn Kathīr there are several views: from Ibn ʿAbbās, "that is, then he did not doubt"; from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, "he stayed upright on the Sunna and the community"; from Qatāda, "he held to Islam until he died"; from Sufyān al-Thawrī, "he knew that this has a reward"; and then he noted that *thumma* (then) here is "to order one report after another"[^17].

This is a difference of variety more than of contradiction. Being guided after the deed is, for al-Ṭabarī and Qatāda, steadfastness and continuance; for Ibn ʿAbbās, certainty without doubt; for al-Thawrī, a knowledge of the recompense that moves the heart. If we take Ibn Kathīr's view that *thumma* orders the report and not the time, the verse presents guidance as the summit of a chain and not a link that comes late after it. What appears to me is that the Qur'an distributes the two verbs between the two agents: the servant is the one who *yahtadī* (is guided) for himself, as in ﴿مَّنِ اهْتَدَىٰ فَإِنَّمَا يَهْتَدِي لِنَفْسِهِ﴾ "Whoever is guided is guided only for his own soul" (17:15), while *hadā* (guided) is, for the most part, God's act. That is what we saw in the arc of Abraham: "He has guided me" comes from God, and its fruit, "being guided", is his.

### Before Revelation: A Guidance That Covers All Things

The Qur'an does not confine guidance to revelation. In Moses' reply to Pharaoh: ﴿رَبُّنَا الَّذِي أَعْطَىٰ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ خَلْقَهُ ثُمَّ هَدَىٰ﴾ "Our Lord is He who gave everything its form, then guided it" (20:50). Here too the exegetes differed: al-Ṭabarī related from Ibn ʿAbbās that He guided it "to its mating, its food, its drink, its dwelling and its birthplace"; Ibn Kathīr reported from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr: "He gave every creature what suits it of its creation"; and, from some, that the meaning is like His words "He ordained and guided", that is, He ordained an ordinance and guided creatures to it[^18]. The first confines it to mating and livelihood, the second extends it to the whole of the ordaining. And al-Rāghib made this verse a witness for the first, general guidance. On this view, guidance precedes revelation and begins with creation itself, even if no book had been sent down.

This meets the scene of Moses himself. When he set out toward Midian in fear, with no guide, he said: ﴿عَسَىٰ رَبِّي أَن يَهْدِيَنِي سَوَاءَ السَّبِيلِ﴾ "Perhaps my Lord will guide me to the right way" (28:22), asking for guidance on the physical road. Then, years later, he saw a fire and said to his family: ﴿لَّعَلِّي آتِيكُم مِّنْهَا بِقَبَسٍ أَوْ أَجِدُ عَلَى النَّارِ هُدًى﴾ "Perhaps I may bring you a brand from it, or find at the fire some guidance" (20:10), meaning guidance for the road, and then he was called to revelation. The one root gathers, in the life of one prophet, the road between two lands and the road between the servant and his Lord.

### The Picture of Guidance When It Is Complete

If we gather what has gone before, a coherent picture appears. Guidance in the Qur'an is a gentle showing of the way, which goes ahead of you and does not drag you. Its highest rank belongs to God alone, and it is the enabling that no prophet possesses; the human being has of it only what he can hold: the call and the clear statement. Beyond that, guidance is a sequence of ranks that follow one another: reason and innate nature, then revelation that calls, then enabling that accepts, then a Paradise that admits. And of the last, the people of Paradise say: ﴿الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ الَّذِي هَدَانَا لِهَٰذَا وَمَا كُنَّا لِنَهْتَدِيَ لَوْلَا أَنْ هَدَانَا اللَّهُ﴾ "Praise be to God, who has guided us to this; we would never have been guided had God not guided us" (7:43); so the road ends where it began: an acknowledgment that guidance is from God.

And of the particular quality of the Book He says: ﴿ذَٰلِكَ الْكِتَابُ لَا رَيْبَ ۛ فِيهِ ۛ هُدًى لِّلْمُتَّقِينَ﴾ "That is the Book, about which there is no doubt, a guidance for the God-fearing" (2:2). Al-Ṭabarī asks: had the Book been a light for those who are not God-fearing, God would not have singled out the God-fearing; then he answers: "the believer in it is guided, and the disbeliever in it is one against whom the proof stands". Al-Zamakhsharī, for his part, explains the specification by God's knowledge of the two groups: one group whose remaining in error is known, and one group whose end is guidance, "so it is not a guidance for the group that remains in error"[^19]. The first makes the Book a clear statement for all, from which the God-fearing benefit; the second looks to God's knowledge of the outcome of the two groups. And both leave the door open and the responsibility on the human being.

Guidance also admits increase, as we saw in ﴿وَالَّذِينَ اهْتَدَوْا زَادَهُمْ هُدًى﴾ "And those who are guided, He increases them in guidance" (47:17); there is no guided person among the people who has reached the end and so is free of the need to ask. This may explain why the first supplication in the Muṣḥaf is a supplication for guidance, repeated in every prayer cycle, and why Moses, peace be upon him, said as he set out toward Midian "perhaps my Lord will guide me", and was then guided at the fire to something greater than the road; for every guidance that is attained opens a door to asking for the guidance that follows it.

### Guidance Today: Between the One Who Convinces and the One Who Steadies

This difference between showing the way and enabling carries a direct educational effect. The preacher, the teacher, the father and the imam in the mosque all possess what the Prophet ﷺ possessed: the call and the clear statement; and none of them possesses what was denied him of enabling. Whoever thinks that the success of his call is measured by everyone he speaks with being guided has forgotten that love does not create guidance, and it was in the love of the Prophet ﷺ for his uncle that it was revealed: ﴿إِنَّكَ لَا تَهْدِي مَنْ أَحْبَبْتَ﴾ "Indeed, you do not guide whom you like"; the one who loves is not blamed for his love, nor is love expected to create guidance. And, on the other hand, whoever thinks that "God guides whom He wills" excuses him from mastering clear statement, from fair presentation and from long patience has turned the verse upside down, because the other verse affirms to him the call: ﴿وَإِنَّكَ لَتَهْدِي﴾ "And indeed, you guide". The duty is to join the two ends: to offer people a showing of the way with gentleness, as al-Rāghib and Ibn Fāris said, without harshness and without dragging by the clothes, and then to raise our hands asking that God grant enabling.

And for the educator who is raising children in an environment where competing signposts abound, there is the case of Abraham to consider; what appears to me is that the truth was not dictated to him, but was set within a series of questions and the setting of the heavenly bodies. The showing of the road may pass through the question before the answer, and through astonishment before surrender. Let him teach the young to say *ihdinā* out of knowledge and not out of habit, and to join the asking for guidance with the asking for piety, as the hadith and the verse joined them. A person's road may grow long while he is upon the straight path, and he then needs the "then is guided" that means steadfastness to the appointed end, as Qatāda said in what Ibn Kathīr relates. And the young person today, who stands at a crossroads of ideas and platforms as Moses stood on the road to Midian, needs to say: "Perhaps my Lord will guide me to the right way", and not to be content with himself as his own guide.

### When the Servant Says at the End of the Road What He Said at Its Beginning

The road begins with a request and ends with praise. At the start of the scene the servant asks: ﴿اهْدِنَا الصِّرَاطَ الْمُسْتَقِيمَ﴾ "Guide us to the straight path", and at its end, having entered Paradise and seen the rivers flowing beneath him, he says: ﴿الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ الَّذِي هَدَانَا لِهَٰذَا وَمَا كُنَّا لِنَهْتَدِيَ لَوْلَا أَنْ هَدَانَا اللَّهُ﴾ "Praise be to God, who has guided us to this; we would never have been guided had God not guided us" (7:43). And between the two lies a whole life of bewilderment, labour and steadfastness. O God, as You have guided us to Islam, keep us firm upon it, increase us in guidance and grant us our piety, and do not make us among those whose guidance we loved and yet were not enabled to bring about, nor among those for whom God decreed guidance and who left it. O God, guide us and keep us on target, make for us of guidance a light by which we walk, guide us to You along the right way, and admit us to Paradise by Your grace, so that we say there: Praise be to God, who has guided us to this.
