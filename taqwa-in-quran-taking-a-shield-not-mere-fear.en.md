*A study of a linguistic root whose origin is the shield and tangible protection, and of how that very meaning was carried over to describe the heart's protection from the wrath of God. By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA).*

In Sūrat al-Naḥl the Qur'an recalls a simple, tangible blessing: ﴿وَجَعَلَ لَكُمْ سَرَابِيلَ تَقِيكُمُ الْحَرَّ وَسَرَابِيلَ تَقِيكُمْ بَأْسَكُمْ﴾ (al-Naḥl 16:81) — "and He made for you garments that protect you from the heat, and garments that protect you in your [mutual] violence." Shirts that guard the body from the blaze of the sun; coats of mail that guard it from the thrust of the spear. The verb describing both forms of protection is one: "taqī" — from the very root from which, a few verses later in the same sūrah, the greatest word in the Qur'anic moral vocabulary will be derived: *taqwā*. What, then, is the significance of this choice? Why did the Qur'an describe the highest degree of closeness to God with the same word it used for a shirt that protects from heat and a coat of mail that protects from the stab?

### The Occurrences and Their Count

The root «و ق ي» occurs in the Noble Qur'an two hundred and fifty-eight times, distributed across eight forms.[^1] The first form is the base verb «وقى», meaning "to shield and preserve" — direct, tangible protection — which occurs sixteen times (like the verse with which this article opened), together with the active participle «واقٍ», meaning "protector and defender," which occurs three times, always in a context of negation: ﴿مَا لَهُمْ مِنَ اللَّهِ مِنْ وَاقٍ﴾ (al-Raʿd 13:34, 13:37; Ghāfir 40:21) — "they have no protector against God." This first field, nineteen occurrences, is the direct, tangible meaning of the root: the protection of a body from external harm.

The second field, the focus of this article, comprises the forms in which that same meaning is carried over to moral protection: the augmented verb «اتّقى» (of the pattern *ifticāl*), which occurs one hundred and sixty-six times and is the most frequent; the active participle «مُتَّقِين», forty-nine times; the verbal noun «تقوى», seventeen times; the elative «أتقى», twice; and the adjective «تقيّ», which occurs in places including 19:13, 19:18 and 19:63. The total of this moral field comes to about two hundred and thirty-eight occurrences, and it is the direct lexical witness to the concept of *taqwā* in its Qur'anic sense. One occurrence remains outside both fields: «تُقاة» in the words of the Exalted, ﴿إِلَّا أَنْ تَتَّقُوا مِنْهُمْ تُقَاةً﴾ (Āl ʿImrān 3:28) — "except when you guard yourselves against them with caution." This is a juristic term specific to dissimulation toward an adversary under compulsion (*taqiyyah*), which has its own independent chapter in jurisprudence and does not fall within the scope of this article.

### The Linguistic Root: From the Shield to the Heart

Ibn Fāris states in *Maqāyīs al-Lugha* that the root wāw-qāf-yā' «يدلّ على دفع شيءٍ عن شيء بشيء» — "indicates warding off one thing from another by means of something."[^2] This definition applies literally to the bee-sūrah's shirt and its coat of mail, and to *taqwā* in its technical sense, equally. There is always a party that is feared, a party that is to be protected, and a barrier placed between them. When the Qur'an shifts the verb from its simple form «وقى» (something protects something) to its augmented form «اتّقى» (of the pattern *ifticāl*, which usually signals that the agent performs the action for himself), the meaning moves from a protection granted by an external party (the shirt, the coat of mail) to a protection a person fashions for himself with his own hands: he takes his obedience and compliance as a barrier between himself and what is feared, just as he takes his shirt as a barrier between himself and the heat.

### Taqwā Is Not Fear, Nor Even Khashyah

To clarify what distinguishes *taqwā* from its sister words for trembling dread, it helps to recall the two points of comparison chosen by the Arabs themselves. Ibn Fāris says under the entry «خوف» that the letters khā'-wāw-fā' are «أصلٌ واحدٌ يدلّ على الذُّعر والفزع» — "a single root indicating terror and panic."[^أ] Fear is thus an emotion that befalls the heart suddenly before what is frightening — a state that happens to a person, not an act he performs. As for *khashyah*, al-Rāghib al-Aṣfahānī, in *al-Mufradāt fī Gharīb al-Qur'ān*, distinguishes it from fear as «خوفٌ يشوبه تعظيم» — "fear mixed with veneration" — and as often arising «عن علمٍ بما يُخشى منه» — "from knowledge of what is feared."[^ب] That is why the Qur'an reserved it for the scholars alone: ﴿إِنَّمَا يَخْشَى اللَّهَ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ الْعُلَمَاءُ﴾ (Fāṭir 35:28) — "Only those of His servants who have knowledge fear God." *Khashyah* is higher than fear because it is joined to knowledge, yet it remains — like all its sisters — a state that dwells in the heart. *Taqwā* is a further step: it is what the heart does after it has felt *khashyah*, when it translates that awe into a practical, adopted protection. He who feels awe but does not take protection has been content with an inward emotion; he who takes protection has turned his awe into a shield worn and a conduct practiced. This is the subtle difference that is overlooked when *taqwā* is translated as mere "fear of God": *taqwā* is the fruit of *khashyah*, not its synonym.

### The Central Structure: Taking a Protection, Not Merely Fearing

This fine morphological distinction resolves a common problem in rendering the word "taqwā" as "fear of God," a deficient translation that overlooks the positive action latent in the word. Fear is a feeling that overtakes the heart; guarding oneself (*ittiqāʾ*) is an act performed by the limbs: to advance one foot and hold back the other. So ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, may God be pleased with him, once asked Ubayy ibn Kaʿb about the meaning of *taqwā*, and Ubayy answered him with a question: «أما سلكتَ طريقًا ذا شوك؟» — "Have you never walked a thorny path?" ʿUmar said: «بلى» — "Yes." He asked: «فما عملت؟» — "What did you do?" ʿUmar said: «شمّرتُ واجتهدتُ» — "I hitched up my garment and exerted myself." Ubayy said: «فذلك التقوى» — "That is *taqwā*."[^3] In this illustration handed down from the Companions, *taqwā* is not the paralysis of fear but the alertness of a wayfarer on a path strewn with things that would hurt him, scrutinizing the place of every step. This is precisely the meaning of "taking a protection": a deliberate, recurring act, not a passing state of emotion.

### A Model That Confirms the Metaphor: The Garment of Taqwā

What further establishes this central metaphor is that the Qur'an itself revives it explicitly in another passage, in a sūrah other than the one with which this article opened; in Sūrat al-Aʿrāf, after God mentions the blessing of clothing that covers the nakedness and serves as adornment, He concludes with the words: ﴿وَلِبَاسُ التَّقْوَىٰ ذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ﴾ (al-Aʿrāf 7:26) — "and the garment of piety, that is best." The Qur'an is not content with using the same linguistic root to describe both tangible and moral protection; it goes further and describes *taqwā* explicitly as a "garment" — that is, a coat of mail and a shirt that is worn, exactly like the garments of al-Naḥl. In this way the Qur'an closes the circle it opened in Sūrat al-Naḥl: the shirt protects the body, *taqwā* is a shirt that protects the heart, and the second is better than the first because its protection is more lasting and more comprehensive.

### Another Model: The Place of Taqwā in the Human Being

If *taqwā* is a garment that is worn, where is its place on the body? The Qur'an answers in the context of the rites of Ḥajj: ﴿ذَٰلِكَ وَمَنْ يُعَظِّمْ شَعَائِرَ اللَّهِ فَإِنَّهَا مِنْ تَقْوَى الْقُلُوبِ﴾ (al-Ḥajj 22:32) — "That [is so]. And whoever honors the symbols of God — indeed, it is from the *taqwā* of hearts." *Taqwā* is not an outward form by which a person's appearance is judged; it is a state that dwells first in the heart, and then its effects appear in the honoring of the rites and in all other deeds. This agrees with what is related in the Prophetic ḥadīth, when the Prophet ﷺ pointed to his chest three times, saying: «التقوى هاهنا، التقوى هاهنا، التقوى هاهنا» — "*Taqwā* is here, *taqwā* is here, *taqwā* is here."[^4] The original seat of *taqwā*, then, is the heart, not the limbs alone, even though it is the limbs that display its effect.

### When the Watcher Is Absent: The Report of the Milk-Seller and Her Daughter

If the seat of *taqwā* is the heart and not the limbs, then the truest test of it is solitude, when every human watcher is absent. Among the best-known accounts transmitted in the books of history and literature to depict this meaning — though it has not been established by a connected chain of transmission — is the report that ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, may God be pleased with him, was walking about by night checking on the condition of his subjects, when he heard a woman telling her daughter to mix the milk with water to increase it for sale. The girl replied: «أوَ ما علمتِ بنهي أمير المؤمنين عن الغشّ؟» — "Do you not know of the Commander of the Faithful's prohibition of fraud?" The mother said: «وأين عمر منّا الآن؟» — "And where is ʿUmar from us now?" The girl answered: «إن كان عمر لا يرانا، فإنّ ربّ عمر يرانا» — "If ʿUmar does not see us, the Lord of ʿUmar sees us." This answer, whatever the scholars' differences over the soundness of its chain, has become proverbial because the essence of *taqwā* is not that a person be upright before people, but that presence and absence be the same to him, since the true Watcher never disappears. It is exactly what Ibn al-Qayyim formulated as a comprehensive definition of *taqwā*, transmitted from Ṭalq ibn Ḥabīb, when he said that it is: «أن تعمل بطاعة الله على نورٍ من الله، ترجو ثواب الله، وأن تترك معصية الله على نورٍ من الله، تخشى عقاب الله» — "that you act in obedience to God, upon a light from God, hoping for God's reward, and that you abandon disobedience to God, upon a light from God, fearing God's punishment."[^ج] *Taqwā*, then, is not hostage to the presence of people, but to a light of knowledge of God that accompanies a person in his solitude as it accompanies him in public.

### A Third Model: The Best Provision

In the context of the journey of Ḥajj, when some pilgrims set out without material provisions, relying on others, the verse was revealed: ﴿وَتَزَوَّدُوا فَإِنَّ خَيْرَ الزَّادِ التَّقْوَىٰ﴾ (al-Baqarah 2:197) — "And take provisions, for indeed, the best provision is *taqwā*." *Taqwā* here is a provision taken for a journey, just as food and water are taken as provisions for the journey of this world; except that this provision protects its bearer on a far longer journey than the pilgrimage: the journey of a whole lifetime to the Hereafter. In this is a further extension of the central metaphor: as tangible protection (the shirt, the provision) is taken in preparation for an anticipated hardship, *taqwā* is taken in preparation for what is certainly to come.

### A Fourth Model: The Sole Measure of Distinction

This metaphor reaches its deepest social effect in a single verse that demolishes every criterion of superiority known to the Jāhiliyyah — lineage, wealth, and color: ﴿يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ إِنَّا خَلَقْنَاكُمْ مِنْ ذَكَرٍ وَأُنْثَىٰ وَجَعَلْنَاكُمْ شُعُوبًا وَقَبَائِلَ لِتَعَارَفُوا إِنَّ أَكْرَمَكُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ أَتْقَاكُمْ﴾ (al-Ḥujurāt 49:13) — "O mankind, indeed We have created you from male and female and made you peoples and tribes that you may know one another. Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of God is the most righteous of you." After establishing that humanity has a single origin, the Qur'an makes the measure of honor in God's sight single as well: the one who best takes protection, not the one who inherited lineage or wealth. This transforms *taqwā* from an individual virtue into a social measure that cuts across all inherited distinctions.

### A Fifth Model: The Condition of Acceptance

*Taqwā* attains yet another rank when the Qur'an makes it a condition for the acceptance of a deed, not merely for its performance. In the story of the two sons of Ādam, when each of them offered a sacrifice, God accepted it from one and not the other, and the explanation came plainly on the tongue of the one whose offering was accepted: ﴿إِنَّمَا يَتَقَبَّلُ اللَّهُ مِنَ الْمُتَّقِينَ﴾ (al-Māʾidah 5:27) — "God only accepts from the righteous." The difference between the two offerings was not in their size or kind, but in the state of the heart of the one who presented them: did he present it having taken protection as the foundation of his deed, or did he present it devoid of that foundation? This places *taqwā* at a point prior to the deed itself: it is not a fruit gathered after the deed, but a condition on which the heart is looked at before the deed itself is looked at.

### The Prophetic Witness

Al-Tirmidhī narrated, from the ḥadīth of Abū Dharr and Muʿādh ibn Jabal, may God be pleased with them both, that the Prophet ﷺ said: «اتّقِ اللهَ حيثما كنتَ، وأتبِعِ السيّئةَ الحسنةَ تَمْحُها، وخالِقِ الناسَ بخُلُقٍ حَسَنٍ» — "Be mindful of God wherever you are; follow a bad deed with a good one and it will wipe it out; and treat people with good character."[^5] Three successive counsels that sum up practical *taqwā*: a vigilance that is not interrupted by a change of place; an immediate repair of error through the effacing good deed; and good character in dealings that translates the inward protection into a visible effect among people. *Taqwā* in this ḥadīth is not a withdrawal from people, but a bearing that accompanies a person everywhere, mends his mistakes and improves his character.

### A Maqāṣidī Reading

A number of scholars observe that the Qur'an linked *taqwā* to most of the legal obligations in general, not to a particular act of worship. Fasting was prescribed «لعلّكم تتّقون» — "so that you may attain *taqwā*" (al-Baqarah 2:183); in retribution (*qiṣāṣ*) there is life, «لعلّكم تتّقون» (al-Baqarah 2:179); in Ḥajj there is the honoring of the rites, «من تقوى القلوب» — "from the *taqwā* of hearts" (al-Ḥajj 22:32); and even the Qur'an itself was sent down as «هدًى للمتّقين» — "guidance for the righteous" (al-Baqarah 2:2). They see in this repetition an indication that *taqwā* is not the goal of the individual acts of worship each taken separately, but the comprehensive goal by which the worth of every act of worship is measured: if an act of worship does not yield a true protection for the heart, its performer has done it without the spirit for which it was prescribed. This also explains why the Qur'an chose to describe the Book itself as «هدًى للمتّقين»: *taqwā* is not only the fruit of guidance, but a condition of receptivity to it; whoever has taken protection from the outset grows in guidance through the Qur'an.

What deepens this reading is what al-Ḥāfiẓ Ibn Rajab al-Ḥanbalī mentioned in his commentary on the ḥadīth «اتّقِ الله حيثما كنت» in his book *Jāmiʿ al-ʿUlūm wa-l-Ḥikam*. He observed that the Prophet's ﷺ third counsel in the ḥadīth — «وخالِق الناس بخُلُقٍ حَسَنٍ» — is one of the traits of *taqwā* itself and not a counsel separate from it, and that «الجمعَ بين القيام بحقوق الله وحقوق عباده عزيزٌ جدًّا، لا يقوى عليه إلا الكُمَّل من الأنبياء والصدّيقين» — "combining the fulfillment of God's rights with the rights of His servants is exceedingly rare; none are able to do so but the most perfect of the prophets and the truthful."[^د] *Taqwā*, then, is not completed by protecting the relationship with God alone; it extends to include protecting the relationship with people as well. One who is mindful of God in his solitude and then behaves badly toward people has attained only half of *taqwā*.

### Three Links, Not One

If one wishes to see *taqwā* embodied, not merely as an abstract meaning, the Qur'an itself draws for the righteous a composite portrait of converging qualities, not a single quality. At the opening of Sūrat al-Baqarah, after describing the Book as "guidance for the righteous," it hastens to define them: ﴿الَّذِينَ يُؤْمِنُونَ بِالْغَيْبِ وَيُقِيمُونَ الصَّلَاةَ وَمِمَّا رَزَقْنَاهُمْ يُنْفِقُونَ﴾ (al-Baqarah 2:3) — "Who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and spend out of what We have provided for them." The first qualities mentioned are an unseen that is believed in, a prayer that is established, and wealth that is spent — a relationship with the unknown, a relationship with the Creator, and a relationship with the creature, all three together, none of them in isolation from its sisters. Then Sūrat Āl ʿImrān adds detail to the picture: ﴿الَّذِينَ يُنْفِقُونَ فِي السَّرَّاءِ وَالضَّرَّاءِ وَالْكَاظِمِينَ الْغَيْظَ وَالْعَافِينَ عَنِ النَّاسِ﴾ (Āl ʿImrān 3:134) — "Who spend [in the cause of God] during ease and hardship, who restrain anger, and who pardon the people." It adds to the picture a spending that does not stop when circumstances change, anger that is restrained and not unleashed, and forgiveness that is granted rather than vengeance taken. The following verse concludes the picture with its hardest quality: those who, when "فَعَلُوا فَاحِشَةً أَوْ ظَلَمُوا أَنْفُسَهُمْ ذَكَرُوا اللَّهَ فَاسْتَغْفَرُوا لِذُنُوبِهِمْ" (Āl ʿImrān 3:135) — "they commit an immorality or wrong themselves, remember God and seek forgiveness for their sins." The righteous person is not immune from slipping; he is one who, when he slips, hastens to repair the protection that has cracked rather than surrender to its collapse. These qualities together — belief, worship, spending, restraint of anger, pardon, and return to God — are the practical face of everything this article has discussed by way of the metaphor of protection: as a coat of mail is not complete with a single plate, *taqwā* is not complete with a single trait.

### The Contemporary Practical Dimension

Many confuse *taqwā* with rigidity or withdrawal, picturing the righteous person as one burdened with prohibitions, shrinking back from life. But the central metaphor this article has uncovered proposes a different picture: *taqwā* is not a wall behind which a person is confined, but a shirt he puts on in order to move with greater freedom, not less. Just as the garments against heat and violence did not prevent a person from working but enabled him to work in conditions that would have prevented him without them, so *taqwā* enables its bearer to face life's temptations and dangers with steadiness, not contraction. And whoever takes *taqwā* as a true protection, not a slogan repeated, finds what the Qur'an promised: ﴿وَمَنْ يَتَّقِ اللَّهَ يَجْعَلْ لَهُ مَخْرَجًا﴾ (al-Ṭalāq 65:2) — "And whoever is mindful of God, He will make for him a way out" — a way out of the narrowness of life, and not merely deliverance from the punishment of the Hereafter alone.

### Conclusion

From a shirt that protects from heat, to a coat of mail that protects from the stab, to a garment of the heart that protects from the wrath of God, the Qur'an uses the same linguistic root with marvelous constancy to say one unchanging thing: that *taqwā* is not a fear that immobilizes its bearer, nor merely a *khashyah* that dwells in his heart, but a protection a person takes up by his own action and will, a fruit of his knowledge of his Lord, accompanying him in his solitude as it accompanies him in public, completed by converging qualities and not by a single trait — just as he takes up his shirt on a day of heat.

And God knows best; He is the One who grants success, and there is no lord but Him.
