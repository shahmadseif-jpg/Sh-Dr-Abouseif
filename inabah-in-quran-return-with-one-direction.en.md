*A study of a root that appears in the Qur'an only in the causative form, of its single direction, and of its relation to repentance. Inābah is a return of the heart and the servant to God alone, which draws close to tawbah and adds to it sincerity, turning towards Him, and obedience.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

Two litigants come in to David, peace be upon him, not by the door but ﴿تَسَوَّرُوا الْمِحْرَابَ﴾ "they climbed over the wall of the sanctuary" (38:21). He judges between them, and as he judges a single thought crosses his heart, which the Qur'an relates in one line: ﴿وَظَنَّ دَاوُودُ أَنَّمَا فَتَنَّاهُ فَاسْتَغْفَرَ رَبَّهُ وَخَرَّ رَاكِعًا وَأَنَابَ﴾ "And David realized that We had only put him to the test, so he asked his Lord for forgiveness, fell down bowing, and turned back in inābah" (38:24). Three movements follow one upon another: a plea for forgiveness on the tongue, a falling to the ground in the body, and then a third act that is neither seen nor heard, which is "anāba". The Qur'an did not say "tāba" (he repented); it brought another word, one whose direction the Qur'an never mentions except as "to God". This word, which came in the station of prophethood, in the station of trial, and in the station of supplication in affliction, is the subject of this study.

### Eighteen Occurrences and a Single Form

The Quranic Arabic Corpus says that the root n-w-b («ن و ب») occurs eighteen times in the Qur'an, in two derived forms of one pattern, the causative (ifʿāl): eleven verbs from "anāba" and seven active participles, "munīb"[^1]. This means that the root never comes in the Qur'an in its simple form: no "nāba", no "nāʾiba", no "nawb". It comes always in the form of inābah.

The verbs are these: ﴿وَإِلَيْهِ أُنِيبُ﴾ "and to Him I turn" in the words of Shuʿayb (11:88); ﴿مَنْ أَنَابَ﴾ "whoever turns back" (13:27); ﴿مَنْ أَنَابَ إِلَيَّ﴾ "whoever turns back to Me" (31:15); David (38:24); Solomon (38:34); ﴿وَأَنَابُوا إِلَى اللَّهِ﴾ "and turn back to God" (39:17); the command ﴿وَأَنِيبُوا إِلَىٰ رَبِّكُمْ﴾ "And turn back to your Lord" (39:54); ﴿مَن يُنِيبُ﴾ "whoever turns back" in (40:13) and in (42:13); ﴿وَإِلَيْهِ أُنِيبُ﴾ "and to Him I turn" (42:10); and ﴿وَإِلَيْكَ أَنَبْنَا﴾ "and to You we turn" (60:4). As for "munīb", it came as a description of Abraham (11:75); of the believers, ﴿مُنِيبِينَ إِلَيْهِ﴾ "turning back to Him" (30:31), and of people when harm touches them (30:33); of the human being when harm touches him, ﴿دَعَا رَبَّهُ مُنِيبًا إِلَيْهِ﴾ "he calls upon his Lord, turning back to Him" (39:8); in ﴿لِكُلِّ عَبْدٍ مُّنِيبٍ﴾ "for every servant who turns back" (50:8) and (34:9); and in ﴿بِقَلْبٍ مُّنِيبٍ﴾ "with a heart turned back to Him" (50:33). The reader will notice that David and Solomon are mentioned by the verb and not by the adjective, and that the participle is joined to a particular prophet only in the case of Abraham.

### A Direction Written into the Sentence

The first thing that strikes one on reading all the occurrences is that every verse in which the one turned to is stated makes inābah transitive with a single particle, "ilā" (to), and there are nine such occurrences: "and to Him I turn" in (11:88) and (42:10); "whoever turns back to Me" (31:15); "turning back to Him" (30:31, 30:33); "turning back to Him" (39:8); "turn back to God" (39:17); "turn back to your Lord" (39:54); "to You we turn" (60:4). Among the eighteen occurrences there is no transitivity with any other particle, and no inābah to anyone other than God. As for His words "He guides to Himself whoever turns back", the pronoun there belongs to guidance, and so I have not counted it among these nine. And the occurrences that are silent about the direction ("forbearing, tender-hearted, turning back", "with a heart turned back") have a context that sends the reader back to God alone.

Ibn ʿĀshūr explained the use of "ilā" with the verb by the sense of return in inābah and tawbah[^2]. So inābah is not merely flight from something; it is a journey towards something. And whoever reads the root in its linguistic origin finds the same meaning.

### A Bee Returning to Its Hive

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī says under the entry "n-w-b": "Nawb is the return of a thing time after time. One says nāba nawban and nawbatan, and the bees are called nawb because of their return to their dwelling places"; then he says: "and inābah to God, exalted is He, is returning to Him through repentance and sincerity in deeds"[^3]. Ibn Fāris makes the origin a single word "that indicates habituation to a place and return to it", and he mentions that "the nawb are the bees", presenting it with the phrase "it is said", and then says: "and it has been said that it is the plural of nāʾib"[^4]. And in Lisān al-ʿArab: "nāba fulān ilā Allāh taʿālā, and anāba ilayhi inābatan, so he is munīb: he turned towards Him, repented, and returned to obedience", and it mentions that the bees were called nawb "because they graze and return to their place"[^5].

This is a beautiful image, but it is well to fix it properly: for the bee, in the words of the linguists, is a witness to the origin and not its source, and with Ibn Fāris it is a statement not decisively affirmed. The origin is the repeated return to a customary place. The bee does not go to the flower to stay there; it goes in order to come back laden to its home, and it has no direction but that home. This is a meaning close to what we find in the verses: the munīb goes out into the world and returns, errs and returns, is tried and returns, and all of it towards one place.

### Between Inābah and Tawbah: Where Do They Part?

Here the question arises from which no one who studies the word is spared: is inābah not simply tawbah? Ibn Manẓūr explained inābah as "returning to God through repentance", and, of the verse of al-Zumar, "that is, repent to Him and return"[^5]. Ibn ʿĀshūr said of the same verse: "and inābah is repentance"[^2]. In al-Ṭabarī: "and turn towards your Lord, O people, in repentance, and return to Him in obedience to Him", and he transmitted from Ibn Zayd: "Inābah is return to obedience and abandoning what they were on. Do you not see that He says: 'turning back to Him, and be mindful of Him'?"[^6].

But those who said this did not stop at repentance, for in their phrases an addition kept recurring. Al-Rāghib says: "through repentance and sincerity in deeds". And al-Qurṭubī on the same verse: "that is, return to Him in obedience... and inābah is returning to God with sincerity"[^7]. So inābah, for them, is repentance and something with it: sincerity, turning towards Him, obedience. And this is what makes it, in my reading of these texts, wider than the moment of confessing a sin and nearer to a state on which the heart settles. We have already set out in an earlier article in this series that repentance is twofold: a repentance from God towards the servant and a repentance from the servant towards God. Inābah completes this picture from the servant's side: how does the return take place? With a face deliberately set towards God and a sincere heart, not a passing return.

This difference reached the point of dispute over the very same verse. Al-Zamakhsharī, as al-Rāzī relates from the *Kashshāf*, holds that God mentioned inābah after forgiveness in His words ﴿قُلْ يَا عِبَادِيَ الَّذِينَ أَسْرَفُوا عَلَىٰ أَنفُسِهِمْ لَا تَقْنَطُوا مِن رَّحْمَةِ اللَّهِ﴾ "Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of God's mercy" and then ﴿وَأَنِيبُوا﴾ "and turn back", "lest anyone who hopes should hope for it without repentance, and to indicate that it is a necessary condition of it". But al-Rāzī does not accept this and says: "I say, this statement is very weak", and he builds his reply on the difference between pardon at the outset and pardon after punishment[^8]. Whichever is preferable, the verse commands inābah in a particular circumstance: ﴿مِن قَبْلِ أَن يَأْتِيَكُمُ الْعَذَابُ ثُمَّ لَا تُنصَرُونَ﴾ "before the punishment comes upon you, and then you will not be helped" (39:54), so it is a command to hasten.

### Abraham: A Return That Shows a Tender Heart

Inābah finds no clearer human face than the face of Abraham, peace be upon him. After the fear had left him and the glad tidings had come to him, he ﴿يُجَادِلُنَا فِي قَوْمِ لُوطٍ﴾ "pleaded with Us for the people of Lot" (11:74); then comes the description: ﴿إِنَّ إِبْرَاهِيمَ لَحَلِيمٌ أَوَّاهٌ مُّنِيبٌ﴾ "Indeed, Abraham was forbearing, tender-hearted, turning back to God" (11:75). Three attributes follow one another: forbearance, tender-heartedness, and inābah. In interpreting it the expressions differ in a way that enriches the meaning. Al-Ṭabarī: "slow to anger, humble before his Lord, submissive to Him, yielding to His command, munīb, returning often to obedience to Him", and from Mujāhid: "*awwāh munīb*," he said: the devout, the one who returns often". And al-Qurṭubī: "the munīb is the one who returns... and Abraham, may God bless him and grant him peace, was returning to God in all his affairs". And al-Zamakhsharī: "munīb: repentant, returning to God with what He loves and is pleased with. And these attributes indicate tenderness of heart, compassion and mercy". As for al-Rāzī, he ties it to the context of the plea itself: "because the one in whom this great compassion for others appears, he turns back, repents and returns to God to remove that punishment from them"[^9].

The sayings run in gradation: a return to obedience with al-Ṭabarī and Mujāhid, a generalization to all matters with al-Qurṭubī, tenderness of heart with al-Zamakhsharī, and compassion for people that drives one towards God with al-Rāzī. The finest thing in this variety is that it shows that inābah is not confined to the one who has sinned; for Abraham in this verse no sin is mentioned, only a merciful heart that, whenever something distresses it, takes refuge in its Lord. The Qur'an completes his portrait on his tongue and the tongues of those with him: ﴿رَّبَّنَا عَلَيْكَ تَوَكَّلْنَا وَإِلَيْكَ أَنَبْنَا وَإِلَيْكَ الْمَصِيرُ﴾ "Our Lord, in You we have put our trust, and to You we turn back, and to You is the final return" (60:4), which is among the verses al-Rāghib cites under the entry of the root. Trust and inābah are paired in three places: (11:88), (42:10) and (60:4). Trust is entrusting the matter to God, and inābah is the return of the heart to Him, and both are two faces of one heart.

### David and Solomon: When Trial Is a Door

In the story of David and Solomon inābah is joined to trial. In both it is said: ﴿فَتَنَّاهُ﴾ "We had put him to the test" and then ﴿وَأَنَابَ﴾ "and he turned back", and: ﴿وَلَقَدْ فَتَنَّا سُلَيْمَانَ وَأَلْقَيْنَا عَلَىٰ كُرْسِيِّهِ جَسَدًا ثُمَّ أَنَابَ﴾ "And We certainly tested Solomon and placed on his throne a body; then he turned back" (38:34). Al-Zamakhsharī interpreted David's inābah as "he returned to God, exalted is He, with repentance and disavowal", and al-Qurṭubī said: "that is, he repented of his error and returned to God", and al-Ṭabarī said: "and returned to the good pleasure of his Lord, and repented of his error". Ibn ʿĀshūr held that David knew that God had made the event a trial for him, so his inābah came after that[^10]. So inābah here is an answer to a trial: David's act is called nothing more than that he sought forgiveness, prostrated and turned back, and we ought not to add to the Qur'an what is not in it.

As for Solomon, the exegetes differed about the "body" that was placed on his throne: in al-Ṭabarī, from Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḥasan and Mujāhid, that it was a devil, and al-Rāzī went to the view that it was an illness. And according to the difference the meaning of "anāba" changes: a return to God through repentance, as al-Qurṭubī said ("then he turned back, that is, he returned to God and repented"), or a return to health, as al-Rāzī transmitted in one of the sayings. I build nothing on these details, for the editor of al-Qurṭubī's commentary pointed out in his note that some of what has been reported on this is "decidedly not sound"[^11]. What remains firm in the verse is that Solomon was tried and then turned back, and that the Qur'an made inābah follow the trial. In this is a lesson for us: whoever is tried and then returns suffers no diminution of his standing, for the Qur'an did not count the trial a disgrace but mentioned what came after it.

### The Fruit of Guidance and Its Path

The two verses that tie inābah to guidance come and make the matter a wonder: ﴿وَيَهْدِي إِلَيْهِ مَنْ أَنَابَ﴾ "and guides to Himself whoever turns back" (13:27), and ﴿اللَّهُ يَجْتَبِي إِلَيْهِ مَن يَشَاءُ وَيَهْدِي إِلَيْهِ مَن يُنِيبُ﴾ "God chooses for Himself whomever He wills and guides to Himself whoever turns back" (42:13). So there is first a choosing by God, then guidance for the one who turns back. Al-Zamakhsharī said on the verse of al-Raʿd: "anāba: he turned towards the truth, and in reality he entered into the turn of goodness (nawbat al-khayr)", and al-Ṭabarī transmitted from Qatāda: "that is, whoever repented and turned towards". And on the verse of al-Shūrā Ibn Kathīr said: "that is, He is the one who decrees guidance for whoever deserves it, and writes misguidance against whoever preferred it to the path of right guidance"[^12].

Al-Zamakhsharī's phrase "entered into the turn of goodness" is a gentle key that returns the word to "nawbah", which is the single occasion within an alternation. And I read in it, by way of ijtihād and not transmission, that the munīb is one who has entered the turn of goodness and walks in it. Perhaps in the structure of the root there is something that helps towards this: it occurs in the Qur'an only in the causative pattern, with no simple counterpart, as though the direction were intended in it from the very construction. This is a deduction I have not found anyone stating explicitly, so let whoever wishes count it a linguistic nicety and not a ruling.

In the two verses is a subtle meaning: guidance is suspended on inābah, yet inābah itself stands only by God's favour. For God "guides to Himself whoever turns back", and it is He, glory be to Him, who "chooses for Himself whomever He wills". So the servant neither feels secure in his inābah nor despairs of it.

### A Turning Heart and a Reminder for a Turning Servant

Inābah in the Qur'an has a place in the heart: ﴿مَّنْ خَشِيَ الرَّحْمَٰنَ بِالْغَيْبِ وَجَاءَ بِقَلْبٍ مُّنِيبٍ﴾ "Whoever feared the Most Merciful in the unseen and came with a heart turned back to Him" (50:33). Al-Ṭabarī interpreted it with his words: "and came to God with a heart repentant of its sins, returning from what God dislikes to what pleases Him", and from Qatāda: "that is, turning to its Lord, inclined towards Him". Al-Qurṭubī said: "inclined to obedience. And it was said: sincere". And al-Zamakhsharī said: "He described the heart by inābah, which is return to God, exalted is He; because what counts is what of it is firmly fixed in the heart"[^13]. What counts, then, is not merely the posture of the body when it bows, but the heart that returns.

Then the Qur'an links inābah to remembrance: ﴿وَمَا يَتَذَكَّرُ إِلَّا مَن يُنِيبُ﴾ "and none takes heed except the one who turns back" (40:13), and makes the heaven and the earth ﴿تَبْصِرَةً وَذِكْرَىٰ لِكُلِّ عَبْدٍ مُّنِيبٍ﴾ "an insight and a reminder for every servant who turns back" (50:8). The signs are before everyone, but the one who benefits from them is the one whose heart is directed. It is as if inābah were the direction without which the signs pass over the heart as light passes over a wall from which nothing is reflected.

### Inābah in Affliction: When People Turn and Then Look Away

Among the most truthful things in the Qur'an about the soul is that it mentions inābah in states for which its people are not praised: ﴿وَإِذَا مَسَّ النَّاسَ ضُرٌّ دَعَوْا رَبَّهُم مُّنِيبِينَ إِلَيْهِ ثُمَّ إِذَا أَذَاقَهُم مِّنْهُ رَحْمَةً إِذَا فَرِيقٌ مِّنْهُم بِرَبِّهِمْ يُشْرِكُونَ﴾ "And when harm touches people, they call upon their Lord, turning back to Him; then when He lets them taste mercy from Him, at once a party of them associate others with their Lord" (30:33). And in al-Zumar: ﴿وَإِذَا مَسَّ الْإِنسَانَ ضُرٌّ دَعَا رَبَّهُ مُنِيبًا إِلَيْهِ ثُمَّ إِذَا خَوَّلَهُ نِعْمَةً مِّنْهُ نَسِيَ مَا كَانَ يَدْعُو إِلَيْهِ مِن قَبْلُ﴾ "And when harm touches the human being, he calls upon his Lord, turning back to Him; then when He bestows on him a favour from Himself, he forgets what he used to call upon Him for before" (39:8). Affliction directs the heart to God in a way that costs its owner no striving, and then the blessing comes and he turns away. And I read in these two places that the Qur'an wants to distinguish between an inābah that hardship imposes and an inābah that the free person chooses in ease; this distinction I have not found anyone stating in this form, so it is a personal understanding that I offer, and I do not make the text carry more than it bears. It is enough for us from the two verses what is in them in plain words: that harm may direct to God someone who soon forgets.

### In the Depth of the Night: "And to You I Turn"

In the Sunna we find inābah on the tongue of the Prophet ﷺ in the place where the servant is alone with his Lord. Al-Bukhārī narrated that when he rose in the night to pray tahajjud he would say: «اللَّهُمَّ لَكَ أَسْلَمْتُ، وَبِكَ آمَنْتُ وَعَلَيْكَ تَوَكَّلْتُ، وَإِلَيْكَ أَنَبْتُ، وَبِكَ خَاصَمْتُ، وَإِلَيْكَ حَاكَمْتُ، فَاغْفِرْ لِي مَا قَدَّمْتُ وَمَا أَخَّرْتُ...» "O God, to You I have submitted, in You I have believed, in You I have put my trust, to You I have turned back, by You I have contended, and to You I have brought my judgment; so forgive me what I have sent ahead and what I have left behind..."[^14]. Note the order: submission, then belief, then trust, then inābah, and these are the very terms with which inābah is paired in the Qur'an: submission in (39:54), and trust in (11:88), (42:10) and (60:4). Thus the verses of the Qur'an and the supplication of the Prophet ﷺ coincide: inābah does not stand alone but strung on one thread with submission and trust. And the supplication, in ending with a request for forgiveness, reminds us that returning does not remove the need for pardon.

### How Do We Nurture Inābah?

If we want to translate all this into an educational programme, let us remember that inābah is a direction before it is a feeling. The first thing the verses teach us is to give our hearts a known direction. The teacher, the imam and the guardian need to teach those they raise that sin is not the end of the road, that return is possible, and that God commanded it before the punishment comes. And it is better that we teach the young inābah in ease and not in hardship alone: that we accustom them to take refuge in God in joy as in distress, so that when good comes to them they say: "from God", and when a trial strikes them they say: "to You".

Among everyday applications is that a Muslim learn the swift turning back. If he swerves a step, he does not say "it is too late", but returns as the bee returns, time after time, to the same place. The Qur'an sets no number of times for the munīb; it sets for him a direction. And how many in our families and mosques have sinned and thought the door shut, and it is enough for them to hear that the Qur'an named the one who returns "munīb", the very word with which it described Abraham. And let us keep a portion of the night in which we are alone with God, even for minutes, saying in it what the Prophet ﷺ said, and then go on to our day with a heart that returned before it went out.

### What the Single Word Carries

So inābah in the Qur'an, after all this, is the return of a heart and a servant to God alone; its root occurs eighteen times in a single form, and in every place where the direction is stated it is made transitive with the particle "ilā". It draws so near to repentance that the great exegetes explain it by it, and it adds to it sincerity, turning towards Him, and obedience. It came in the description of the compassionate Abraham, in the trial of David and Solomon, in a heart that fears in the unseen, and in people when harm touches them. It is a fruit of guidance and a cause of it, a companion of trust and submission, and its place is the heart before the body.

O God, make us among Your servants who turn back to You, and return us to You whenever we have drifted far, a goodly return; choose us for Yourself and guide us to Yourself, and do not let harm alone be our road to Your door. ﴿رَّبَّنَا عَلَيْكَ تَوَكَّلْنَا وَإِلَيْكَ أَنَبْنَا وَإِلَيْكَ الْمَصِيرُ﴾ "Our Lord, in You we have put our trust, and to You we turn back, and to You is the final return" (60:4).
