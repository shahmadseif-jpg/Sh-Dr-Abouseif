*Qurʾanic shūrā is not a ritual of seeking leave for a decision already made, but the genuine drawing-out of what lies hidden in another's mind, as honey is drawn from the comb. It is commanded even toward those who have just erred, and it reaches from the household to the succession of rulers.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

After the ordeal of Uḥud, when an error on the part of some of the Muslims brought about a painful reverse, the Qurʾan did not direct the Prophet ﷺ to tighten his grip or to dispense with those who had erred. It directed him to something that seems at first startling: ﴿فَاعْفُ عَنْهُمْ وَاسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُمْ وَشَاوِرْهُمْ فِي الْأَمْرِ﴾ — “So pardon them, and ask forgiveness for them, and consult them in the matter” (3:159). Pardon, then forgiveness, then consultation. The revelation was not content to forgive the fault; it commanded the Prophet, divinely protected and in receipt of revelation, to include in the next decision the very Companions who had just made a mistake. This command, addressed to one who is outwardly in no need of anyone else's opinion, is the door by which this article enters the Qurʾanic concept of *shūrā*.

### The Word and Its Count

The root sh-w-r occurs in the Qurʾan only four times, in four different forms: the verb *shāwir* (3:159); the verb *ashārat*, meaning “she gestured with her hand” (19:29, of Mary pointing to her child); the noun *shūrā* (42:38); and the verbal noun *tashāwur* (2:233). This striking rarity (four places only, in a Book of more than six thousand verses) does not reflect any marginality in the concept but rather the distinctiveness of its position. An entire sura of the Qurʾan bears its name (*al-Shūrā*), and the one place where the noun itself appears is set within a context enumerating the essential traits of the believers, and not in some passing margin of legislation. The numerical fewness here signals no lack of weight; it signals that when the concept is mentioned, it is mentioned at pivotal points, precisely weighed.

### The Linguistic Root: From Extracting Honey to Extracting Opinion

Ibn Fāris notes in *Maqāyīs al-Lughah* that the root goes back to two neighboring origins: one is the disclosing, showing, and displaying of a thing; the other is the taking and extracting of a thing. From this second sense the Arabs said *shurtu al-ʿasal*, “I extracted the honey,” when one draws it from its place in the hive. Ibn Manẓūr records in *Lisān al-ʿArab* that some philologists hold that *shāwartu fulānan fī amrī* (“I consulted so-and-so about my affair”) derives from the extraction of honey itself, as though the one who seeks counsel draws the opinion out of the breast of the one he consults, as honey is drawn from its comb[^1]. This linguistic origin reveals a subtle dimension in the meaning of consultation. It is not a mere question put in expectation of a superficial answer, but an active process of extraction, in which the consulter makes a real effort to draw out what lies latent in the minds of those he consults, just as the honey-gatherer labors to take it from its place.

### The Central Structure: Extraction, Not Asking Leave

This distinction, between active extraction and formal asking of leave, is the heart of what sets Qurʾanic consultation apart from many forms of merely formal “consultation” familiar today, where an opinion is summoned only to endorse a decision already made. When the Qurʾan commands the Prophet ﷺ to consult those who had just erred at Uḥud, it does not ask him to seek their leave as a formality, but to draw out their real opinions and take them into account in the next decision. The proof that this extraction is serious, and not a formal asking of leave, is that the Qurʾan follows the command to consult directly with: ﴿فَإِذَا عَزَمْتَ فَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ﴾ — “Then, when you have resolved, put your trust in God.” Resolve, that is, comes after consultation and not before it, as if the opinions drawn out truly enter into the forming of the decision, and are not heard only to be set aside.

### Why “Shāwirhum” and Not “Istashirhum”?

The precision of the Qurʾanic choice is deepened by the fact that the verb in the verse of Āl ʿImrān is *shāwirhum*, on the pattern *fāʿala*, and not *istashirhum*, on the pattern *istafʿala*, though both patterns are used in Arabic from the same root. In Arabic morphology the pattern *fāʿala* generally implies participation between two parties, as in *qātala* (fighting from both sides) and *ḥāwara* (mutual dialogue), whereas *istafʿala* implies a request from one party alone, as in *istakhraja* (to seek an extraction) or *istafhama* (to seek understanding). When the Qurʾan chose *shāwirhum* over *istashirhum*, it was not a fleeting stylistic preference but a morphological indication that the consultation intended is a truly participatory act between two parties who together contribute to the shaping of an opinion, and not a one-way request that issues from a commander and awaits an answer from his subordinate. This confirms the distinction this article has drawn between genuine extraction and formal asking of leave. Asking leave is a relationship in which one party seeks permission from the holder of the decision; consultation on the pattern *fāʿala* is a relationship between two parties who exchange opinion before the decision is settled.

### A Model Revealing the Standing of Shūrā: Between Prayer and Almsgiving

Among the most eloquent signs of the standing of consultation is that its sole appearance under its explicit name comes set between two of the pillars of Islam in the portrait of the believers' traits: ﴿وَالَّذِينَ اسْتَجَابُوا لِرَبِّهِمْ وَأَقَامُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَأَمْرُهُمْ شُورَىٰ بَيْنَهُمْ وَمِمَّا رَزَقْنَاهُمْ يُنْفِقُونَ﴾ — “And those who respond to their Lord, and establish prayer, and whose affair is [conducted by] consultation among them, and who spend from what We have provided them” (42:38). The verse mentions the establishment of prayer, then consultation, then spending, in a single continuous context, as though consultation were not a marginal administrative arrangement but a social act of worship, equal in standing among the believers' traits to the establishment of prayer and spending in the cause of God. The word “their affair” is general, covering everything that concerns them, and not a particular political matter alone.

### Another Model: Consultation in the Most Intimate Details of the Family

The breadth of consultation's scope in the Qurʾan is shown by the fact that the fourth occurrence of the root appears in a context far removed from governance and politics: the decision to wean an infant, between its two parents. The Qurʾan says: ﴿فَإِنْ أَرَادَا فِصَالًا عَنْ تَرَاضٍ مِنْهُمَا وَتَشَاوُرٍ فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِمَا﴾ — “If the two desire weaning, by mutual consent and consultation, there is no blame on them” (2:233). Consultation is here required not in the decision of a state or a battle, but in a small family decision that concerns the parents alone. This widens the circle of consultation from the sphere of public governance to every shared decision, however small, so that the principle of drawing out and consulting the other party becomes a foundation of every relationship built on shared decision-making, and not an exception reserved for the ruler.

### A Model from the Same Root: The Gesture That Discloses Meaning

Even the remaining occurrence of the root, when Mary (peace be upon her) gestures to her child instead of speaking (19:29), carries an echo of the same central meaning. A gesture is a “disclosing and showing” of the intended meaning without direct speech, which is the first strand of the root's origin that Ibn Fāris mentioned. It is as though the Qurʾan, by using one root in two utterly different contexts (consultation and gesture), keeps one root-meaning present: the bringing out into the open of what lies hidden, whether an opinion in the breast or a meaning in the hand.

### “Advise Me, O People”

This central structure is embodied in practice in a famous episode of the Prophet's biography, which preceded the verse of Āl ʿImrān itself by years. When news of the Quraysh caravan reached the Prophet ﷺ shortly before Badr, and the matter turned into a military confrontation to which the Anṣār had not pledged themselves at the Pledge of al-ʿAqabah, the Prophet ﷺ stood and openly sought the opinion of his Companions, saying: «أشيروا عليّ أيها الناس» — “Advise me, O people.” He was not satisfied with the answer of the Emigrants alone, until Saʿd ibn Muʿādh rose, speaking for the Anṣār, to affirm their readiness to go with him wherever he turned. This episode embodies precisely the distinction this article has drawn. The Prophet ﷺ was not formally seeking his Companions' leave for a decision already taken; he was drawing out their real opinion on a fateful matter, since it was the answer of the Anṣār in particular on which he based his next plan.

### When ʿUmar Entrusted His Gravest Decision to Six

If the consultation at Badr embodied the principle at a moment of urgent military decision, its farthest historical application, in the gravest decision any community faces, namely who is to succeed the ruler, came in the last moments of the life of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb (may God be pleased with him). When ʿUmar was stabbed, he did not name one particular man to follow him, as Abū Bakr had done before him, but made the matter a consultation among six of the senior Companions: ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān, ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, Ṭalḥah, al-Zubayr, Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ, and ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf. He justified his choice of them in these words: «ما أجد أحقّ بهذا الأمر من هؤلاء الرهط الذين توفّي رسول الله ﷺ وهو عنهم راضٍ» — “I find none more deserving of this matter than these men with whom the Messenger of God ﷺ was pleased when he died”[^2]. ʿUmar, who held absolute authority at that moment, did not act alone on his own opinion in the gravest decision the community would face after him. He entrusted the decision itself to an organized process of consultation among a trusted elite, and they met and consulted until they pledged allegiance to ʿUthmān. This application carries the principle of consultation beyond the seeking of a passing opinion to the founding of an institutional mechanism for the transfer of authority itself, the farthest reach the principle can attain in practice.

### The Prophetic Witness

Al-Tirmidhī related, on the authority of Abū Hurayrah (may God be pleased with him), and graded it ḥasan, with corroborating witnesses from other chains that strengthen it, the saying: «ما رأيتُ أحدًا أكثر مشورةً لأصحابه من رسول الله ﷺ» — “I have never seen anyone who consulted his Companions more than the Messenger of God ﷺ”[^3]. The bearer of revelation, whose rulings are not weighed against anyone's opinion for their soundness, was the most consultative of men toward those around him. This accords fully with what the verse of Āl ʿImrān indicated: consultation is no procedure to which one resorts for lack of knowledge, but a prophetic custom adorning the one who knows best of all people the affairs of his religion and his world, that he might be an example to those after him in this trait.

### A Maqāṣidī Reading

A number of scholars connect the placing of consultation between the establishment of prayer and spending in the verse of *al-Shūrā* with a deeper purpose: that Islam does not separate individual worship (prayer), social worship (consultation), and financial worship (spending), but makes of them one integrated system, which is completed in faith only through their coming together. They see in this an answer to those who suppose that religion is a purely private matter between servant and Lord, with no bearing on how collective decisions are made. The verse expressly counts consultation among the fruits of responding to God, and not among purely worldly arrangements apart from faith.

Others observe that when the Qurʾan speaks of consultation it does not detail its mechanisms, the number of those consulted, or the method of weighing differing opinions, but contents itself with stating the general principle: that the affair of the believers is conducted by consultation among them. They see in this deliberate brevity a space left to every age and place to develop the mechanisms suited to applying the fixed principle, so that the form of consultation does not harden into a single historical image, while its essence, the drawing out of real opinion before resolving upon a decision, remains fixed and unchanging.

This also connects with what was established in another article of this series on wisdom, where it became clear that the wise person does not rest content with his own opinion alone, however great his certainty. Consultation, from this angle, is a practical application of wisdom in the arena of collective decision. The true consulter restrains himself from rushing ahead in ignorance of others' opinions, just as the wise man restrains himself from rushing ahead in ignorance of the limits of his own knowledge.

As to the juristic standing of consultation, Shaykh al-Islām Ibn Taymiyyah says in *al-Siyāsah al-Sharʿiyyah*: “The one in authority cannot dispense with consultation”[^4]. He establishes that the ruler's need to draw out opinion is not a luxury that may be dropped as his power grows, but a necessity attending every office, however great. A number of commentators on his words have explained that, in his view, the obligation of consultation grows most pressing in matters on which no decisive text has come and which are drawn in different directions by the various considerations of interest-based legal reasoning. This is precisely what explains why Abū Bakr appointed ʿUmar by a single designation, without wide consultation, when the benefit of doing so was manifest, whereas ʿUmar himself resorted to the council of six when the possible options multiplied and there was no decisive tipping consideration.

### The Contemporary Application

Many in positions of responsibility today, in the family, the workplace, and institutions, confuse “informing” others of a decision already made with genuinely consulting them about it. The linguistic origin laid out here offers a decisive test to tell them apart: real consultation draws out something that was not apparent, and so changes something in the final decision, if only in part; formal asking of leave changes nothing, because the decision is complete before the question is put. The scene of the Prophet ﷺ consulting those who had just erred at Uḥud stands as a model that challenges a common human instinct: that a leader should set aside the opinion of one who has recently erred and not go on including him. True consultation does not exclude the one who erred; it draws out his opinion as well, for a mistake in a past decision does not necessarily cancel the worth of his opinion in the next.

### Conclusion

From a divine command to consult those who had just erred, to a verse that sets consultation between prayer and spending, to a small family decision in which mutual consultation is required, to ʿUmar entrusting the gravest decision facing a community to six who deliberate together, the Qurʾan and history trace for *shūrā* one unchanging meaning: a genuine, participatory drawing-out of what lies latent in another's opinion, and not a merely formal asking of leave that precedes a decision already made.

And with God is all success, and God knows best.
