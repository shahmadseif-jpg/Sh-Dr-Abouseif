*A study of a verb that occurs five times in the Qur'an with a single meaning, to prefer one thing over another, where only the doer and the object preferred change. The article follows the five verses and asks of each: whom does God prefer, whom do people prefer, and over what?*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

A man came to the Prophet ﷺ hungry, and the Prophet sent word to his wives asking for food for him, but they all replied: We have nothing but water. Then he ﷺ said: «من يضمّ، أو يضيف هذا؟» "Who will take in, or host, this man?" A man of the Anṣār said: I will. He took the guest home and made ready what he had, then put out the lamp, and he and his household made the guest believe that they were eating, though they were not eating: «فباتا طاويين» "and the two of them spent the night hungry." Then the Prophet ﷺ said: «ضحك الله الليلة ـ أو عجب ـ من فعالكما» "God laughed tonight, or marvelled, at what you two did." And God revealed: ﴿وَيُؤْثِرُونَ عَلَىٰ أَنفُسِهِمْ وَلَوْ كَانَ بِهِمْ خَصَاصَةٌ ۚ وَمَن يُوقَ شُحَّ نَفْسِهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ﴾ "And they prefer others over themselves, even if they are in need. And whoever is protected from the stinginess of his own soul, it is they who are the successful" (59:9)[^1]. A house in darkness, empty stomachs, a guest who has eaten his fill, and a heaven that marvels. It is as though the Qur'an would tell us that the most beautiful part of this scene is what the guest never saw.

People have differed over the name of the host. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr name him Abū Ṭalḥa, while al-Qurṭubī reports from al-Mahdawī and al-Naḥḥās, on the authority of Abū Hurayra, that the verse was revealed concerning Thābit ibn Qays and another man of the Anṣār called Abū al-Mutawakkil[^2]. What stands in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, however, is simply "a man of the Anṣār," without a name. We do not claim to settle on any one name; the man's name is unknown in the report, but his deed is known in this world and the next.

### Five Times, No More

On our tongues, the word "īthār" is the name of a virtue, but in the Qur'an it is only ever a verb. The root a-th-r occurs twenty-one times in the Qur'an, in four forms according to the Qur'anic Arabic Corpus: one passive form of the basic verb, the quadriliteral verb "āthara" five times, the noun "athar/āthār" fourteen times, and the verbal noun "athāra" once[^3]. Of all these, only the five belong to the subject of īthār. As for "yūthar" in the verse ﴿إِنْ هَٰذَا إِلَّا سِحْرٌ يُؤْثَرُ﴾ "This is nothing but magic that is handed down" (74:24), it means "is related and transmitted"; "āthār" are the traces that remain; and "athāra" in ﴿أَوْ أَثَارَةٍ مِّنْ عِلْمٍ﴾ "or any remnant of knowledge" (46:4) is a remnant of it. These are meanings that branch out from a single root, and what concerns us here is one branch among them: the verb "āthara" alone.

These five are: ﴿لَقَدْ آثَرَكَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا﴾ "God has indeed preferred you over us" (12:91); ﴿لَن نُّؤْثِرَكَ عَلَىٰ مَا جَاءَنَا﴾ "We will never prefer you over what has come to us" (20:72); ﴿وَيُؤْثِرُونَ عَلَىٰ أَنفُسِهِمْ﴾ "And they prefer others over themselves" (59:9); ﴿وَآثَرَ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا﴾ "and preferred the life of this world" (79:38); and ﴿بَلْ تُؤْثِرُونَ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا﴾ "Rather, you prefer the life of this world" (87:16). Two things stand out in this inventory: the noun "īthār" never occurs as a verbal noun anywhere in the Qur'an, and the verb is made transitive with "ʿalā" ("over") in three places (Yūsuf, Ṭā Hā and al-Ḥashr) and with a direct object in two (al-Nāziʿāt and al-Aʿlā). In the Qur'an, then, īthār is a movement that takes place, not a quality that is claimed.

### What the Scholars of the Language Say

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī says under the entry "athar": "Al-maʾāthir are what is related of a person's noble deeds, and 'athar' is borrowed for excellence, and 'īthār' for bestowing preference, whence: I preferred him (āthartuhu)." He then cites as evidence the verse ﴿وَيُؤْثِرُونَ عَلَىٰ أَنفُسِهِمْ﴾, together with ﴿لَقَدْ آثَرَكَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا﴾ and ﴿بَلْ تُؤْثِرُونَ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا﴾[^4]. In a single statement he brought together three verses that differ in doer and object, and in praise and blame, and counted them all under "bestowing preference," that is, putting forward and preferring. In the same entry al-Rāghib mentions the hadith that says "After me there will be athara," and explains it as some of you taking exclusive possession to the exclusion of others, then adds: "And istithār is to be alone in having a thing to the exclusion of others." In our estimation, on this explanation istithār is the counterpart of īthār from the opposite side: the one reserves the thing for himself, the other puts it forward for someone else.

Ibn Fāris, for his part, traces the root back to three origins: "putting a thing first, mentioning a thing, and the trace of a thing that remains," and he reports from Ibn al-Aʿrābī: "I preferred him with the thing (āthartuhu bi-l-shayʾ), as an act of īthār, and the noun is al-athara and al-ithra"[^5]. It appears to us that the three origins correspond to the Qur'anic branches: putting a thing first is "āthara"; mentioning it and relating it is "yūthar" in a magic that is handed down; and its lasting trace is athar, āthār and athāra. This is a connection we propose; we did not read it stated in Ibn Fāris, so we offer it as our reading and not as his statement.

### One Meaning, Different Doers

Here is the key to the subject. When we look into the five verses, we do not find five meanings but a single meaning: choosing a thing and putting it before another. What changes in each place is the doer and the object preferred. In Sūrat Yūsuf the doer is God and the one preferred is Joseph. In Ṭā Hā the doers are the magicians and the one rejected is Pharaoh. In al-Ḥashr the doers are the Anṣār and the ones preferred (omitted in the wording) are their brothers, the Muhājirūn. In al-Nāziʿāt and al-Aʿlā the doer is the human being and the thing preferred is the life of this world.

So īthār in the Qur'an is not "generosity" alone, as it commonly is on the tongue, but the act of preference in its pure form, and its worth depends on its object. It may be praise, as in al-Ḥashr; it may be steadfastness upon the truth, as in Ṭā Hā; it may be blame, as in al-Nāziʿāt and al-Aʿlā; and it may be an acknowledgment of God's favour, as in Yūsuf. This is a conclusion we draw ourselves from the inventory and not a text from anyone, yet the verses point to it: the verb is one, so ask about the object.

### "God Has Indeed Preferred You Over Us"

We begin with the first of them in the order of the muṣḥaf: Joseph's brothers standing before him, after he has said to them: I am Joseph, and this is my brother. Al-Ṭabarī relates from al-Suddī that when they heard this they apologised to him[^6]. They said: ﴿قَالُوا تَاللَّهِ لَقَدْ آثَرَكَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا وَإِن كُنَّا لَخَاطِئِينَ﴾ "They said: By God, God has indeed preferred you over us, and we were truly in the wrong" (12:91).

Al-Ṭabarī explained "āthara-ka" thus: "God has indeed favoured you over us and has preferred you with knowledge, forbearance and excellence." Ibn Kathīr explained it as their saying, in acknowledgment of him, that he has "the excellence and the preferment over them in physical form and character, in ample means and sovereignty, in authority and also prophethood, according to those who do not count them as prophets"[^6]. It is notable that "al-athara" in Ibn Kathīr's wording is not the exclusive possession that the hadith condemns but praiseworthy preference, so the meaning is one, putting forward and preferring, and only the one who puts forward and the one put forward differ.

The verse holds a lesson beyond its wording: those who stood in that place acknowledge that God chose someone other than themselves. They did not say "you overcame us," nor "fate wronged us," but "God has preferred you." This is the first meaning of īthār in the Qur'an: that God prefers whom He wills with what He wills, and that the sincere servant acknowledges it and does not contest it, even when it goes against himself. And the brothers made it the fruit of a confession of error: "and we were truly in the wrong."

### "We Will Never Prefer You": Preferring the Truth over the Established Fact

Then we stand in Egypt itself, but in Pharaoh's palace and not Joseph's. The magicians are cast down in prostration, having believed in the Lord of Aaron and Moses, and Pharaoh threatens them with the cutting off of hands and feet on opposite sides and with crucifixion on trunks of palm trees. They say: ﴿لَن نُّؤْثِرَكَ عَلَىٰ مَا جَاءَنَا مِنَ الْبَيِّنَاتِ وَالَّذِي فَطَرَنَا ۖ فَاقْضِ مَا أَنتَ قَاضٍ ۖ إِنَّمَا تَقْضِي هَٰذِهِ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا﴾ "We will never prefer you over the clear proofs that have come to us, nor over Him who created us. So decree whatever you are going to decree; you can decree only for this worldly life" (20:72).

Al-Ṭabarī said: "We will never prefer you, so as to follow you and deny Moses for your sake, over the clear proofs that have come to us," making īthār a following. Ibn Kathīr said: "We will never choose you over the guidance and certainty that have come to us… we do not choose you over Him who originated us and created us," making it a choosing. Both interpret "nuʾthiruka" as putting you ahead of the clear proofs[^7]. The phrase "wa-lladhī faṭaranā" admits two readings, both mentioned by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr: that it is an oath, or that it is coordinated with "al-bayyināt" (the clear proofs). On either reading the verb remains a denial of preferring Pharaoh over the proof and over the Creator.

The paradox here is that īthār appears negated, and that the negation is itself a stand. The magicians said "never" under the threat of mutilation and crucifixion, and justified their steadfastness with a simple argument: ﴿إِنَّمَا تَقْضِي هَٰذِهِ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا﴾ "you can decree only for this worldly life," which al-Ṭabarī explains as meaning that you cannot punish us except in this worldly life that passes away. Then they say after it: ﴿وَاللَّهُ خَيْرٌ وَأَبْقَىٰ﴾ "and God is better and more lasting" (20:73). The very phrase "khayrun wa-abqā" ("better and more lasting") will return some time later in Sūrat al-Aʿlā, in speaking of the Hereafter. It is as if these magicians understood early what the Qur'an would later reproach people for neglecting.

### When a Person Prefers What Perishes

On the other bank of this meaning comes the blame: ﴿فَأَمَّا مَن طَغَىٰ، وَآثَرَ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا، فَإِنَّ الْجَحِيمَ هِيَ الْمَأْوَىٰ، وَأَمَّا مَنْ خَافَ مَقَامَ رَبِّهِ وَنَهَى النَّفْسَ عَنِ الْهَوَىٰ، فَإِنَّ الْجَنَّةَ هِيَ الْمَأْوَىٰ﴾ "As for him who transgressed and preferred the life of this world, Hellfire will be his refuge. But as for him who feared standing before his Lord and restrained the soul from desire, Paradise will be his refuge" (79:37-41). Al-Ṭabarī said on "and preferred the life of this world": "He preferred the enjoyment of worldly life over the honour of the Hereafter and what God has prepared there for His friends, so he worked for this world, strove for it, and abandoned working for the Hereafter." Ibn Kathīr said: "He put it ahead of the command of his religion and of his Hereafter."

In al-Aʿlā the Qur'an addresses people generally, not an individual in particular: ﴿قَدْ أَفْلَحَ مَن تَزَكَّىٰ، وَذَكَرَ اسْمَ رَبِّهِ فَصَلَّىٰ، بَلْ تُؤْثِرُونَ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا، وَالْآخِرَةُ خَيْرٌ وَأَبْقَىٰ﴾ "Successful indeed is he who purifies himself, and remembers the name of his Lord and prays. Rather, you prefer the life of this world, while the Hereafter is better and more lasting" (87:14-17). Al-Ṭabarī says: "Rather you prefer, O people, the adornment of worldly life over the Hereafter," and relates from Qatāda: "So people chose the immediate, except those whom God protected." Ibn Kathīr says: "You put it ahead of the affair of the Hereafter, and give it priority over what holds their benefit and well-being in their livelihood and their return"[^8].

Among the things related about this verse is that ʿArfaja al-Thaqafī asked Ibn Masʿūd to recite Sūrat al-Aʿlā for him, and when he reached "Rather, you prefer the life of this world" he stopped reciting and turned to his companions and said: We have preferred this world over the Hereafter. Al-Ṭabarī reports it with its chain of transmission, and we have not found a ruling on its chain, so we mention it as evidence of how the early generations heard the verse, not as proof for a legal ruling[^9]. Yet what is beautiful in this report is that it shows us the blameworthy īthār as the one who reads the verse sees it: not as though it were an address to someone else, but as an address to himself.

A comparison between what is in Sūrat al-Aʿlā and what is in Sūrat al-Ḥashr also draws the eye: ﴿قَدْ أَفْلَحَ مَن تَزَكَّىٰ﴾ "Successful indeed is he who purifies himself" (87:14) and ﴿فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ﴾ "it is they who are the successful" (59:9). Success belongs to the one who purifies himself, and to the one who is protected from the stinginess of his soul. This is an observation we read from the two texts and have not found transmitted as a statement, so we present it as our own effort of reasoning.

### "And They Prefer Others over Themselves": What Does the Verse Say?

We return to the central verse and read it in full: ﴿وَالَّذِينَ تَبَوَّءُوا الدَّارَ وَالْإِيمَانَ مِن قَبْلِهِمْ يُحِبُّونَ مَنْ هَاجَرَ إِلَيْهِمْ وَلَا يَجِدُونَ فِي صُدُورِهِمْ حَاجَةً مِّمَّا أُوتُوا وَيُؤْثِرُونَ عَلَىٰ أَنفُسِهِمْ وَلَوْ كَانَ بِهِمْ خَصَاصَةٌ ۚ وَمَن يُوقَ شُحَّ نَفْسِهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ﴾ "And those who, before them, had settled in the abode and in faith love those who migrated to them, and find in their hearts no need for what they were given, and they prefer others over themselves, even if they are in need. And whoever is protected from the stinginess of his own soul, it is they who are the successful" (59:9).

Consider its order: first a love in the heart ("they love those who migrated to them"), then a chest empty of any desire ("and find in their hearts no need for what they were given"), then īthār in deed ("and they prefer"), then the hard condition ("even if they are in need"), then the cure ("and whoever is protected from the stinginess of his own soul"). Were we to describe īthār by its stages, we would add nothing to what is in the verse.

The commentators differed over some of its words. On "ḥāja" (need), al-Ṭabarī relates from al-Ḥasan that it means envy, and in al-Qurṭubī there is another possibility, connected with the smallness of what they are given, so they are content with it. As for "yuʾthirūna," its object is omitted. Al-Qurṭubī said: "The object of īthār is omitted," and Ibn ʿĀshūr said: "The object of 'they prefer' is not mentioned because the words 'of what they were given' point to it." There is a subtlety in this: perhaps there is breadth in it, for it did not say "they prefer their brothers," so the picture is not confined to one particular class (and this is from our own understanding)[^10].

Al-Qurṭubī defines īthār as "putting others before oneself and its worldly shares, out of desire for religious shares. That arises from strength of certainty, intensity of love, and patience in hardship." So he gave it three roots in the heart: a certainty that brings calm, a love that drives, and a patience that endures. Ibn ʿĀshūr defines it as "giving preference to one thing over another because of a noble quality or a benefit," and sees it as "a higher degree than what is conveyed by the words: and find in their hearts no need for what they were given." Ibn Kathīr holds that this station is "higher than the state of those whom God described by saying: and they give food, despite their love for it," because they "put the needy ahead of their own need, and begin with others before themselves at a time when they themselves need it." Between charity and īthār there lies the difference between giving out of your surplus and giving out of your need[^10].

You will notice among these five places that this is the only verse in which the one preferred over is the self: "over themselves." In Yūsuf the one preferred over is the brothers, in Ṭā Hā it is the clear proofs, but here the human being contends with his own self and puts something ahead of it. This is the hardest kind of preference, because the adversary is the one between the two sides of the ribs.

### The Stinginess of the Soul and Its Success

Then the verse closes with what makes īthār possible: ﴿وَمَن يُوقَ شُحَّ نَفْسِهِ﴾ "And whoever is protected from the stinginess of his own soul." The verb is in the passive, and in that there is an intimation, according to our reading, that the one who protects is not the owner of the soul alone. Ibn Kathīr said: "Whoever is safe from stinginess has succeeded and prospered."

They differed over what shuḥḥ is. Al-Ṭabarī says: "Shuḥḥ in the speech of the Arabs is miserliness and withholding of surplus wealth… As for the scholars, they hold that shuḥḥ in this place is only the consuming of people's wealth without right," and he reports from Ibn Masʿūd: "Shuḥḥ is only that you consume your brother's wealth unjustly; that is miserliness, and miserliness is an evil thing." Ibn ʿĀshūr relates from al-Ṭībī that "the difference between shuḥḥ and miserliness is extremely hard to draw," then says that "miserliness is the effect of shuḥḥ." So shuḥḥ, according to Ibn ʿĀshūr, is a root in the soul and miserliness is an effect that appears from it[^11].

What we take most nearly from this disagreement is that the Qur'an tied īthār to the cure of shuḥḥ, not to the availability of wealth. The Anṣār were not rich men giving from their surplus, and the verse supposes that they have "khaṣāṣa," which al-Ṭabarī explains as "need and want." The measure, then, is not abundance but the chest: a chest protected from shuḥḥ has room to put others first, and a chest not protected stays narrow even if the world is in its hand.

### The Anṣār: The Verse's Face in History

The verse did not remain an idea. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr relate in its context that when the Prophet ﷺ wished to assign the Anṣār land from al-Baḥrayn, they said: No, unless you assign the like of it to our brothers of the Muhājirūn. In al-Ṭabarī's version he ﷺ said to them: «فتكفونهم وتقاسمونهم الثمر» "Then you will spare them the labour and share the fruit with them," and they said: Yes[^12]. Ibn ʿĀshūr pointed out that the origin of the story is "in the Ṣaḥīḥ," without a number, and we were not able to find its number, so we mention it according to what these commentators transmitted.

In all of this we must not overlook that God described them as having "settled in the abode and in faith," so they are the people of the abode. Even so, they preferred those who came to them. Here you see īthār as the Qur'an presents it: not the sacrifice of someone who owns nothing, but the giving of someone who owns his land, his house and his sustenance, and who may himself need them.

### What Joins the Five Verses?

Let us read the verses together once more. God preferred Joseph with knowledge, forbearance and excellence, so here preference is a divine gift. The magicians refused to prefer Pharaoh over the clear proofs, so here preference is steadfastness. The Anṣār preferred their brothers over themselves, so here preference is generous giving. Others preferred this world over the Hereafter, so here preference is loss. And the verb in every place is the same verb.

What the Qur'an teaches us here, in our estimation, is that a person's whole life stands upon a single question: what do you prefer? And that īthār is not the privilege of the pious alone but the act of every human being every day, from which no one is free. Whoever has not preferred another over himself has preferred himself, and whoever has not preferred the Hereafter has preferred this world. What differs between people is the object of the preference, not its occurrence. This is an understanding we draw from the inventory, not a statement transmitted from anyone.

Perhaps, then, in speaking of God's preferring Joseph, the Qur'an meant to teach us that God Himself prefers His servant with what He does not prefer another with, and that the most beautiful human īthār is to be an echo of this divine preference: as God preferred you with a blessing, prefer another with it over yourself. This too is a matter of reflection and not of transmission.

### In Our Homes and Our Schools

It is good to distinguish between an īthār that educates and an īthār that neglects duty. By īthār we do not mean that a person should neglect the rights of those he supports: the Anṣārī's guest was a guest whom the Prophet ﷺ had sent to them, and the story of patience in hunger was a moment, not a method for life. The verse does not call to abandoning duty, but to a soul wide enough for the one who competes with it in duty.

The educator has practical entry points from the verse itself. Let him begin by teaching the child that "love" comes before "giving," for the Anṣār loved first and then preferred. Let him teach the child to ask himself in every choice: what do I prefer here? For that question is deeper than "what do I want?" And let him beware of praising outward īthār while raising hidden selfishness, for we say "share" and we are glad when our children take the largest portion. Let us remember that istithār is the counterpart of athara, as al-Rāghib indicates. And let everyone remember that shuḥḥ is guarded against, and that the servant asks for this protection from God as he asks it from himself.

In public life the picture recurs in many forms: a seat in a hall that someone else is waiting for, a post you step aside from for one more deserving, time you carve out of your rest for someone who needs you, a right you leave when no one's right is lost by it. And higher than these is to prefer the truth over someone you love or someone you fear, as the magicians did.

### Conclusion

Three scenes are enough to remind us: a dark house and a table that pretends to be eaten from, a prostration of magicians under Pharaoh's sword, and brothers acknowledging to Joseph that God preferred him over them. The first is giving, the second is steadfastness, and the third is acknowledgment, and their verb is one. So, O God, do not make us among those who preferred a hasty, perishing world, nor among those who held back their hand when called to give, protect us from the stinginess of our souls, and grant us the certainty that gives, the love that drives, and the patience that endures. ﴿رَبَّنَا اغْفِرْ لَنَا وَلِإِخْوَانِنَا الَّذِينَ سَبَقُونَا بِالْإِيمَانِ وَلَا تَجْعَلْ فِي قُلُوبِنَا غِلًّا لِّلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا رَبَّنَا إِنَّكَ رَءُوفٌ رَّحِيمٌ﴾ "Our Lord, forgive us and our brothers who preceded us in faith, and do not put in our hearts any resentment toward those who believe. Our Lord, You are indeed Kind and Merciful" (59:10).
