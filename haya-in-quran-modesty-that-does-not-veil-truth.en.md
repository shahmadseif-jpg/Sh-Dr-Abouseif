*Ḥayāʾ, modesty, shares its root with life itself, and is the sign of a living heart. In the four places where the Qurʾan uses the word in this sense, and in the example of the Prophet ﷺ, modesty governs how the truth is spoken, but it never excuses withholding it.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

The Messenger of God ﷺ was, as Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī (may God be pleased with him) describes him, «أَشَدَّ حَيَاءً مِنَ الْعَذْرَاءِ فِي خِدْرِهَا» — "more modest than a virgin in her chamber."[^1] She is that maiden, not yet used to mingling with people, whose eyes are lowered when she is looked at and in whose face modesty rises at the slightest cause. And yet this very man is the one who stood in the mosque addressing the people, commanding and forbidding, answering the hypocrites, and receiving the Qurʾan with rulings that touched the most private matters of his own household when the occasion required it. How can such intense modesty be joined with such boldness for the truth? This apparent contradiction is the entrance to a Qurʾanic concept that is rare in occurrence and abundant in effect.

### The Linguistic Foundation: Modesty from Life

The scholars of language note that ḥayāʾ (modesty) is derived from ḥayāt (life) itself. The Arabs say ḥayiya al-rajulu ḥayāʾan, "the man felt modesty," when he was ashamed, and the root of the meaning is one. As the living body moves, is affected, and responds, so the living heart is touched by shyness and shrinking when it confronts what is shameful, unlike the dead heart that cares for nothing. This is why Imām Ibn al-Qayyim (may God have mercy on him) says, in effect, that modesty is among the strongest proofs of the heart's life, and its departure a sign of the heart's death. This derivational link is no marginal linguistic detail. It is the key by which the few verses in which this root occurs are understood in their precise meaning, as will follow.

### Delimiting the Word: Four Places out of One Hundred and Eighty-Four

The root ḥ-y-y occurs in the Qurʾan one hundred and eighty-four times, in twelve forms. The great majority of these occurrences, about one hundred and seventy, turn upon an entirely different meaning: "life," in the sense of existence and subsistence, as in "He gives life and causes death," "the life of this world," and "the Ever-Living, the Self-Subsisting." This is the meaning that dominates the root, and it has no connection to ḥayāʾ, shyness and shrinking, which is our concern here.

More precisely still, the form yastaḥyī itself, the form closest in wording to the meaning of ḥayāʾ, occurs nine times, but six of these do not mean "shyness" at all. They mean "keeping alive," as in the description of Pharaoh:

﴿يُذَبِّحُ أَبْنَاءَهُمْ وَيَسْتَحْيِي نِسَاءَهُمْ﴾ (28:4)
*"…slaughtering their sons and keeping their women alive."*

This is another use of the same form, drawn from the meaning of "life" and not of "modesty." If the word is restricted to its precise meaning, shyness and inner shrinking before what is deemed shameful, only four places remain: the verbal form yastaḥyī twice in a single verse (33:53); once at the opening of Sūrat al-Baqarah (2:26); and once in the verbal noun istiḥyāʾ, in the story of Moses and the two daughters of Shuʿayb (28:25). This is among the rarest of the words treated in this series. That is why reliance on the Sunnah is wider than usual for this concept, though these four places remain the cornerstone upon which its Qurʾanic meaning is built.

### The Central Structure: A Modesty That Does Not Veil the Truth

What is striking is that these four places, few as they are, all meet in a single coherent meaning: modesty is a quality that regulates how the truth is spoken, not a justification for concealing it. The clearest witness is a single verse that gathers both sides in one breath:

﴿...إِنَّ ذَٰلِكُمْ كَانَ يُؤْذِي النَّبِيَّ فَيَسْتَحْيِي مِنكُمْ وَاللَّهُ لَا يَسْتَحْيِي مِنَ الْحَقِّ...﴾ (33:53)
*"…Indeed, that used to trouble the Prophet, and he is shy of [dismissing] you; but God is not shy of the truth…"*

This verse was revealed, as the books on the occasions of revelation relate, when some guests prolonged their stay in the Prophet's house after a banquet. The Prophet ﷺ was troubled by it but was too shy to ask them to leave, and so the Qurʾan took upon itself to say what he did not say out of modesty, declaring in the very same sentence that "God is not shy of the truth." The verse thus affirms the Prophet's modesty as a praiseworthy quality, and at the same time establishes that this modesty may never be a cause for the loss of a truth that must be spoken. What human modesty could not do, revelation undertook.

Consistent with this is the passage in Sūrat al-Baqarah:

﴿إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَسْتَحْيِي أَن يَضْرِبَ مَثَلًا مَّا بَعُوضَةً فَمَا فَوْقَهَا﴾ (2:26)
*"Indeed, God is not shy to set forth a parable, be it of a gnat or of something above it."*

It was revealed in answer to the disdain of some of the polytheists for the Qurʾan's use of small things, such as the gnat, the fly, and the spider, in its parables, which they thought beneath the dignity of revelation. God made known that He is not "shy," that is, He does not hold back out of embarrassment, from using any example that conveys the truth, however lowly it may appear in people's eyes.

As for the place of istiḥyāʾ in the story of the two daughters of Shuʿayb, it describes the gait of one of them when she was sent to invite Moses (peace be upon him):

﴿فَجَاءَتْهُ إِحْدَاهُمَا تَمْشِي عَلَى اسْتِحْيَاءٍ﴾ (28:25)
*"Then one of the two women came to him, walking with modesty."*

Modesty here is a manner of propriety in movement and bearing, yet it did not prevent her from completing her task and speaking to a stranger in order to deliver her father's message. Three places, three different pictures: a divine address, a teaching by parable, and an ordinary human situation. All agree on the same rule: modesty is propriety in the performance, not an excuse from it.

### The Prophetic Witness: A Branch of Faith, and No Veil over Knowledge

It is recorded in the two Ṣaḥīḥs, in the hadith of Abū Hurayrah (may God be pleased with him), that the Messenger of God ﷺ said: «الْإِيمَانُ بِضْعٌ وَسَبْعُونَ، أَوْ بِضْعٌ وَسِتُّونَ، شُعْبَةً، فَأَفْضَلُهَا قَوْلُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ، وَأَدْنَاهَا إِمَاطَةُ الْأَذَى عَنِ الطَّرِيقِ، وَالْحَيَاءُ شُعْبَةٌ مِنَ الْإِيمَانِ» — "Faith is seventy-odd, or sixty-odd, branches: the best of them is the saying 'There is no god but God,' the humblest is the removal of harm from the road, and modesty is a branch of faith."[^2] He thereby made modesty a part of the very edifice of faith, and no incidental social trait.

Another hadith, from ʿImrān ibn Ḥuṣayn (may God be pleased with them both), confirms this meaning: the Prophet ﷺ said, «الْحَيَاءُ لَا يَأْتِي إِلَّا بِخَيْرٍ» — "Modesty brings nothing but good."[^3] Another delicate hadith in the same sense is narrated by ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar (may God be pleased with them both): the Messenger of God ﷺ passed by a man of the Anṣār who was rebuking his brother about modesty, saying to him, in effect: You are so modest that modesty has done you harm! The Messenger of God ﷺ said: «دَعْهُ، فَإِنَّ الْحَيَاءَ مِنَ الْإِيمَانِ» — "Leave him, for modesty is part of faith."[^5] The Prophet ﷺ himself intervened to prevent modesty from being treated as a defect deserving of blame, establishing that it is a quality that brings nothing but good.

Yet the Sunnah, exactly like the Qurʾan, took care that this quality should not become a barrier to seeking knowledge and understanding of religion. ʿĀʾishah (may God be pleased with her) related that when a woman of the Anṣār asked the Prophet ﷺ about how to perform the ritual bath after menstruation, in a delicate matter, ʿĀʾishah remarked: «نِعْمَ النِّسَاءُ نِسَاءُ الْأَنْصَارِ، لَمْ يَمْنَعْهُنَّ الْحَيَاءُ أَن يَتَفَقَّهْنَ فِي الدِّينِ» — "How excellent are the women of the Anṣār: modesty did not prevent them from gaining understanding in religion."[^4] This hadith is a literal application of the Qurʾanic principle in 33:53: a modesty praiseworthy in temperament, which does not keep one from asking about what is necessary. Imām al-Bukhārī gave this meaning a chapter of its own, which he titled "The Chapter on Modesty in Knowledge," gathering in it both propriety and boldness in asking.

Thus the two hadiths meet. The first forbids that a person of modesty be blamed for his modesty; the second forbids that modesty be taken as a pretext for abandoning an obligation. There is no contradiction between them. They are the two sides of the same prophetic balance whose foundation was laid in the verse of al-Aḥzāb.

### From the Sayings of the People of Knowledge

Ibn Rajab al-Ḥanbalī, in *Jāmiʿ al-ʿUlūm wa-l-Ḥikam*, expounding the hadith "modesty is a branch of faith," distinguishes between two kinds of modesty. The first is innate, natural modesty, found in a person without acquisition, a quality that restrains from indecency and ill manners. This kind was most manifest in the Messenger of God ﷺ, so that he was described as more modest than a virgin in her chamber. The second is acquired, faith-born modesty, which arises from the servant's knowledge of his Lord and his keeping present in mind that God watches over him in every state. This is the higher of the two degrees, since it bears the fruit of mindful watchfulness, and is more than shyness before people alone. Al-Ghazālī, in *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn*, links modesty to the station of "watchfulness" (murāqabah), regarding it as a fruit of sensing God's nearness, and no mere social habit. Both views meet the linguistic origin with which this article began: the more alive the heart is through faith, the more complete its modesty, and the more exact its balance between good manners and the duty to make things clear.

### The Contemporary Application

Many today err in using "modesty" as a cover for silence about what must be said: whether in confronting an evil, or in refraining from seeking necessary religious knowledge out of shyness to ask, or in avoiding advice for fear of embarrassment. This appears plainly in the questions of jurisprudence concerning purification and the marital relationship, where many, men and women alike, refrain from asking even when their ignorance results in the corruption of an act of worship or hardship in married life. Here precisely the hadith of ʿĀʾishah about the women of the Anṣār is to be recalled: their asking about the finest details of the ritual bath after menstruation was no blemish on their modesty but a proof of their understanding.

The Qurʾanic and prophetic lesson is plain: modesty is a quality in the performance, not in the abstention. It refines the tone, chooses the moment, and guards dignity, but it does not drop the obligation. On the other hand, it is not permissible to exploit the banner of "frankness" or "boldness" as a pretext for abandoning modesty altogether, so that a person becomes coarse and harsh under the claim of speaking the truth, forgetting that the Prophet ﷺ, who conveyed the revelation complete and undiminished, was at the same time the most modest of people. Boldness against falsehood and modesty before what is shameful do not contradict one another; they are joined in the sound personality as they were joined in him ﷺ. The prophetic balance holds the two together: a modesty that refines the tongue, and a truth that is not veiled in the name of modesty.

### Conclusion

From only four places in the Qurʾan, and from a man described as more modest than a virgin in her chamber who nevertheless stood up to convey the most exacting and weighty of rulings, it becomes clear that modesty in this religion is neither weakness nor a shrinking from the truth. As its linguistic root shares with life itself, it is the sign of a living heart, one that refines its speech and does not abandon it. And God, Exalted is He, knows best; He is the Guardian of success.
