*In the Qurʾan, shukr is not a feeling kept hidden in the breast but a visible trace, a fullness within that overflows into word and deed. It runs in two directions, since God is Himself “Shakūr,” and its benefit returns to the one who gives thanks.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

The Prophet ﷺ stands in prayer through the night until his feet swell, and ʿĀʾishah (may God be pleased with her) says to him: Do you do this, O Messenger of God, when God has already forgiven you what is past of your sin and what is to come? He answers with a question that needs no reply: «أَفَلَا أُحِبُّ أَنْ أَكُونَ عَبْدًا شَكُورًا؟» — “Should I not love to be a grateful servant?”[^1]. This scene (two feet swollen from long standing, belonging to a man whose sin was already forgiven, and who therefore had no apparent need of further works) overturns a common understanding of gratitude as a calm inward feeling of thankfulness. *Shukr*, as the Prophet ﷺ embodied it that night, was not a feeling to be content with in the heart. It was an act that wearied the feet until they swelled.

This is precisely what the linguistic root of the word *shukr* discloses: it is manifestation, not concealment.

### The Word and Its Count

The root sh-k-r occurs in the Qurʾan seventy-five times[^3], in six forms: forty-six times as the triliteral verb *shakara* (most often as *tashkurūn* at the close of verses that enumerate blessings); once as the noun *shukr* (34:13); ten times as the intensive adjective *shakūr*; twice as the verbal noun *shukūr*; fourteen times as the active participle *shākir*; and twice as the passive participle *mashkūr*.

What is striking in this distribution is that the intensive adjective *shakūr*, which one would suppose at first glance to belong to grateful servants alone, occurs in several places as a description of God Himself: «إِنَّهُ غَفُورٌ شَكُورٌ» — “He is Forgiving, Appreciative” (35:30, 35:34, 42:23), and «وَاللَّهُ شَكُورٌ حَلِيمٌ» — “And God is Appreciative, Forbearing” (64:17). God is described by the very word that describes His grateful servant, an observation that deserves to be placed at the center.

### The Linguistic Root: Manifestation Against Concealment

In the origin of the language, *shukr* turns upon manifestation and disclosure, and not upon an inward feeling. One says *shakirat al-dābbah* of a beast when more fatness shows upon it than its ration of fodder warrants; that is, the trace of the little food it ate appears upon it in excess of its true measure. It is also said that *shakara* is an inversion of *kashara*, which means to uncover and reveal, and that its opposite on this view is *kufr*, the forgetting of a blessing, the veiling and concealing of it. And it is said that its origin lies in *ʿaynun shakrā*, an eye that is full; so *shukr* is a visible fullness of remembrance of the Benefactor which cannot be hidden in the breast[^2].

These three origins, for all their differences, meet at one meaning: *shukr* is not a hidden sensation but a manifest trace, whether a fatness that is seen, an uncovering that is proclaimed, or a fullness that overflows. This explains exactly why the gratitude of the Prophet ﷺ was an act that wearied the body, and not a feeling kept in the heart. True *shukr*, by virtue of its linguistic origin, must show its trace upon the limbs, just as fatness shows upon the grateful beast.

### The Central Structure: A Movement in Two Directions

Unlike most virtues, in which the act passes from the servant to God alone, the Qurʾan draws for *shukr* a movement in two opposing directions. On the one hand, the servant is asked to thank his Lord: «وَاشْكُرُوا لِي وَلَا تَكْفُرُونِ» — “And be grateful to Me, and do not be ungrateful to Me” (2:152). On the other hand, the Qurʾan describes God Himself as *Shakūr*, One who answers His servant's gratitude with yet more bounty: «لَئِنْ شَكَرْتُمْ لَأَزِيدَنَّكُمْ» — “If you are grateful, I shall surely increase you” (14:7). The Qurʾan goes further still, describing the striving of the righteous servant as *mashkūr*, a passive participle that makes God the One who gives thanks for His servant's effort: «فَأُولَٰئِكَ كَانَ سَعْيُهُمْ مَشْكُورًا» — “Those, their striving is appreciated” (17:19; likewise 76:22).

This double structure (a servant who thanks his Lord, and a Lord who gives thanks for His servant's striving) is rare among Qurʾanic concepts, for most virtues are ascribed to servants alone, without God being described by them in the same sense. *Shukr* breaks this pattern and becomes a mutual conversation rather than an act in one direction: each time the servant's gratitude becomes visible in his deed, God meets it with further bounty that merits fresh gratitude, in an ascending cycle that never breaks.

### A Recurring Model: “That You May Be Grateful”

The phrase «لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ» (“that you may give thanks”) recurs in the Qurʾan more than fifteen times, closing verses that enumerate tangible blessings: the ships that sail by His command (16:14, 30:46, 45:12); hearing, sight, and hearts (16:78, 23:78, 32:9, 67:23); the night and the day; sweet water. This recurrence shows that *shukr*, in the Qurʾan's conception, is not an incidental reaction but the very purpose for which these blessings were created and made serviceable. It is as if every tangible blessing carried within its folds an unspoken question to the one who benefits from it: will you let its trace appear upon you in a measure equal to its size?

One of the most exact portrayals of this model comes from the lips of Solomon (peace be upon him) when he saw the throne of Bilqīs set before him: «هَٰذَا مِنْ فَضْلِ رَبِّي لِيَبْلُوَنِي أَأَشْكُرُ أَمْ أَكْفُرُ» — “This is from the bounty of my Lord, to test me whether I am grateful or ungrateful” (27:40). Even a prophet granted a dominion such as none after him should have did not treat the blessing as a guaranteed entitlement, but as an open test whose outcome depends on his own conduct and not on his rank. Elsewhere God commands the House of David directly to turn gratitude into deed rather than word: «اعْمَلُوا آلَ دَاوُودَ شُكْرًا» — “Work, O House of David, in gratitude” (34:13). The command does not come as “remember” or “praise,” but as “work,” affirming once more that Qurʾanic *shukr* is an act and not merely an utterance.

### A Qurʾanic Model: Gratitude for One's Own Sake, Not for God's

In two nearly identical passages the Qurʾan makes clear that the fruit of gratitude returns to the servant himself and not to God, who is beyond any need of His creatures' thanks: «وَمَنْ شَكَرَ فَإِنَّمَا يَشْكُرُ لِنَفْسِهِ وَمَنْ كَفَرَ فَإِنَّ رَبِّي غَنِيٌّ كَرِيمٌ» — “Whoever is grateful is grateful only for his own soul; and whoever is ungrateful, my Lord is Self-Sufficient, Generous” (27:40). The same meaning recurs in Luqmān's counsel: «وَمَنْ يَشْكُرْ فَإِنَّمَا يَشْكُرُ لِنَفْسِهِ» — “Whoever is grateful is grateful only for his own soul” (31:12). *Shukr*, like the purification (*tazkiyah*) treated in an earlier article of this series, is thus a deed whose benefit returns first to its doer, and not a gift God requires. This dispels any thought that it is a transaction in which the servant is asked for something that profits his Lord; it becomes instead a mirror in which the servant beholds himself and his Lord's favor upon him together.

### The Prophetic Witness

The scene with which this article opened, the Prophet's ﷺ standing in prayer until his feet swelled, was narrated by al-Mughīrah ibn Shuʿbah, and it is a hadith agreed upon in the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s (al-Bukhārī no. 4573)[^1]. The precision of this witness lies in the fact that his reply ﷺ used the very Qurʾanic phrase with which Noah (peace be upon him) is described: «إِنَّهُ كَانَ عَبْدًا شَكُورًا» — “He was a grateful servant” (17:3). The Prophet ﷺ did not coin a new expression; he summoned the same Qurʾanic attribute by which the most grateful of the prophets was described, showing that *shukr* does not halt at the boundary of assured forgiveness but continues for as long as the servant is able to act.

### A Maqāṣidī Reading

From the recurrence of “grateful” set against “ungrateful,” as in «إِنَّا هَدَيْنَاهُ السَّبِيلَ إِمَّا شَاكِرًا وَإِمَّا كَفُورًا» — “We guided him to the way, whether he be grateful or ungrateful” (76:3), we see that the Qurʾan presents *shukr* as the first choice open to every human being guided onto the path, and not as a supplementary virtue demanded only of the righteous. Many of the exegetes have noted, too, that the one root that yields *kufr* in the sense of denial and rejection also yields *kufrān al-niʿmah*, the denial and veiling of a blessing. Disbelief in God and ingratitude for a blessing are thus two faces of one coin: the concealment of a manifest truth, whether the truth of divinity or the truth of benefaction. This lends *shukr* a doctrinal dimension and not merely a moral one: whoever gives thanks for a small blessing trains himself in acknowledging a manifest truth, and so draws nearer to acknowledging the greatest truth.

This observation meets what appeared in the previous article of this series on patience (*ṣabr*), for the two words are joined four times in one identical phrase: «إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِكُلِّ صَبَّارٍ شَكُورٍ» — “Truly in that are signs for every one who is steadfast in patience and grateful” (14:5, 31:31, 34:19, 42:33). As the Prophet's ﷺ hadith on the wondrous affair of the believer showed, patience and gratitude are not virtues chosen by turns according to circumstance, but complementary responses to the only two conditions every human being passes through: a blessing that calls for manifestation, or a trial that calls for holding the soul back from anxious grief. Whoever has mastered one without the other has not yet completed for himself the description “abundantly patient and grateful,” to which the Qurʾan has attached its clear signs.

### The Contemporary Application

Many today confuse *shukr* with a passing positive feeling, in which a person feels content for a moment and then forgets. Modern psychological practices commending “gratitude journaling” have spread widely. This is a beneficial practice, but it remains within the bounds of mental record-keeping unless it becomes, as the linguistic root itself teaches, a visible trace that goes beyond the written page: a word spoken to the one who showed favor, money spent, or work that puts the blessing to the use for which it was given. The difference between “feeling thankful” and Qurʾanic *shukr* is the difference between a feeling that stays hidden and a trace that appears.

The linguistic origin reminds us that true *shukr* needs a trace that can be seen: an explicit word of thanks that is spoken and not merely held within; or work that employs the blessing in its proper way; or wealth spent from what one has been given. Whoever feels grateful in his heart but does not turn that feeling into a word or deed that makes the blessing manifest has not yet reached the full meaning carried by the Qurʾanic term. And the scene of the Prophet ﷺ reminds us that *shukr* does not stop at the attainment of a lofty station or deliverance from danger, but continues as a renewed act for as long as the blessing endures, since there is no upper limit beyond which one may rest content with stillness.

### Conclusion

From a beast on which fatness shows beyond its ration of fodder, to two feet swollen with long standing belonging to a servant whose sin was already forgiven, the Qurʾan draws for *shukr* one unchanging meaning: a visible trace overflowing from an inward fullness, and not a feeling hidden in the breast. Whoever is grateful is grateful only for his own soul. And God Most High knows best; with Him is all success.
