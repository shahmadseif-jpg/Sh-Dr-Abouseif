*A study of the root sh-r-k as the Qur'an uses it: a partnership between people may be valid, but the like of it with God never is. The Qur'an does not treat shirk as an abstract creed but as a relationship, in which a share is given to someone else in what belongs to God alone.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

A verse came down upon the Companions of the Messenger of God ﷺ, and their hearts were shaken by it: ﴿الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَلَمْ يَلْبِسُوا إِيمَانَهُم بِظُلْمٍ﴾ "Those who believe and do not cloak their faith with wrongdoing" (6:82). Finding this hard to bear, they said: Which of us has not cloaked his faith in wrongdoing? The Prophet ﷺ did no more than turn them back to a word spoken long before their time: «إنه ليس بذاك، ألا تسمع إلى قول لقمان لابنه: ﴿إِنَّ الشِّرْكَ لَظُلْمٌ عَظِيمٌ﴾» "It is not as you think. Have you not heard what Luqman said to his son: 'Indeed, shirk is a great wrong'?"[^1]. These were men trembling for fear that some flaw lay in their faith which they could not see, and the answer reached them from a scene of a father counselling his son. It is a scene of counsel, not of oratory: ﴿يَا بُنَيَّ لَا تُشْرِكْ بِاللَّهِ ۖ إِنَّ الشِّرْكَ لَظُلْمٌ عَظِيمٌ﴾ "O my son, do not associate anything with God. Indeed, associating others with God is a great wrong" (31:13). Ibn Kathīr observed that Luqman first charged his son to worship God alone and only then gave the warning, so that the warning serves as the reason for what precedes it and is not a fresh beginning[^2].

The word has a wider place in the Qur'an than the phrase "the worship of many gods", which is what first comes to mind, can contain. So let us ask: what does the Qur'an do with this very root?

### One Hundred Sixty-Eight Times in Eight Forms

The root sh-r-k («ش ر ك») occurs in the Qur'an one hundred sixty-eight times, distributed over eight forms[^3]: the verb *ashraka* in its conjugations seventy-one times; the noun *sharīk* together with *shurakāʾ* forty times; the active participle *mushrik* with its derivatives forty-four times; the noun *shirk* five times; then *mushrikāt* three times, *mushrika* twice, *mushtarikūn* twice, and the verb *shārik* once, in the address to Iblis: ﴿وَشَارِكْهُمْ فِي الْأَمْوَالِ وَالْأَوْلَادِ﴾ "and share with them in wealth and children" (17:64). I took this count from the Qur'anic dictionary page of the corpus.quran.com project, and what I report of it is what the page itself records.

What strikes one in this list is that the numbers themselves carry a message: the verb *ashraka* alone accounts for more than a third of all occurrences. That is to say, the Qur'an speaks of shirk as an act that happens and recurs more than as a fixed attribute. We shall see that the object of this verb varies in a way that discloses the meaning.

### A Thing Between Two That Neither Holds Alone

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī says of the basic meaning of the chapter: "*Al-shirka* and *al-mushāraka* mean the mixing of two properties; it is also said: that a thing exist for two or more, whether that thing is a substance or a meaning"[^4]. Closer still is what Ibn Fāris sets down when he gives the root two origins, the first being "accompaniment and the opposite of being alone". He says: "The first is *al-shirka*, which is for a thing to be between two, neither of whom holds it alone", and he cites God's words about Moses: ﴿وَأَشْرِكْهُ فِي أَمْرِي﴾ "and make him a partner in my task" (20:32). He then mentions the second origin: "*Al-sharak*: the broad track of the road", from which comes "the hunter's snare, so called because of its stretching out"[^5].

All of this is innocent language and carries no blame in itself. Partnership may exist among brothers in an inheritance: ﴿فَهُمْ شُرَكَاءُ فِي الثُّلُثِ﴾ "then they are partners in the third" (4:12); it may also be the help that a prophet asks for his brother. What the Qur'an condemns is not partnership itself but transferring it to a place where it is not valid. It seems to me that Ibn Fāris's "second origin", the snare the hunter sets, meets what al-Rāghib reports from some scholars on the meaning of "the *sharak* of this world": "that is, its snare"[^4]. The root thus brings together association and the snare, as though whoever associates falls into both at once: into a partnership that is not valid, and into a snare that cannot be undone. I offer this as my own reading and not as a transmitted opinion.

### What the Qur'an Does with the Root: Setting Up a Partner in What Is God's

When we look at what the verb and the noun govern in the verses, we find that the Qur'an does not confine shirk to idols carved from stone. There is shirk in worship: ﴿وَلَا يُشْرِكْ بِعِبَادَةِ رَبِّهِ أَحَدًا﴾ "and let him not associate anyone in the worship of his Lord" (18:110); shirk in rule: ﴿وَلَا يُشْرِكُ فِي حُكْمِهِ أَحَدًا﴾ "and He associates no one in His rule" (18:26); shirk in legislation: ﴿أَمْ لَهُمْ شُرَكَاءُ شَرَعُوا لَهُم مِّنَ الدِّينِ مَا لَمْ يَأْذَن بِهِ اللَّهُ﴾ "Or do they have partners who have laid down for them in religion what God has not permitted?" (42:21); and shirk in creation and dominion: ﴿أَمْ لَهُمْ شِرْكٌ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ﴾ "Or do they have a share in the heavens?" (35:40).

There is a fine point in the way the verb is made to take its object. Most often it is "*ashraka bi-*" God, whereas "*ashraka fī*" occurs where the object is an authority or a prerogative, such as rule in 18:26 and the task in 20:32. Moreover, the negated object comes indefinite in the context of negation, "anything" or "anyone", as in ﴿يَعْبُدُونَنِي لَا يُشْرِكُونَ بِي شَيْئًا﴾ "They worship Me, associating nothing with Me" (24:55), and as in ﴿وَلَا أُشْرِكُ بِرَبِّي أَحَدًا﴾ "and I associate no one with my Lord" (18:38); and an indefinite noun in negation is general, covering everything other than God. In several places shirk is joined with the denial of any proof: ﴿أَمْ أَنزَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ سُلْطَانًا فَهُوَ يَتَكَلَّمُ بِمَا كَانُوا بِهِ يُشْرِكُونَ﴾ "Or have We sent down to them an authority, so that it speaks of what they used to associate with Him?" (30:35). In the Qur'an, then, shirk is a claim without authority and without knowledge.

The Qur'an also draws a contrast that exposes its method. It uses the very same root for partnership among people, and then turns it into an argument against them concerning God: ﴿هَل لَّكُم مِّن مَّا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُكُم مِّن شُرَكَاءَ فِي مَا رَزَقْنَاكُمْ﴾ "Do you have, among those whom your right hands possess, any partners in what We have provided for you?" (30:28). That is: if you are not content that your slaves should share your provision with you, how can you set up for God partners from among His servants in His dominion? It is an argument by analogy with daily life, taking a person from his own experience to what lies beyond his sight.

### "Indeed, Shirk Is a Great Wrong": Luqman's Counsel

Let us return to Luqman. The interpreters differed over the words ﴿إِنَّ الشِّرْكَ لَظُلْمٌ عَظِيمٌ﴾: are they part of Luqman's own speech, or a statement from God set apart from his speech yet joined to it in confirming the meaning? Al-Qurṭubī records the disagreement on this[^6]. Al-Ṭabarī explained "wrong" as "a grave error of speech", and Ibn Kathīr said: "that is, it is the greatest wrong"[^7]. The two explanations do not conflict: whoever makes anything a partner with God has erred in word and deed together, and has erred at the point beyond which there is no further error.

The report about the Companions points back to this meaning: they understood "wrongdoing" at first as every sin that cloaks faith, and the Prophet ﷺ made clear to them that the wrongdoing meant in the verse is shirk. This clarification is as much pedagogical as it is exegetical, for it reassures a frightened heart without softening the gravity of what is being warned against.

### "Except That They Associate Others": Belief in the Creator, Worship of Another

The most awakening word in this chapter is God's saying: ﴿وَمَا يُؤْمِنُ أَكْثَرُهُم بِاللَّهِ إِلَّا وَهُم مُّشْرِكُونَ﴾ "And most of them do not believe in God except while associating others with Him" (12:106). How can belief in God and shirk exist together? Al-Ṭabarī answers that they acknowledge that God is their Creator and Provider and the Creator of all things, and yet associate others with Him in the worship of idols and images; and he reports from Ibn ʿAbbās: "Part of their belief is that when they are asked, 'Who created the heavens, who created the earth, who created the mountains?' they say, 'God.' And they are polytheists." Ibn Kathīr mentions that the polytheists used to say in their talbiyah: «لبيك لا شريك لك، إلا شريكًا هو لك، تملكه وما ملك» "At Your service, You have no partner, except a partner who is Yours, whom You own and all that he owns"[^8]. They did not deny God. They made Him the head of an assembly in which there were others.

The exegetes genuinely differ here about who the verse was revealed concerning. Al-Qurṭubī gathered several views: that it concerns people who acknowledged God as their Creator and yet worshipped idols; or the People of the Book, in whom shirk and belief were mixed, as reported from al-Ḥasan; or the hypocrites. He transmitted from ʿAṭāʾ a striking remark on its meaning: "This concerns supplication: the disbelievers forget their Lord in times of ease, but when affliction strikes them they are sincere in supplication"[^9]. Al-Rāghib has another observation on the same verse, to which we shall return. These views indicate that the verse cannot be confined to a single group, and that the shirk which coexists with acknowledging the Creator is wider than any one historical form. Ibn Masʿūd asked the Prophet ﷺ: Which sin is greatest with God? He said: «أن تجعل لله ندًّا وهو خلقك» "That you set up a rival to God when He created you"[^10]. The last clause, "when He created you", is precisely the paradox of the verse: a rival to the One who created you.

### Partners in the Blessing and Partners in Legislation

Among the most delicate points of disagreement in this chapter is God's saying: ﴿فَلَمَّا آتَاهُمَا صَالِحًا جَعَلَا لَهُ شُرَكَاءَ فِيمَا آتَاهُمَا﴾ "But when He gave them a sound child, they made partners for Him in what He had given them" (7:190). Al-Ṭabarī mentioned that the interpreters differed over the partners they set up in what they were given of the child. Some said: partners in the name, and he reported the hadith of Samura about Eve and the naming of her child "ʿAbd al-Ḥārith". Ibn Kathīr, however, reported the hadith and then found it defective, and he transmitted from al-Ḥasan that the verse was about certain religious communities and not about Adam, and that they were the Jews and Christians to whom God gave children and who raised them as Jews and Christians. Then he said: "And this is one of the best explanations, and the one to which the verse is most properly applied"[^11]. Al-Rāghib cited the verse as evidence for what he called minor shirk[^4]. Perhaps the fruit of this disagreement is that we notice that the blessing is the place of the test: a child is a gift to be thanked for, not worshipped, and when the gift is attributed to someone other than its giver, the slide begins.

As for God's saying ﴿أَمْ لَهُمْ شُرَكَاءُ شَرَعُوا لَهُم مِّنَ الدِّينِ مَا لَمْ يَأْذَن بِهِ اللَّهُ﴾ "Or do they have partners who have laid down for them in religion what God has not permitted?" (42:21), Ibn Kathīr explained the partners as what was laid down for them by "their devils among the jinn and mankind": the prohibition of the *baḥīra*, *sāʾiba*, *waṣīla* and *ḥāmī*, and the permitting of carrion and blood. He cited from the Ṣaḥīḥ the saying of the Prophet ﷺ: «رأيت عمرو بن لحي بن قمعة يجرّ قصبه في النار» "I saw ʿAmr ibn Luḥayy ibn Qamaʿa dragging his intestines in the Fire", because he was the first to set loose the *sāʾiba* animals[^12]. Al-Zamakhsharī held that their partners were their devils, who made shirk appear fair to them, and then said: "And it is said: their partners are their idols. They are ascribed to them only because they took them as partners for God"[^13]. The disagreement between devils and idols stands, and in both cases a shirk appears that requires no prostration: when a created being is given the right to make lawful and unlawful in what belongs to God. What comes in the context of the devils supports this: ﴿وَإِنْ أَطَعْتُمُوهُمْ إِنَّكُمْ لَمُشْرِكُونَ﴾ "and if you obey them, you would indeed be polytheists" (6:121).

### The Parable of a Man Owned by Quarrelling Partners

The Qur'an depicts the effect of shirk upon the heart in a parable that can almost be seen: ﴿ضَرَبَ اللَّهُ مَثَلًا رَّجُلًا فِيهِ شُرَكَاءُ مُتَشَاكِسُونَ﴾ "God sets forth a parable: a man in whom are partners who disagree with one another" (39:29). Al-Ṭabarī said that he is shared among a group of owners "who differ and contend, and whose characters are bad". Al-Zamakhsharī added the psychological counterpart, picturing a slave owned jointly by partners, each claiming that he is his servant, so that he is "bewildered in his affairs"; the intended likeness is of one who affirms many gods and remains "bewildered and lost, not knowing which of them to worship and on whose lordship to rely"[^14]. Here it becomes clear that shirk is not merely an error of creed but a tearing of the heart. Whoever worships one Lord has a heart gathered, and whoever worships many lords is plundered by his desires.

### When Shirk Is Exposed

The Qur'an has a pattern that recurs regarding shirk: it is exposed in hardship and returns when rescue comes. ﴿ثُمَّ إِذَا كَشَفَ الضُّرَّ عَنكُمْ إِذَا فَرِيقٌ مِّنكُم بِرَبِّهِمْ يُشْرِكُونَ﴾ "Then when He removes the harm from you, behold, a group of you associate others with their Lord" (16:54), ﴿فَلَمَّا نَجَّاهُمْ إِلَى الْبَرِّ إِذَا هُمْ يُشْرِكُونَ﴾ "but when He delivers them to the land, behold, they associate others with Him" (29:65). This agrees with what al-Qurṭubī transmitted from ʿAṭāʾ on the verse of Joseph. Then comes the scene of the Gathering, where the partners disown those who worshipped them: ﴿فَزَيَّلْنَا بَيْنَهُمْ ۖ وَقَالَ شُرَكَاؤُهُم مَّا كُنتُمْ إِيَّانَا تَعْبُدُونَ﴾ "and We shall separate them, and their partners will say, 'It was not us that you worshipped'" (10:28), ﴿وَيَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ يَكْفُرُونَ بِشِرْكِكُمْ﴾ "and on the Day of Resurrection they will deny your association" (35:14). The polytheists then resort to denying what they had been upon: ﴿ثُمَّ لَمْ تَكُن فِتْنَتُهُمْ إِلَّا أَن قَالُوا وَاللَّهِ رَبِّنَا مَا كُنَّا مُشْرِكِينَ﴾ "Then their only excuse will be to say, 'By God, our Lord, we were never polytheists'" (6:23). The Qur'an called this "their trial": what they clung to is the very thing they were put to trial by, and it is what in the end turns into denial and disowning. The Qur'an also recorded their argument in this world: pleading destiny ﴿لَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ مَا أَشْرَكْنَا وَلَا آبَاؤُنَا﴾ "Had God willed, neither we nor our fathers would have associated anything" (6:148), and pleading tradition ﴿إِنَّمَا أَشْرَكَ آبَاؤُنَا مِن قَبْلُ﴾ "It was only our fathers who associated others before" (7:173).

### Those Who Disowned It: Abraham, Joseph, and the Man of the Two Gardens

Against them stand men whom the Qur'an made exemplars. In the scene of Abraham with his people, he says, having seen what they worship: ﴿إِنِّي بَرِيءٌ مِّمَّا تُشْرِكُونَ﴾ "I am free of what you associate" (6:78); then he declares: ﴿حَنِيفًا ۖ وَمَا أَنَا مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ﴾ "as one upright in faith, and I am not of the polytheists" (6:79); then he turns the argument back upon his people: ﴿وَكَيْفَ أَخَافُ مَا أَشْرَكْتُمْ وَلَا تَخَافُونَ أَنَّكُمْ أَشْرَكْتُم بِاللَّهِ مَا لَمْ يُنَزِّلْ بِهِ عَلَيْكُمْ سُلْطَانًا﴾ "How could I fear what you associate, while you do not fear that you have associated with God that for which He sent down no authority upon you?" (6:81). The Qur'an defined his creed by negation: ﴿حَنِيفًا ۖ وَمَا كَانَ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ﴾ "upright in faith, and he was not of the polytheists" (2:135), and ﴿كَانَ أُمَّةً قَانِتًا لِّلَّهِ حَنِيفًا وَلَمْ يَكُ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ﴾ "was a community in himself, devoutly obedient to God, upright in faith, and was not of the polytheists" (16:120). In the Qur'an, being a *ḥanīf* is defined by contrast with shirk.

Joseph, peace be upon him, cut off from his family, says: ﴿مَا كَانَ لَنَا أَن نُّشْرِكَ بِاللَّهِ مِن شَيْءٍ﴾ "It was not for us to associate anything with God" (12:38). It is as though the thing the righteous person guards most in exile is his heart, not his body.

The owner of the two gardens is an example that holds both lessons together. His believing companion says: ﴿وَلَا أُشْرِكُ بِرَبِّي أَحَدًا﴾ "and I associate no one with my Lord" (18:38), and the owner of the gardens himself, after everything he used to boast of is gone, says: ﴿يَا لَيْتَنِي لَمْ أُشْرِكْ بِرَبِّي أَحَدًا﴾ "I wish I had not associated anyone with my Lord" (18:42). It is a regret in the negated past tense, with the anguish of one who now knows what he did not know.

History offers a counter-example, ʿAmr ibn Luḥayy, whom Ibn Kathīr mentioned in the hadith cited above: the Prophet ﷺ saw him dragging his intestines in the Fire, and he was punished, as the hadith says, because he was the first to set loose the *sāʾiba* animals. It seems to me that there is a warning in this, that a law laid down by one individual may drag an entire nation behind it.

### Hidden Shirk: What Enters upon Those Who Pray

Al-Rāghib divides shirk into two kinds: "Great shirk, which is affirming a partner to God Almighty", and "minor shirk, which is regarding someone other than God together with Him in some matters, and that is showing off and hypocrisy"[^4]. He pointed out that the word shirk is among the words with more than one sense, and that the verse of al-Kahf, ﴿وَلَا يُشْرِكْ بِعِبَادَةِ رَبِّهِ أَحَدًا﴾ "and let him not associate anyone in the worship of his Lord", "is to be taken as covering both kinds". From this one understands that a door of shirk may open upon someone who prays and fasts.

The Sunna agrees with this meaning. It is established in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim from Abū Hurayra: «قال الله تبارك وتعالى: أنا أغنى الشركاء عن الشرك، من عمل عملًا أشرك فيه معي غيري تركته وشِركه» "God, Blessed and Exalted, said: I am the most self-sufficient of partners, needing no partnership. Whoever does a deed in which he associates someone else with Me, I leave him and his association"[^15]. God accepts no partnership in a deed, because He is the most self-sufficient of partners, that is, He has need of no one's help. Al-Rāghib also reports from the Prophet ﷺ: «الشرك في هذه الأمة أخفى من دبيب النمل على الصفا» "Shirk in this community is more hidden than the creeping of an ant upon a smooth rock"; the editor of the edition referred its sources to Aḥmad and al-Ṭabarānī, from the hadith of Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī, and transmitted the statement of al-Mundhirī: "and the rest of its narrators are trustworthy"[^16]. I have not found another verification of its grade, so I content myself with attributing it as the editor reported it. We say, too, what came in the supplication that accompanies it in the source references: «اللهم إنا نعوذ بك من أن نشرك بك شيئًا نعلمه، ونستغفرك لما لا نعلم» "O God, we seek refuge in You from associating anything with You knowingly, and we ask Your forgiveness for what we do not know."

### When the Threads Come Together

If we gather what has preceded, it becomes clear that the Qur'an does not use "shirk" as the name of an abstract creed but of a relationship: that God has what admits of no division, and another shares it with Him. One shares with Him in worship, in rule, in legislation, and in dominion. This sharing establishes no proof; it is exposed in hardship and ends in the partners disowning their associates. Yet God, for all the severity of this chapter, opened the door of hope for what is less than it, saying: ﴿إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَغْفِرُ أَن يُشْرَكَ بِهِ﴾ "Indeed, God does not forgive that partners be associated with Him" (4:48), and al-Ṭabarī explained that He does not forgive shirk and forgives what is less than that for whom He wills. Al-Qurṭubī said: "This is among the firmly established rulings agreed upon, about which there is no disagreement within the community", and then he judged that there is no abrogation in this chapter[^17]. Then the Qur'an made empowerment belong to those whose hearts are free of it: ﴿يَعْبُدُونَنِي لَا يُشْرِكُونَ بِي شَيْئًا﴾ "They worship Me, associating nothing with Me" (24:55), and it made that one of the qualities of the believers: ﴿وَالَّذِينَ هُم بِرَبِّهِمْ لَا يُشْرِكُونَ﴾ "and those who do not associate anything with their Lord" (23:59).

### What This Means for Our Lives: For the Individual and the Educator

The first thing asked of the individual is to question himself about the "partners" he does not name: Whom does he seek to please in his work? Whom does he obey in what is lawful and unlawful? Does he remember God alone when he is rescued, as he remembered Him when he was in distress? For the pattern of ﴿إِذَا هُمْ يُشْرِكُونَ﴾ "behold, they associate others" is not history that has passed, but a possibility in every rescue. It seems to me that the link between shirk and orientation, I mean the direction toward which the heart and effort turn, is clearer in the Qur'an than its link with abstract doctrinal denial. Whoever is occupied with people's approval over his Lord's approval has come near the door that al-Rāghib named minor shirk. The hadith of "the most self-sufficient of partners" teaches us not to be content with the outward soundness of a deed, but to look at who has shared in it with us.

As for the educator, he has in Luqman a teacher: he began with "O my son", and built his counsel on a reason, ﴿إِنَّ الشِّرْكَ لَظُلْمٌ عَظِيمٌ﴾ "Indeed, associating others with God is a great wrong", and according to Ibn Kathīr he was among those to whom God gave wisdom[^2]. We ought not to teach tawḥīd without teaching along with it what closes the doors of hidden shirk. In the Companions' stance there is another lesson: whoever fears for his faith is in good standing, but we must distinguish for him between praiseworthy fear, which drives him to self-review, and the whispering doubt that paralyses. And we teach our children that being a *ḥanīf* is not isolation but a steadfastness that does not turn from its direction: ﴿حُنَفَاءَ لِلَّهِ غَيْرَ مُشْرِكِينَ بِهِ﴾ "Be upright in faith for God, associating none with Him" (22:31).

### O God, Unite Our Hearts

A man in whom are quarrelling partners, and a man wholly devoted to one master: these are two parables the Qur'an struck for two hearts, and to one of the two we are heading. O God, gather our hearts upon You, and let no one have a share in them of our love for You, our fear of You, and our hope in You. O God, we seek refuge in You from associating anything with You knowingly, and we ask Your forgiveness for what we do not know. And join us to Abraham, upright in faith, a Muslim, who was not of the polytheists.
