*A study of the root b-l-w: its purpose, the double sense in which it means both testing and blessing, and its effect on the formation of the believer. The Qur'an treats ease and hardship as two settings of a single question.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

A father had been given a forbearing son, and when the boy was old enough to work alongside him, the command came to the father in a dream. What passes between father and son is a dialogue that tears at the heart: ﴿فَلَمَّا بَلَغَ مَعَهُ السَّعْيَ قَالَ يَا بُنَيَّ إِنِّي أَرَىٰ فِي الْمَنَامِ أَنِّي أَذْبَحُكَ فَانظُرْ مَاذَا تَرَىٰ ۚ قَالَ يَا أَبَتِ افْعَلْ مَا تُؤْمَرُ ۖ سَتَجِدُنِي إِن شَاءَ اللَّهُ مِنَ الصَّابِرِينَ﴾ "And when he reached the age to work with him, he said, 'O my son, I see in a dream that I am slaughtering you, so see what you think.' He said, 'O my father, do as you are commanded; you will find me, God willing, among the patient.'" (37:102). Then the words fall silent and the scenes speak: ﴿فَلَمَّا أَسْلَمَا وَتَلَّهُ لِلْجَبِينِ﴾ "And when they had both submitted, and he had laid him down on his forehead," (37:103). And at this moment, when time itself seems to stand still, there comes the brief divine comment: ﴿إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَهُوَ الْبَلَاءُ الْمُبِينُ﴾ "Indeed, this was the clear test." (37:106), and then the relief: ﴿وَفَدَيْنَاهُ بِذِبْحٍ عَظِيمٍ﴾ "And We ransomed him with a great sacrifice." (37:107).

The Qur'an did not say "this is an ordeal," nor "this is a calamity." It said "the clear *balāʾ*," choosing the word that in its origin means testing before it means pain. This is the door through which we enter the concept of *ibtilāʾ*: a word that in our speech today describes hardship, but which in the Qur'an is wider and more exact. It holds both the trial (*miḥna*) and the favour (*minḥa*), and it holds a divine act that never misses its purpose.

### Thirty-Eight Times in Five Forms

The root b-l-w («ب ل و») occurs in the Qur'an thirty-eight times, distributed over five forms[^1]: the plain verb *balā/yablū* twenty times; the augmented verb *yublī* twice; the verb *ibtalā*, of the *iftiʿāl* pattern (form VIII), eight times; the noun *balāʾ* six times; and the active participle *mubtalī* twice. It is worth noting that the last occurrence of the plain verb, in God's words about a kingdom that does not perish: ﴿لَّا يَبْلَىٰ﴾ "that does not perish" (20:120), has nothing to do with testing. It comes from *baliya al-thawb*, "the garment wore out" and perished. That leaves nineteen occurrences of the plain verb in the sense of testing.

This remark is not a linguistic luxury; it puts our hands on two origins that meet in the same root. Ibn Fāris says: "The letters bāʾ, lām, wāw and yāʾ have two origins: one is the wearing out of a thing, and the other is a kind of testing, to which reporting is also carried over."[^2] Al-Rāghib, by contrast, holds that the origin is one, and says: "*Baliya al-thawb* means the garment wore out… and *balawtuhu* means I tested him, as though I had worn him out by the frequency of my testing of him."[^3] Ibn Fāris adds what bears on our subject: "*Balāʾ* may be in good and in ill. God Almighty tests the servant with a good trial and with a bad trial… for by this he is tested in his patience and his gratitude."[^2] Whether we follow Ibn Fāris in the independence of the two origins or al-Rāghib in their connection, the picture al-Rāghib draws is an eloquent one: the one who tests turns the tested thing over and returns to it again and again until he wears it out, and the substance of a thing is known only by long acquaintance. The whole of the Qur'an's usage of *ibtilāʾ* belongs to the second origin, that of testing, apart from that one verse.

### A Recurring Purpose: "Which of You Is Best in Deed"

The first thing that strikes one in this usage is that the verb *yablū* is joined to one particular purpose in three particular places: in the creation of the heavens and the earth, with His Throne upon the water (11:7); in making what is on the earth an adornment for it (18:7); and in the creation of death and life: ﴿الَّذِي خَلَقَ الْمَوْتَ وَالْحَيَاةَ لِيَبْلُوَكُمْ أَيُّكُمْ أَحْسَنُ عَمَلًا ۚ وَهُوَ الْعَزِيزُ الْغَفُورُ﴾ "He who created death and life that He may test you as to which of you is best in deed; and He is the Almighty, the Forgiving." (67:2). It is as though the whole cosmos, from its heights to its earth, from its adornment to its perishing, had been arranged to be a single field for a single question.

The commentators have approaches to this verse that complement one another and do not conflict. Al-Ṭabarī takes the meaning to be "that He may test you and see which of you, O people, is more obedient to Him and quicker to seek His good pleasure."[^4] Ibn Kathīr draws attention to a fine point of wording, taking *aḥsan* ("best") to mean "better in deed," and said: "as Muḥammad ibn ʿAjlān said: He did not say 'more in deed.'" So the measure is goodness, not quantity; excellence, not abundance.[^5] Al-Qurṭubī widens the meaning, reporting that "that He may test you" means "that He may deal with you as one who tests, that is, that He may test the servant by the death of one dear to him to show his patience, and by life to show his gratitude," and he reports from al-Zajjāj that the lām attaches to the creation of life, not to the creation of death.[^6] Thus you see death and life both entering the examination: the one tests patience, the other gratitude.

If we reflect on what these verses share, we see that in the Qur'an *ibtilāʾ* is not an event that befalls life but the declared purpose of its existence. Hence the question "Why were we tested?" gives way to a more useful one: "How do we do good work in a field created for this very purpose?"

### By Ill and by Good: A Scale That Knows Neither Honour nor Disgrace

Then comes a usage that breaks a notion common in our minds: ﴿كُلُّ نَفْسٍ ذَائِقَةُ الْمَوْتِ ۗ وَنَبْلُوكُم بِالشَّرِّ وَالْخَيْرِ فِتْنَةً ۖ وَإِلَيْنَا تُرْجَعُونَ﴾ "Every soul will taste death, and We test you with ill and with good as a trial, and to Us you will be returned." (21:35). So testing falls by means of both opposites. And in another place: ﴿وَبَلَوْنَاهُم بِالْحَسَنَاتِ وَالسَّيِّئَاتِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَرْجِعُونَ﴾ "And We tested them with good things and bad that perhaps they would return." (7:168). On the first, al-Ṭabarī says that the test is "by ill, which is hardship," and "by good, which is ease, plenty and well-being," and he reports from Ibn ʿAbbās: "by ease and by hardship, and both are *balāʾ*," and from Ibn Zayd: "that We may see how they give thanks in what they love, and how they endure in what they dislike."[^7]

The picture reaches its fullest in Sūrat al-Fajr: ﴿فَأَمَّا الْإِنسَانُ إِذَا مَا ابْتَلَاهُ رَبُّهُ فَأَكْرَمَهُ وَنَعَّمَهُ فَيَقُولُ رَبِّي أَكْرَمَنِ﴾ "As for man, whenever his Lord tests him and honours him and favours him, he says, 'My Lord has honoured me.'" (89:15), ﴿وَأَمَّا إِذَا مَا ابْتَلَاهُ فَقَدَرَ عَلَيْهِ رِزْقَهُ فَيَقُولُ رَبِّي أَهَانَنِ﴾ "But when He tests him and restricts his provision, he says, 'My Lord has humiliated me.'" (89:16). Notice that the verb is the same in both places: "*ibtalāhu*." So God Almighty calls honouring and blessing a test, and calls tightening of provision a test, while it is the human being who reads the first as a deserved honour and the second as a humiliation. Al-Ṭabarī explained the verse by saying that God "tested him with blessings and wealth and honoured him with property," and the human being thinks this an honour.[^8] In these verses there is a correction to the scale by which we read things: wealth is not a sign of approval, nor poverty a sign of displeasure; they are two settings for one question.

On this foundation al-Rāghib built one of the grounds for calling obligation (*taklīf*) a *balāʾ*, saying: "God Almighty's testing of His servants, sometimes by what gladdens so that they may give thanks, sometimes by what harms so that they may endure. So both the trial and the favour become *balāʾ*: the trial calls for patience, the favour calls for gratitude. And fulfilling the claims of patience is easier than fulfilling the claims of gratitude, so the favour became the greater of the two tests."[^9] This is a statement that deserves a pause. We dread the trial and long for the favour, and al-Rāghib says the heavier test is the second. The scene of Solomon, peace be upon him, bears witness to this, when he saw the throne of Bilqīs set before him in the blink of an eye: ﴿فَلَمَّا رَآهُ مُسْتَقِرًّا عِندَهُ قَالَ هَٰذَا مِن فَضْلِ رَبِّي لِيَبْلُوَنِي أَأَشْكُرُ أَمْ أَكْفُرُ ۖ وَمَن شَكَرَ فَإِنَّمَا يَشْكُرُ لِنَفْسِهِ ۖ وَمَن كَفَرَ فَإِنَّ رَبِّي غَنِيٌّ كَرِيمٌ﴾ "And when he saw it placed before him, he said, 'This is from the favour of my Lord, to test me whether I will be grateful or ungrateful. And whoever is grateful is grateful only for himself; and whoever is ungrateful, then indeed my Lord is Free of need and Generous.'" (27:40). A prophet and a king, and at the moment of being granted power he does not see the blessing as the end of the road but sees it as a question: shall I give thanks or be ungrateful? Ibn Kathīr explained it with the words: "to test me, whether I shall be thankful or ungrateful."[^10]

### A Word That Carries Both Hardship and Blessing

In the noun *balāʾ* there is a double meaning for which there is scarcely a parallel. In God's words about the Children of Israel: ﴿وَإِذْ نَجَّيْنَاكُم مِّنْ آلِ فِرْعَوْنَ يَسُومُونَكُمْ سُوءَ الْعَذَابِ يُذَبِّحُونَ أَبْنَاءَكُمْ وَيَسْتَحْيُونَ نِسَاءَكُمْ ۚ وَفِي ذَٰلِكُم بَلَاءٌ مِّن رَّبِّكُمْ عَظِيمٌ﴾ "And [recall] when We delivered you from the people of Pharaoh, who afflicted you with the worst torment, slaughtering your sons and keeping your women alive; and in that was a great trial from your Lord." (2:49). Al-Ṭabarī reports from Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, al-Suddī and Ibn Jurayj that *balāʾ* here means "blessing," and then he concludes that "the origin of *balāʾ* in the speech of the Arabs is testing and trial, and then it is used for good and for ill."[^11] Al-Rāghib combined the two views, saying that *balāʾ* here "goes back to both matters: to the trial in His words… 'slaughtering your sons…', and to the favour of delivering them."[^12] So the verse is one and the word is one, and in it are both the face of affliction and the face of deliverance.

This double meaning reaches its peak in God's words in Sūrat al-Anfāl: ﴿وَلِيُبْلِيَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ مِنْهُ بَلَاءً حَسَنًا﴾ "and that He might bestow upon the believers from Himself a good *balāʾ*, that is, a fine bestowal" (8:17). Here "a good *balāʾ*" certainly cannot mean hardship. Al-Ṭabarī explains it as "a fine and beautiful blessing."[^13] Ibn ʿĀshūr added an analysis of the form, saying: "The usual meaning is being afflicted with ill, then it was extended to cover being afflicted with good," and that those who used it "made the hamza in it signify removal, that is, the removal of the *balāʾ* that came to be used chiefly for affliction with ill," and then he explained the verse as God giving the believers "a good gift for which they give thanks."[^14] It is as though the hamza in *yublī* strips the word of the weight of hardship, leaving the meaning of a beautiful testing: a gift that is at the same time a question.

From this it becomes clear that the noun *balāʾ* may be hardship and may be blessing, and what joins them is that both are tests. Here the commentators differ on a decisive point: in God's words "Indeed, this was the clear *balāʾ*" (37:106), al-Ṭabarī holds that it is "the test which makes clear, to anyone who reflects on it, that it is a severe affliction and a great trial," and he reports from Ibn Zayd that "*balāʾ* in this place is ill and is not a test."[^15] Ibn Kathīr, however, makes it "the plain and manifest test, since he was commanded to slaughter his son and hastened to do so, submitting to God's command."[^16] I incline, and I offer this as a reading and not a ruling, to the view that the closest to the root's usage in the verse is the manifest test, because God Almighty attached to it what shows its outcome: ﴿قَدْ صَدَّقْتَ الرُّؤْيَا﴾ "You have fulfilled the vision." (37:105), and it is a test from which one expects a confirmation. But in the saying of Ibn Zayd there is a warning of the severity of what took place, which must not be belittled.

### Abraham: From the Words to the Sacrifice

What stands out is that the prophet himself who passed through this scene is the one with whom the Qur'an opened the door of *ibtilāʾ* in al-Baqara: ﴿وَإِذِ ابْتَلَىٰ إِبْرَاهِيمَ رَبُّهُ بِكَلِمَاتٍ فَأَتَمَّهُنَّ ۖ قَالَ إِنِّي جَاعِلُكَ لِلنَّاسِ إِمَامًا﴾ "And [recall] when Abraham was tried by his Lord with commands and he fulfilled them. He said, 'Indeed, I am making you a leader for the people.'" (2:124). Between the two scenes there is a link worth seeing. Al-Ṭabarī interprets the testing here as "a test by obligations He laid upon him and a command He commanded him," and notes that the people of interpretation differed in identifying those words.[^17] Ibn ʿĀshūr, for his part, says: "*Ibtilāʾ* is the *iftiʿāl* form of *balāʾ*… and it is a well-known figurative use of it, because when someone charges another with something, his charge includes waiting for the other to do it or leave it, and so testing necessarily follows; it is thus a figure upon a figure, and what is meant here is obligation (*taklīf*)," and he adds that there is no difficulty in attributing *ibtilāʾ* to God "once you know that it is figurative for obligation."[^18]

Here an aspect of al-Rāghib comes to light that we cannot do without. He establishes that when *ibtilāʾ* is attributed to God, what is meant is "only the manifestation of a thing's goodness or badness, not coming to know its state… since God is the Knower of the unseen," and he illustrates this with this very verse.[^19] That is, what is meant is the manifestation of whatever goodness or badness is in the servant, not the arising of knowledge in God. On this principle, in my reading, God's words are to be understood: ﴿وَلَنَبْلُوَنَّكُمْ حَتَّىٰ نَعْلَمَ الْمُجَاهِدِينَ مِنكُمْ وَالصَّابِرِينَ وَنَبْلُوَ أَخْبَارَكُمْ﴾ "And We will surely test you until We know those among you who strive and are patient, and We test your affairs." (47:31); so that the knowledge here is knowledge of manifestation and occurrence, which is an interpretation I offer and do not ascribe to anyone. And perhaps the first to benefit from this manifestation is the servant himself, who sees his reality after it had been hidden from him.

It is worth noting, after that, what Ibn ʿĀshūr mentioned: that God's words "I am making you a leader for the people" may be a substitution for "tested" or an explanation of the testing.[^18] So testing by obligations here leads to leadership, not to hardship. And if we put this verse together with the verse of al-Ṣāffāt, we realise that Abraham's testing was not a single stroke but a course: obligations fulfilled, which became leadership, then a sacrifice submitted to, which became a ransom. All that a servant completes of his testing raises him a degree. This is a conclusion I offer as reflection on the two texts; the commentators did not state it in this form.

### Three Grounds for Calling Obligation a *Balāʾ*

One of the fruits of this chapter is that al-Rāghib does not stop at saying that obligation is a test; he gives three grounds for it. First, that all obligations are "hardships on the bodies," and in this respect they are *balāʾ*. Second, that they are tests. Third, what was mentioned above, that God tests by what gladdens and by what harms.[^9] This explains why in the Qur'an the meaning of obligation is never separate from the meaning of testing: prayer, fasting, struggle and the fulfilment of trusts are all questions in one field, and each answer shows the quality of the one who gives it.

### Between Ibtilāʾ and Fitna

It may be asked: where does *fitna* stand in all this, which we treated in an earlier article of this series? Here it is worth noticing that the two words meet in a single verse, the verse of al-Anbiyāʾ (21:35) which we met above: "We test you… as a *fitna*." Al-Ṭabarī reported a delicate distinction from Qatāda: "We test you with ill as *balāʾ*, and with good as *fitna*," while Ibn ʿAbbās made hardship and ease "both *balāʾ*"[^7]; and al-Qurṭubī mentioned that *fitna* is a verbal noun of a different form from its verb, so that the meaning is "We try you by hardship and ease, the lawful and the unlawful, and see how you give thanks and how you endure."[^20]

As for Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt, it says: ﴿أَحَسِبَ النَّاسُ أَن يُتْرَكُوا أَن يَقُولُوا آمَنَّا وَهُمْ لَا يُفْتَنُونَ﴾ "Do the people think that they will be left to say, 'We believe,' and not be tried?" (29:2). This verse is often cited for the "law of *ibtilāʾ*," yet the root b-l-w does not occur in it; the word there is "*yuftanūn*." Ibn Kathīr explained it with the other word, saying that God "must test His believing servants."[^21] So the two words lie side by side in usage and in interpretation. I incline in my reading, which I have not found anyone state explicitly, to the view that *ibtilāʾ* is the frame that encloses the whole of life and its obligation, and that *fitna* is the moment of refining in which the metal shows itself within that frame; yet this is a difference of usage and not a uniform rule, and the reader may consult the article on *fitna* in this series for the detail of its side.

### What the Prophet ﷺ Said

The Sunna clarifies this chapter. Muṣʿab ibn Saʿd narrated from his father, who said: I said, O Messenger of God, which of the people suffer the severest *balāʾ*? He said: «الأنبياء ثم الأمثل فالأمثل، فيُبتلى الرجل على حسب دينه، فإن كان دينه صلبًا اشتدّ بلاؤه، وإن كان في دينه رقّة ابتُلي على حسب دينه، فما يبرح البلاء بالعبد حتى يتركه يمشي على الأرض ما عليه خطيئة» "The prophets, then the next best and the next best. A man is tested according to his religion: if his religion is firm, his trial is severe, and if there is softness in his religion, he is tested according to his religion. And trial does not cease to be with the servant until it leaves him walking on the earth with no sin upon him."[^22] So the trial here is measured according to the religion, and its stated aim in the hadith is that the servant comes out of it "with no sin upon him." Ibn Mājah narrated it with the same sense in the wording "The prophets, then the next best and the next best."[^23]

As for the other half of *ibtilāʾ*, which is testing by blessing, the Prophet ﷺ combined it with the first on one scale: «عجبًا لأمر المؤمن إن أمره كله خير، وليس ذاك لأحد إلا للمؤمن: إن أصابته سرّاء شكر فكان خيرًا له، وإن أصابته ضرّاء صبر فكان خيرًا له» "How wondrous is the affair of the believer! All of his affair is good, and this is true of no one but the believer: if ease befalls him he gives thanks, and that is good for him; and if hardship befalls him he endures, and that is good for him."[^24] This is precisely al-Rāghib's division: the favour calls for gratitude and the trial calls for patience.

### When We Read Ibtilāʾ in Our Own Lives

When we move from the text to life, we find that this understanding has a direct effect on upbringing and formation. The first thing it teaches us is to stop reading our states by the scale of "He honoured me / He humiliated me." The sick person has not been humiliated, and the wealthy person has not necessarily been honoured; each stands before a question. This is no call to passivity before pain, for al-Rāghib said that "the trial calls for patience," but it is a patience regarded as an answer and not as surrender.

The second is that we change where the question is placed. Instead of asking, when will this end? we ask, what is the "best deed" now, in this very state? The Qur'an did not make the measure the abundance of deeds but their goodness, and a small, well-done deed may show what a great deal of hurried work does not. This is of benefit to parents, teachers and imams especially; how many a young person is measured in his surroundings by how much he does and not by how well, and how many who serve people exhaust themselves while forgetting that God asked about "the best."

The third is that we awaken attention to the testing of blessing, which is the more hidden. Whoever has been given knowledge, a pulpit, wealth and health should stand at the words of Solomon, peace be upon him, "shall I give thanks or be ungrateful?" and make them a daily question. And finally, that we follow Abraham, peace be upon him: that we strive to complete what we are charged with, for he is the one who completed the words in the verse, and after it came his Lord's word to him: "I am making you a leader for the people."

### Conclusion

Thirty-eight times this root has come in the Qur'an, and in its places of testing the question was one though the pictures were many: what will you do with what We gave you and with what We withheld? Whoever understands that life and death were created to test him as to which of the doers is best in deed will not attach his heart to ease nor be defeated before hardship, and will go on looking in every state to the face of the question in it.

O God, do not make this world our greatest concern nor the limit of our knowledge. Grant us, at times of trial, the patience of the patient, and at times of favour, the gratitude of the grateful. Make us among those who, when You test them, do good work; when You give them, give thanks; when You withhold from them, endure; and when they sin, seek forgiveness. And do not leave us to ourselves for the blink of an eye. ﴿وَهُوَ الْعَزِيزُ الْغَفُورُ﴾ "And He is the Almighty, the Forgiving." (67:2).
