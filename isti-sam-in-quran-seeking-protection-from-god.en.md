*A study of a verb that occurs only once in the Qurʾan, and of its relation to iʿtiṣām (holding fast) and ʿiṣmah (protection). By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA).*

When the woman could bear no longer Joseph's refusal of her summons, she gathered the women, showed them the young man, and then said what she had long concealed: ﴿وَلَقَدْ رَاوَدتُّهُ عَن نَّفْسِهِ فَاسْتَعْصَمَ﴾ ("I certainly sought to seduce him, but he refused firmly, seeking protection") (Yūsuf 12:32). A single word sums up a long stance of steadfastness: «fa-staʿṣama». It is a form that appears nowhere else in the Qurʾan but in this verse, and it is among the most precise descriptions of the act of abstaining from sin, because it does not say "he refrained" or "he kept away"; it says that he sought protection (ʿiṣmah) for himself and took hold of its means.

### Surveying the Term

The verb «istaʿṣama» occurs in the Qurʾan once, in the verse cited above. The root ʿ-ṣ-m, however, has several uses in the Qurʾan, all united by the idea of preventing and preserving: «لا عاصم اليوم من أمر الله إلا من رحم» ("There is no protector today from God's command, except for those on whom He has mercy") (Hūd 11:43); «سآوي إلى جبل يعصمني من الماء» ("I will take refuge on a mountain that will protect me from the water") (Hūd 11:43); «والله يعصمك من الناس» ("And God will protect you from the people") (al-Māʾidah 5:67); «قل من ذا الذي يعصمكم من الله إن أراد بكم سوءًا» ("Say: Who is it that can protect you from God if He intends evil for you?") (al-Aḥzāb 33:17); and then iʿtiṣām, holding fast: «ومن يعتصم بالله فقد هُدي إلى صراطٍ مستقيم» ("Whoever holds fast to God has indeed been guided to a straight path") (Āl ʿImrān 3:101); «واعتصموا بحبل الله جميعًا» ("And hold fast, all together, to the rope of God") (Āl ʿImrān 3:103); «فاعتصموا بالله هو مولاكم» ("So hold fast to God; He is your Protector") (al-Ḥajj 22:78). Accordingly, istiʿṣām, iʿtiṣām and ʿiṣmah form a single semantic family.

### The Linguistic Root: Gripping and Preventing

The basic sense of ʿ-ṣ-m is to grip, to prevent, and to cleave to. From it comes «al-ʿiṣmah», that by which something is held, and «al-ʿiṣām», the cord that ties a waterskin; and one says «iʿtaṣama bi-kadhā» when one has clung to something. The form «istafʿala» carries the sense of seeking, so istiʿṣām is the seeking of ʿiṣmah. The commentators hold that «istaʿṣama» conveys reaching the utmost limit of abstention: as though Joseph did not merely abstain, but went to the furthest length in fortifying himself, and held fast to his plea to God for protection, which is just what his whole scene indicates.

Scholars have also distinguished, in technical usage, between «ʿiṣmah» proper to the prophets, which is God's preservation of them from falling into what does not befit prophethood, and «istiʿṣām», which is the servant's own act in seeking preservation. The first is a grace from God, the second is a deed of the servant, and the Qurʾan joins the two in Joseph's scene: effort, then preservation.

### Istiʿṣām in the Scene: Three Degrees

The word «fa-staʿṣama» in verse thirty-two does not stand alone; it is the outcome of a chain of stances that unfold through the scene. The first is seeking refuge in God at the moment of the solicitation: ﴿مَعَاذَ اللَّهِ ۖ إِنَّهُ رَبِّي أَحْسَنَ مَثْوَايَ ۖ إِنَّهُ لَا يُفْلِحُ الظَّالِمُونَ﴾ ("God forbid! He is my lord, who has given me an honourable home. Indeed, the wrongdoers do not succeed") (Yūsuf 12:23). It contains three arguments: that he is under God's protection, that the ʿAzīz had been good to him, and that there is no success in wrongdoing. The second is the proof he saw: ﴿وَلَقَدْ هَمَّتْ بِهِ وَهَمَّ بِهَا لَوْلَا أَن رَّأَىٰ بُرْهَانَ رَبِّهِ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ لِنَصْرِفَ عَنْهُ السُّوءَ وَالْفَحْشَاءَ ۚ إِنَّهُ مِنْ عِبَادِنَا الْمُخْلَصِينَ﴾ ("She desired him, and he would have desired her, had he not seen the proof of his Lord. Thus it was, that We might turn away from him evil and indecency; he was among Our devoted servants") (Yūsuf 12:24). The third is flight, the race to the door: ﴿وَاسْتَبَقَا الْبَابَ﴾ ("And they raced to the door") (Yūsuf 12:25). Thus istiʿṣām began with the remembrance of God, was reinforced by a proof from Him, and was translated into a physical flight.

In verse twenty-four there is a well-known question about the meaning of «hamma bihā» ("he would have desired her"). This is not the place to set out the disagreement; it suffices to say that the verifying scholars among the commentators held that Joseph, peace be upon him, was free of any resolve toward indecency, and that the verse itself bears witness that evil and indecency were turned away from him and that he was among the devoted (al-mukhlaṣīn). What matters here is that the Qurʾan itself named his stance «istiʿṣām».

### The Prayer in Istiʿṣām: Confessing Weakness

Then comes Joseph's prayer when he was threatened with prison: ﴿قَالَ رَبِّ السِّجْنُ أَحَبُّ إِلَيَّ مِمَّا يَدْعُونَنِي إِلَيْهِ ۖ وَإِلَّا تَصْرِفْ عَنِّي كَيْدَهُنَّ أَصْبُ إِلَيْهِنَّ وَأَكُن مِّنَ الْجَاهِلِينَ﴾ ("He said: My Lord, prison is dearer to me than what they invite me to; and unless You turn their scheming away from me, I will incline toward them and become one of the ignorant") (Yūsuf 12:33). Here istiʿṣām shows its true nature: not self-confidence, but an acknowledgment that, were it not for God's turning away, the soul would incline. The one who seeks protection says to his Lord: I am weak, so turn it away from me. This joining of practical precaution (prison is dearer to me; the flight to the door) with dependence on God (unless You turn away from me) is the very core of the concept. After it comes the answer: ﴿فَاسْتَجَابَ لَهُ رَبُّهُ فَصَرَفَ عَنْهُ كَيْدَهُنَّ﴾ ("So his Lord answered him and turned their scheming away from him") (Yūsuf 12:34).

Here we see the relation between istiʿṣām and the soul that commands to evil (an-nafs al-ammārah; an earlier article has treated it): the soul commands, the servant seeks protection, and God protects. The concept thus has three parties, and does not stand on any one of them alone.

### The Prophetic Witness

It is established in the two Ṣaḥīḥs that the Prophet ﷺ named, among the seven whom God will shade in His shade on the Day when there is no shade but His: «ورجلٌ دعته امرأةٌ ذات منصبٍ وجمال، فقال: إني أخاف الله» ("A man whom a woman of rank and beauty summoned, and he said: I fear God")[^1]. This is the image of istiʿṣām: the inducements are complete (rank and beauty, that is, standing and temptation), and the reply is fear of God, not mere social scruple. The ḥadīth also draws attention to the fact that the invitation may come from a woman of rank, that is, that the capacity to harm and to entice may be combined in people of influence, as it was in Joseph's case.

### A Maqāṣid-Based Reading

The concept addresses the preservation of religion, honour and self together. When istiʿṣām settles into a Muslim's conduct, it protects human dignity from being blackmailed, the family from betrayal, and society from indecency. The Qurʾan teaches that preservation does not come through external prevention alone: neither walls nor locks saved Joseph, but a heart attached to its Lord. Yet the Qurʾan was not content with the heart alone, for it mentions the race to the door, that is, taking the outward means.

It is to be noted that the Qurʾan placed istiʿṣām ahead of the trial of prison: Joseph chose prison over sin and said, «prison is dearer to me». That is, istiʿṣām may carry a heavy price in freedom, reputation and standing, and that price is lighter for the believer than the price of disobedience. In this is a lesson for the Muslim to put the safety of his religion before his own comfort.

### «The Devoted» and the Secret of Preservation

The twenty-fourth verse closes its explanation with the words «إنه من عبادنا المخلَصين» ("He was among Our devoted servants"), which has been read with a fatḥah and with a kasrah on the lām (al-mukhlaṣīn and al-mukhliṣīn). Both readings enrich the meaning: with the fatḥah, it is God who chose him and made him His own; with the kasrah, it is he who made his religion sincere for God. The Qurʾan has joined the two in one place: the servant's sincerity and the Lord's choosing. Accordingly, istiʿṣām does not rest on human striving alone, but on ground that sincerity had prepared beforehand.

In this is an educational lesson: if the beginning of the road is sincerity, then whoever wishes to stand firm at the moment of temptation must build up his heart before it arrives. Whoever has no store of connection with God in his hours of solitude will find nothing to lean on when the solicitation comes. That is why the first thing Joseph uttered was «God forbid!» (maʿādh Allāh), that is, he returned to what had long been settled in his heart.

### From Individual Istiʿṣām to Collective Iʿtiṣām

The Qurʾan moves from istiʿṣām in the individual trial to iʿtiṣām, holding fast to the rope of God, in the common affair: ﴿وَاعْتَصِمُوا بِحَبْلِ اللَّهِ جَمِيعًا وَلَا تَفَرَّقُوا﴾ ("And hold fast, all together, to the rope of God, and do not be divided") (Āl ʿImrān 3:103). It is as though the one root carries two dimensions: preserving the individual from slipping, and preserving the community from division. Thus it becomes clear that the Muslim is called to fortify himself and to fortify his society, and that what protects the first protects the second: holding to revelation.

Among the subtle points is that Joseph, who sought protection from indecency, is the same who gathered his scattered family at the end of the sūrah, as though the Qurʾan were linking personal virtue with its bearer's ability to mend what lies between people.

### The Threat of Prison: When Steadfastness Becomes Costly

After verse thirty-two came an open threat: ﴿وَلَئِن لَّمْ يَفْعَلْ مَا آمُرُهُ لَيُسْجَنَنَّ وَلَيَكُونًا مِّنَ الصَّاغِرِينَ﴾ ("And if he does not do what I command him, he will surely be imprisoned and will be among the humbled"). The woman declares before the women, without shame, that she will punish him if he does not comply. Here it appears that istiʿṣām is not a single moment but a stance tested with every renewed insistence. Joseph bore the insistence, then the threat, then the prison. This establishes that steadfastness requires long breath, and that one who has stood firm once may be asked to stand firm many times.

### The Contemporary Practical Dimension

The forms of temptation change in this age, but its structure remains: a summons in seclusion, an enticement with some benefit, and a threat if one refuses. In workplaces especially, a Muslim may find himself facing blackmail from those in authority, and in the digital space facing open seclusions without doors. The istiʿṣām that the Qurʾan taught offers a method: begin with the remembrance of God and seeking refuge in Him at the hour of temptation; take the tangible means by getting up, leaving the place, deleting and blocking; then ask God to turn it away from you; and be ready to bear the worldly cost if it is required.

And for educators: we ought to teach the young that istiʿṣām is neither withdrawal nor a complex, but strength and prudence. Joseph was not weak and so hid himself; he was in the place where temptation was at its strongest and chose obedience. Nor does this contradict acknowledging weakness before God, for they are two faces of one coin, which is servanthood.

### Testimony after Steadfastness: The Effect of Istiʿṣām in the Emergence of Truth

Joseph's istiʿṣām did not remain a secret. When the king gathered the women he asked them: ﴿مَا خَطْبُكُنَّ إِذْ رَاوَدتُّنَّ يُوسُفَ عَن نَّفْسِهِ ۚ قُلْنَ حَاشَ لِلَّهِ مَا عَلِمْنَا عَلَيْهِ مِن سُوءٍ﴾ ("What was your affair when you sought to seduce Joseph? They said: God forbid! We know of no evil in him") (Yūsuf 12:51); then the ʿAzīz's wife said: ﴿الْآنَ حَصْحَصَ الْحَقُّ أَنَا رَاوَدتُّهُ عَن نَّفْسِهِ وَإِنَّهُ لَمِنَ الصَّادِقِينَ﴾ ("Now the truth has come to light. It was I who sought to seduce him, and he is truly among the truthful") (Yūsuf 12:51). Steadfastness in secret turned into open testimony after years. This teaches that whoever seeks protection for God's sake, God preserves his reputation for him, even if it is delayed, and that the outcome belongs to the God-fearing. It does not mean that a person should do good in order for his reputation to be raised, but that he should rest assured that God does not let the reward of those who do good be lost.

### Marks of True Istiʿṣām

Marks can be drawn from the Josephite model. True istiʿṣām begins with magnifying God in the heart at the moment of the summons; it turns at once to cutting off the cause rather than bargaining with it; it does not despise the one who tempts, but fears for itself; it is accompanied by prayer and neediness; and it accepts its worldly price willingly. The pretended istiʿṣām, by contrast, you see lingering at doors, boasting of its strength, and walking toward places of doubt and then saying that it is fortified.

### Conclusion

A single word, «fa-staʿṣama», condensed the scene of steadfastness: remembrance of God, a proof from Him, a race to the door, a prayer for deliverance, and the preference of prison over sin. In the distinction between the ʿiṣmah that God grants and the istiʿṣām that the servant exerts lies a lesson for every Muslim: seek protection, exert its means, and do not feel secure about yourself.
