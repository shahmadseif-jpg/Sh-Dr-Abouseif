---
title: "Dhikr in the Qur'an: A Presence Set Against Forgetting, Widening Until It Becomes a Name for Revelation"
locale: "en"

---

*A study of a root that joins the heart's calling to mind, the tongue's speech, the sending down of the Book and the lasting of honour, and of its bearing on tranquillity and trembling. The Qur'an places dhikr against forgetting, and the study follows how the word widens from the remembrance of the heart to the name of the revelation itself.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

Moses, peace be upon him, put to his Lord the question of a servant weighed down by blessing who wished to give it its due: «يا رب، كيف أشكرك؟» "My Lord, how am I to thank You?" The answer did not come in the form of some intricate duty, but in two phrases: «تذكرني ولا تنساني، فإذا ذكرتني فقد شكرتني، وإذا نسيتني فقد كفرتني» "Remember Me and do not forget Me; if you remember Me, you have thanked Me, and if you forget Me, you have been ungrateful to Me"[^1]. It is as though everything between gratitude and ingratitude is a single thing swinging between two poles: that God be present in one's awareness, or that He be absent. This is *dhikr* (remembrance) in its Qur'anic origin: not the repeating of words on a heedless tongue, but a presence that has forgetting as its counterpart, as life has death, as we shall see.

### A Root That Fills the Muṣḥaf

The root «ذ ك ر» (dh-k-r) occurs in the Qur'an two hundred and ninety-two times in fourteen forms, according to the Qur'anic Arabic Corpus (corpus.quran.com). Eighteen of these are *dhakar* in the sense of "male" as opposed to "female", an independent root-meaning in the language, which leaves two hundred and seventy-four occurrences for dhikr and *tadhakkur* (remembering). Among them are the plain verb, the intensive *dhakkara*, *tadhakkara*, *iddakara*, and the verbal noun *dhikr* alone in seventy-six places, then *dhikrā*, *tadhkira*, *mudhakkir*, *dhākirūn* and *dhākirāt*. The same page shows that "much" (*kathīran*) is tied to the root dhikr in eight places, among them: ﴿وَاذْكُرُوا اللَّهَ كَثِيرًا لَّعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ﴾ "and remember God often, that you may succeed" (8:45), the verse about meeting the enemy[^2]. I add nothing to this count, for I have not surveyed the other acts of worship in order to compare.

In the context of this verse Qatāda said, in al-Ṭabarī's narration: "God made His remembrance obligatory at the moment when you are most occupied, in the clash of swords"[^3]. That is, the command to remember meets the believers in their most crowded hours, not only in their clearest hours of leisure. At the words of the Most High ﴿يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اذْكُرُوا اللَّهَ ذِكْرًا كَثِيرًا﴾ "O you who believe, remember God with abundant remembrance" (33:41), al-Ṭabarī explained: "Remember God with your hearts, your tongues and your limbs", and he narrated from Ibn ʿAbbās: "God did not impose any obligation on His servants without setting a known limit to it, and then excusing them in a state of excuse, except dhikr: for God set no limit at which it ends, and excused no one for leaving it unless he were overcome in his mind"[^4]. This is the saying of Ibn ʿAbbās as al-Ṭabarī relates it; I pass it on as it stands and build no general ruling upon it.

### A Single Opposite: Forgetting

Ibn Fāris traces this chapter back to two roots, and among what he says of the second: "I remembered the thing, the opposite of my having forgotten it. Then dhikr by the tongue was carried over onto it"[^5]. So forgetting is the opposite, and the tongue is a branch carried over onto the original meaning. And this is exactly what we see in the Qur'an: ﴿وَاذْكُر رَّبَّكَ إِذَا نَسِيتَ﴾ "and remember your Lord when you forget" (18:24), and then the widening of the opposite from forgetting to heedlessness in His words: ﴿وَلَا تَكُن مِّنَ الْغَافِلِينَ﴾ "and do not be among the heedless" (7:205).

As for al-Rāghib, he gives us a precise measure: "Preservation (ḥifẓ) is said with regard to retaining a thing, whereas dhikr is said with regard to calling it to mind." What is preserved is stored away; what is remembered is present. Then he divides: "Dhikr is of two kinds: dhikr by the heart and dhikr by the tongue. Each of them is of two types: dhikr after forgetting, and dhikr not after forgetting but from continued preservation." He cites the verse ﴿فَاذْكُرُوا اللَّهَ كَذِكْرِكُمْ آبَاءَكُمْ أَوْ أَشَدَّ ذِكْرًا﴾ "remember God as you remember your forefathers, or with even greater remembrance" (2:200) as an example of dhikr by heart and tongue together[^6]. And I understand from this, and this is my own reading, not al-Rāghib's saying, that the comparison with the remembrance of forefathers presupposes a remembrance that occupies heart and mouth together, and then something stronger is asked of it; were dhikr a bare utterance, the comparison would not hold.

### One Word, Many Faces

And the most wondrous thing about this root is that the very same word came as a name for revelation: ﴿إِنَّا نَحْنُ نَزَّلْنَا الذِّكْرَ وَإِنَّا لَهُ لَحَافِظُونَ﴾ "It is We who sent down the Reminder, and it is We who will preserve it" (15:9). Al-Ṭabarī said: "It is the Qur'an", and that it is guarded "from having falsehood added to it that is not of it, or having subtracted from it what is of it". Al-Zamakhsharī saw in "It is We" a reply to their denial and mockery when they said: "O you upon whom the Reminder has been sent down", so it was stressed that He is the one who sent it down, beyond doubt. Al-Qurṭubī reported from Qatāda and Thābit al-Bunānī that God preserved it from the additions and subtractions of the devils, unlike what the People of the Book were charged to preserve: ﴿بِمَا اسْتُحْفِظُوا﴾ "with what they were entrusted to preserve", and they altered it (5:44)[^7]. In naming His Book dhikr, God has joined in a single name the meaning of sending down and the meaning of preservation: that by which people are reminded, and that which stays present and is not forgotten.

Then the meaning widens to honour: ﴿وَإِنَّهُ لَذِكْرٌ لَّكَ وَلِقَوْمِكَ ۖ وَسَوْفَ تُسْأَلُونَ﴾ "And indeed it is a reminder for you and for your people, and you will be questioned" (43:44). Al-Rāghib says: "that is, an honour for you and for your people." Al-Ṭabarī said the same on the authority of Ibn ʿAbbās: "The Qur'an is an honour for you", and so did al-Zamakhsharī; Ibn Kathīr relates it from Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, Qatāda, al-Suddī and Ibn Zayd, and says that Ibn Jarīr preferred it. Ibn Kathīr mentioned another view: that it is an honour for them in that it was sent down in their language, "so they ought to be the people who most uphold it". Al-Zamakhsharī's wording is explicit: "and you will be questioned about it on the Day of Resurrection, about your upholding its right, your reverence for it, and your gratitude", and Ibn ʿĀshūr holds that this religion earns the Prophet and his people "a good name among the nations"[^8]. So dhikr here is a good reputation, but a reputation bound to a question: an honour that is followed by an accounting. Perhaps this is why the Qur'an places "you will be questioned" beside "a reminder for you", so that honour does not turn into boasting.

These three faces, calling to mind, revelation and honour, seem to me to come from a single thread, and this is my own understanding, not the saying of anyone I have cited: that dhikr is what remains present in existence. It is present in the heart and so is remembered; it is sent down in a book and so is preserved; it is made lasting in reputation and so is honoured.

As for His words ﴿وَاذْكُر رَّبَّكَ إِذَا نَسِيتَ﴾ "and remember your Lord when you forget" (18:24), the people of interpretation differed over it. Al-Ṭabarī held that it is a lesson in right conduct (taʾdīb) for the Prophet ﷺ not to speak with certainty of what will be unless he joins it to God's will, and then he mentioned views, among them Ibn ʿAbbās on making the exception even after a year, and ʿIkrima's: "Remember your Lord when you disobey", and he himself preferred the meaning: "and remember your Lord when you have left off remembering Him, because one of the meanings of forgetting in the speech of the Arabs is leaving". Ibn Kathīr applied it to the one who forgot to make the exception, so that he makes it when he remembers, which is the view of Abū l-ʿĀliya and al-Ḥasan, and he made clear that what Ibn ʿAbbās meant is that one who forgot may make the exception even after a year. As for al-Qurṭubī, he related from Ibn ʿAṭiyya that the verse "is not about oaths; it is about the practice of making the exception outside an oath"[^9]. These three views, forgetting as leaving, forgetting the exception, and forgetting as disobedience, all show that for the exegetes dhikr is the path to repairing a lapse whenever it occurs.

### "So Remember Me, and I Will Remember You"

The matter reaches its summit in the verse of al-Baqara: ﴿فَاذْكُرُونِي أَذْكُرْكُمْ وَاشْكُرُوا لِي وَلَا تَكْفُرُونِ﴾ "So remember Me, and I will remember you; and give thanks to Me, and do not be ungrateful to Me" (2:152). The verse is built on reciprocity: a command, and then an answer tied to the very same act. The expressions differ in specifying what is remembered with: al-Ṭabarī makes dhikr obedience: "So remember Me, O believers, by obeying Me in what I command you and in what I forbid you, and I will remember you with My mercy to you and My forgiveness of you", and he narrated from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr: "Remember Me by obeying Me, and I will remember you by forgiving you". Ibn Kathīr brought in what we heard at the opening of this article, Moses's answer, and reported from al-Ḥasan, Abū l-ʿĀliya, al-Suddī and al-Rabīʿ: "God remembers the one who remembers Him, increases the one who gives thanks to Him, and punishes the one who is ungrateful to Him". And lest dhikr be understood as a word that shields the disobedient, Ibn Kathīr gave the saying of Ibn ʿUmar about one who remembers God while disobeying Him: "When this one remembers God, God remembers him with His curse"[^10].

Between these two understandings, dhikr as obedience and dhikr by the tongue together with obedience, the verse stands. They are not separable in my view: a tongue that conduct does not bear out remains a shadow, and conduct without the heart's remembrance becomes habit. And in the hadith qudsi there is something that opens the other meaning, namely God's remembering His servant: «يقول الله تعالى: أنا عند ظن عبدي بي، وأنا معه إذا ذكرني، فإن ذكرني في نفسه ذكرته في نفسي، وإن ذكرني في ملأ ذكرته في ملأ خير منهم…» "God the Most High says: I am as My servant thinks of Me, and I am with him when he remembers Me. If he remembers Me within himself, I remember him within Myself; and if he remembers Me in an assembly, I remember him in an assembly better than theirs…"[^11]. And I read in it, as I see it, what we saw in al-Rāghib: "within himself" is the dhikr of the heart, and "in an assembly" is a dhikr that people hear.

Al-Rāghib has an observation on the difference between ﴿فَاذْكُرُونِي﴾ "So remember Me" (2:152) and ﴿اذْكُرُوا نِعْمَتِيَ﴾ "Remember My favour" (2:40): he relates from some scholars that the first is addressed to the Companions of the Prophet ﷺ, who had acquired a surplus of strength in knowing Him, the Most High, so they were commanded to remember Him without an intermediary, and the second is addressed to the Children of Israel, who knew God only through His bounties[^6]. As though dhikr had degrees: the lowest is that you remember the favour and so are guided to the One who bestows it, and the highest is that you remember the Bestower Himself.

### Stillness and Trembling: Tranquillity and Wajal

Then comes the verse people know by heart: ﴿الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَتَطْمَئِنُّ قُلُوبُهُم بِذِكْرِ اللَّهِ ۗ أَلَا بِذِكْرِ اللَّهِ تَطْمَئِنُّ الْقُلُوبُ﴾ "those who believe, and whose hearts find tranquillity in the remembrance of God. Surely, in the remembrance of God do hearts find tranquillity" (13:28). Al-Ṭabarī said: "their hearts grow still and are comforted by the remembrance of God", and Ibn Kathīr said: "they are set at ease, incline toward God's side, grow still at His mention, and are content with Him as Lord and Helper". But al-Qurṭubī sets out their disagreement over what is meant by "the remembrance of God" here: His oneness, or their tongues, or the Qur'an as Mujāhid, Qatāda and others said, or His command as Sufyān ibn ʿUyayna said, or His promise as Muqātil said, or "swearing by His name, or mentioning His grace and favour", from Ibn ʿAbbās[^12]. This very disagreement shows that dhikr is not a single formula confined to a glorification or the like, but a name for whatever brings God to presence in the heart.

Yet al-Rāzī raised a difficulty that cannot be ignored: "Has the Most High not said in Sūrat al-Anfāl: 'The believers are only those whose hearts tremble with awe when God is mentioned'? And *wajal* (trembling) is the opposite of *iṭmiʾnān* (tranquillity), so how does He describe them here as tranquil?" Among his answers: "Trembling comes with the mention of punishment, and tranquillity with the mention of reward"[^13]. So the one heart shivers when it calls to mind the majesty of the One it remembers, and grows still when it calls to mind His mercy and His promise. And I see, and this is from my own understanding and not from transmission, that the two do not merely follow one another but come together: the shiver of reverence that is never apart from the calm of safety, like a child in the lap of a majestic father. The life of faith moves between these two: whoever loses trembling finds his sense of safety turned into delusion, and whoever loses tranquillity finds his fear turned into despair.

### And the Remembrance of God Is Greater

Then comes the verse of al-ʿAnkabūt: ﴿وَلَذِكْرُ اللَّهِ أَكْبَرُ﴾ "and the remembrance of God is greater" (29:45), and over it there is an obvious ancient disagreement. Al-Ṭabarī relates that some said the meaning is that God's remembrance of you is better than your remembrance of Him, and that Ibn ʿAbbās said to ʿAbd Allāh ibn Rabīʿa, when the latter mentioned glorification, praise and *takbīr* in prayer and the recitation of the Qur'an: "You have said something strange, and it is not so. He means only this: God's remembrance of you, in what He has commanded or forbidden, when you remember Him, is greater than your remembrance of Him." Mujāhid and others said likewise, according to Ibn Kathīr, and al-Rāghib interpreted it so: "God's remembrance of His servant is greater than the servant's remembrance of Him, and that is an urging to remember Him abundantly." As for Ibn Kathīr, in his own context, he held that prayer comprises the remembrance of God, "and that is the greatest thing sought"[^14]. So the question the verse opens is: is the greater our remembrance or His? And Ibn ʿAbbās's answer overturns the scale: however far our remembrance reaches, His remembrance of us is greater, and perhaps in this there is something that turns the one who remembers away from pride in his remembrance.

### In Every Condition

And one verse describes the people of understanding: ﴿الَّذِينَ يَذْكُرُونَ اللَّهَ قِيَامًا وَقُعُودًا وَعَلَىٰ جُنُوبِهِمْ﴾ "those who remember God standing, sitting, and on their sides" (3:191). Qatāda said, in al-Ṭabarī's narration: "These are all your states, O son of Adam, so remember Him while you lie on your side, as an ease and a lightening from God", and Ibn Jurayj took it to mean dhikr in prayer and otherwise, and the reciting of the Qur'an[^15]. The verse sets dhikr beside reflection: they remember, and they reflect on the creation of the heavens and the earth. So dhikr is not a substitute for the mind, but its nourishment.

And in al-Aʿrāf: ﴿وَدُونَ الْجَهْرِ مِنَ الْقَوْلِ﴾ "and without raising the voice" (7:205) is a command to a hidden remembrance, with humility and fear. Here there is a disagreement over the general and the restricted: al-Ṭabarī restricts it to taking admonition from the Qur'an and calling the Hereafter (al-maʿād) to mind when hearing it, while Ibn Kathīr extends it to the beginning of the day and its end, and mentions that the verse is Meccan, and that this was before the five prayers were made obligatory on the night of the Isrāʾ[^16].

### From the Hadith: The Living and the Dead

And the loveliest thing in the Sunna is the simile reported by al-Bukhārī: «مثل الذي يذكر ربه والذي لا يذكر مثل الحي والميت» "The likeness of the one who remembers his Lord and the one who does not is the likeness of the living and the dead"[^17]. Whoever is deprived of dhikr is deprived of the pulse of life, even if he moves. And in Muslim the Prophet ﷺ said, on a road: «سيروا، هذا جُمدان، سبق المفرِّدون» "Go on, this is Jumdān; the *mufarridūn* have taken the lead", and when they asked about them he said: «الذاكرون الله كثيرًا والذاكرات» "The men who remember God often, and the women who remember Him"[^18]. And as though he were saying, as I understand it, that the race on this road is not decided by speed but by presence. I add nothing to the text of the hadith by way of describing the location of Jumdān or the state of the riders.

The relation between dhikr and forgetting shows in its simplest form in the hadith of Anas as Ibn Kathīr relates it: «من نسي صلاة فليصل إذا ذكرها، لا كفارة لها إلا ذلك» "Whoever forgets a prayer, let him pray it when he remembers it; there is no expiation for it other than that", and in both narrations it is followed by the words of the Most High: ﴿وَأَقِمِ الصَّلَاةَ لِذِكْرِي﴾ "and establish prayer for My remembrance" (20:14), which in al-Bukhārī's narration is the saying of Hammām, and in Muslim's the saying of Qatāda[^19]. On the meaning of the verse, al-Ṭabarī said: "Establish the prayer for Me, for when you establish it you remember Me", and Ibn Kathīr mentioned that the meaning is: pray so that you remember Me, and it was also said: pray when you remember Me. So prayer is dhikr, and dhikr brings a person back to prayer. And so it is that the opposite of forgetting is a return to duty.

### Scenes of Those Who Remember and Those Who Forget

In the Qur'an there are scenes that set remembrance and forgetting face to face. Joseph says to his companion who will be saved: ﴿اذْكُرْنِي عِندَ رَبِّكَ﴾ "Mention me to your lord", and then the verse says: ﴿فَأَنسَاهُ الشَّيْطَانُ ذِكْرَ رَبِّهِ﴾ "but Satan made him forget to mention it to his lord" (12:42), then the companion remembered after a time: ﴿وَادَّكَرَ بَعْدَ أُمَّةٍ﴾ "and remembered after a period" (12:45). A forgetting the verse ascribes to Satan, and a remembering that came only after a long while. And in the scene of the young man and the fish: ﴿وَمَا أَنسَانِيهُ إِلَّا الشَّيْطَانُ أَنْ أَذْكُرَهُ﴾ "and none made me forget it except Satan, that I should mention it" (18:63), which al-Rāghib classed under "dhikr after forgetting". The two passages together ascribe forgetting to Satan.

And at the opening of the story of Zachariah, peace be upon him: ﴿ذِكْرُ رَحْمَتِ رَبِّكَ عَبْدَهُ زَكَرِيَّا﴾ "A mention of your Lord's mercy to His servant Zachariah" (19:2), a mention of God's mercy to a servant who called upon his Lord. And it comes to my mind that a single meaning runs through these scenes: a human being whose memory betrays him, and a Book that preserves what is forgotten, so what befits the servant is to hold fast to what never goes absent.

### What Changes in Us?

If we want a conclusion, I would say: dhikr in the Qur'an is not merely a movement of the tongue, but a presence guarded by its opposite, forgetting. It widens until it names the revelation that is sent down and preserved, and the honour that is granted and for which its holder is questioned. The reports we have passed through confirm that dhikr has three doors: the heart, the tongue, and the limbs through obedience carried out. And dhikr has two fruits: a tranquillity by which the heart grows still, and a trembling that protects it from delusion. And above all of that, God's remembrance of His servant is greater.

As for application: the educator may teach the young that dhikr is not only a number to be counted. Let us tie the morning and evening remembrances to the conduct that follows them, and remind them that the prayer itself is "for My remembrance". Let us encourage them to remember God in their busiest times, as we saw in the verse of fighting, and not in their leisure alone. The preacher should beware of separating dhikr from obedience, for we saw the saying of Ibn ʿUmar about the disobedient one who remembers; and beware of settling for obedience without presence, for we saw the saying of al-Rāghib about dhikr from "continued preservation". And in our homes it is enough, in the crowd of the day, to recall what was said to Moses: "Remember Me and do not forget Me". Perhaps whoever enters his day with these words will know his way to his heart.

O God, do not make us among those who were reminded and did not remember, nor among those whom heedlessness overcame so that they did not awake. Make us among those whose hearts tremble when God is mentioned, and whose spirits then grow still at Your remembrance. And when we forget, remind us; and when we remember You, remember us in the highest assembly with something better than we remembered You with: ﴿فَاذْكُرُونِي أَذْكُرْكُمْ﴾ "So remember Me, and I will remember you".
