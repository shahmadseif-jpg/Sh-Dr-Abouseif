*For its weightiest covenants the Qurʾan chooses a word drawn not from speech but from ropes and fetters: a mīthāq is a promise made fast, which may be tied freely in trust or imposed as a chain of judgment.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

At the foot of Mount Ṭūr the Children of Israel stand beneath a mountain raised above their heads like a canopy: an immense rock hanging in the air, with nothing between it and their heads but God's command that it remain suspended. In that moment, with the mountain standing over them like a roof about to fall, the covenant is taken from them:

﴿وَإِذْ أَخَذْنَا مِيثَاقَكُمْ وَرَفَعْنَا فَوْقَكُمُ الطُّورَ﴾ (2:63)

*“And when We took your covenant and raised the mount above you.”*

This is no covenant signed with a pen, nor a promise spoken by the tongue alone, but a commitment taken beneath a palpable weight, as though the mountain itself were a material witness drawing the covenant tight to the earth.

That weight and that tightening are exactly what the Qurʾan's chosen word for this covenant carries: *al-mīthāq*, from a root that originally denotes neither speech nor promise but binding and tangible, physical fastening: the rope pulled taut, the fetter made fast. When the Qurʾan wishes to describe a covenant that will not bear breaking, it borrows its word not from the field of speech but from the field of ropes and knots.

### Delimiting the Word and Counting Its Occurrences

The root w-th-q occurs in the Qurʾan thirty-four times, in six forms. It appears once as the verb *wāthaqa* (5:7); once as the verb *yūthiqu*, in the context of punishment in the Hereafter (89:26); three times as the noun *mawthiq* in the story of Joseph (12:66, 80); twenty-five times as the noun *mīthāq*, by far the most frequent form of the root; twice as the noun *wathāq*, meaning a physical fetter (47:4, 89:26); and twice as the adjective *al-wuthqā*, meaning the firmest and most tightly fastened (2:256, 31:22).[^2]

The distribution is striking in itself. Twenty-five of the thirty-four, more than two-thirds of the root's occurrences, turn toward the abstract sense of “a ratified covenant,” while the remaining forms keep their direct, tangible sense: fetter, binding, handhold. This means that when the Qurʾan used *al-mīthāq* it was not summoning a purely abstract word, but bringing to mind along with it, however faintly, the image of the taut rope.

### The Linguistic Root: From Rope to Covenant

In the origin of the language, *wathiqa* denotes making fast and drawing tight. *Awthaqtu al-shayʾ*, “I made the thing fast,” is said when one has tied it with a firm knot that does not come undone, and *al-wathāq* is the fetter or rope with which a captive or a beast is bound. From it comes *al-wuthqā*, the superlative for the most tightly fastened bond, as in “the firmest handhold” (*al-ʿurwah al-wuthqā*), the handhold that does not break, a Qurʾanic metaphor for certainty that does not waver:

﴿فَقَدِ اسْتَمْسَكَ بِالْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا﴾ (2:256)

*“Then he has grasped the firmest handhold, which will never break.”*

When the root passed from its tangible sense (drawing a rope tight) to its abstract sense (ratifying a covenant), it did not lose its original charge but carried it along. A ratified covenant is one made fast as a rope is made fast, not one in which speech alone sufficed. This sets *mīthāq* apart from *ʿahd*, the more general word for promise and commitment in the Qurʾan, by a fine distinction that shows clearly when the two words meet in a single verse, as we shall see.

### The Central Structure: The *ʿAhd* Is the Content, and the *Mīthāq* Is Its Binding

The Qurʾan joins *ʿahd* and *mīthāq* in more than one place, in a manner that discloses the relation between them:

﴿الَّذِينَ يَنْقُضُونَ عَهْدَ اللَّهِ مِنْ بَعْدِ مِيثَاقِهِ﴾ (2:27)

*“Those who break God's covenant after its ratification.”*

The same pairing occurs at 13:20–25. The *ʿahd* here is the commitment itself, its substance and content, while its *mīthāq* is what makes that commitment fast and binds it to the one who bears it: the act of ratification that makes the breaking of the covenant the cutting of a binding and not of a passing word. The *ʿahd* is what is said; the *mīthāq* is what is made fast.

This difference also explains why one particular verb recurs with *mīthāq* more than any other: *akhadha*, “to take.” The Qurʾan says “We took your *mīthāq*,” not “We gave you a *mīthāq*” nor “We promised you,” for God is the One who takes, and the servant is the one from whom the covenant is taken and upon whom it is made fast. This verb recurs with the Children of Israel (2:63, 83, 84, 93; 5:12), with the prophets (3:81; 33:7) and with the Christians (5:14). In every case the initiative lies with God alone, and the servant is the party whose pledge is made fast, not the one who grants it.

### A Striking Pattern: The Solemn Covenant in Three Distant Places

Among the strangest things that tracing the word discloses is that the description *mīthāqan ghalīẓan*, “a solemn covenant,” that is, a covenant of extreme firmness and weight, occurs in the Qurʾan in only three places, some apparently as far from one another as can be. The first is the covenant God took from the prophets:

﴿وَأَخَذْنَا مِنْهُمْ مِيثَاقًا غَلِيظًا﴾ (33:7)

*“And We took from them a solemn covenant.”*

The second is the covenant taken from the Children of Israel when the Mount was raised above them: ﴿وَأَخَذْنَا مِنْهُم مِّيثَاقًا غَلِيظًا﴾ (4:154). The third is the marriage bond between spouses:

﴿وَأَخَذْنَ مِنْكُمْ مِيثَاقًا غَلِيظًا﴾ (4:21)

*“And they have taken from you a solemn covenant.”*

The Qurʾan describes the marital bond with the very weight and firmness with which it describes the covenant of prophethood, setting the greatest religious covenant beside the nearest and most ordinary human one. This closeness is no linguistic coincidence. It signals that marriage, in the Qurʾan's scale, is not an administrative contract to be dissolved lightly, but a covenant that bears the same gravity and sanctity as the covenant of the prophets themselves.

### Another Pattern: Jacob's Pledge to His Sons

Besides *mīthāq*, the root has another form, *mawthiq*, which occurs only three times in the whole Qurʾan, all in the story of Joseph. When Joseph's brothers ask their father to send their younger brother with them, Jacob refuses until he has taken from them a firm pledge:

﴿قَالَ لَنْ أُرْسِلَهُ مَعَكُمْ حَتَّىٰ تُؤْتُونِ مَوْثِقًا مِنَ اللَّهِ لَتَأْتُنَّنِي بِهِ﴾ (12:66)

*“He said: I will never send him with you until you give me a pledge before God that you will bring him back to me.”*

This is an intimate family scene, far from Mount Ṭūr and the awe of the Children of Israel, yet it calls up the very same word from the same root. Even between a father and his sons, when anxiety is at its height, a passing promise does not suffice; there must be “a pledge before God” by which the request is made fast. And when the sons are later put to the test over this pledge (12:80), it becomes clear that the word always carries with it a commitment for which one is held to account, and not speech forgotten once the occasion has passed.

At the exact opposite pole, the Qurʾan uses the same root for a binding in which there is neither choice nor covenant, but punishment:

﴿وَلَا يُوثِقُ وَثَاقَهُ أَحَدٌ﴾ (89:26)

*“And none will bind as He binds.”*

This describes the torment of the one who transgressed and tyrannized, on the Day of Resurrection. Here the two opposing poles of a single root are laid bare. There is a binding contracted freely between two consenting parties, which becomes a noble covenant to be kept; and there is a binding imposed by force on one who refused the first covenant, which becomes a fetter of condemnation. The same rope is either drawn taut with the heart's consent, and so becomes a bond of trust, or drawn taut against its bearer's will, and so becomes a chain of indictment.

### A Methodological Note: The First Covenant Is Not Called by This Name in the Qurʾan

It is common in the language of daʿwah to call the scene mentioned in the following verse “the primordial covenant” or “the covenant of the *fiṭrah*”:

﴿وَإِذْ أَخَذَ رَبُّكَ مِنْ بَنِي آدَمَ مِنْ ظُهُورِهِمْ ذُرِّيَّتَهُمْ وَأَشْهَدَهُمْ عَلَىٰ أَنْفُسِهِمْ أَلَسْتُ بِرَبِّكُمْ قَالُوا بَلَىٰ﴾ (7:172)

*“And when your Lord took from the children of Adam, from their loins, their descendants and made them testify concerning themselves: ‘Am I not your Lord?’ They said: ‘Yes.’”*

But a check of the verse shows that the Qurʾan itself does not use the word *mīthāq* in it at all, neither in this verse nor in any other place in Sūrat al-Aʿrāf that describes this very scene. The common name comes from the Prophetic Sunnah, not from the text of the verse. Ibn ʿAbbās reported from the Prophet ﷺ that he said:

«إنّ الله أخذ الميثاق من ظهر آدم بنعمانَ يومَ عرفة، فأخرج من صلبه كل ذرّيةٍ ذرأها فنثرهم بين يديه…»

*“God took the covenant from the loins of Adam at Naʿmān, on the day of ʿArafah, and brought forth from his back every offspring He had created, and scattered them before Him…”*[^1]

This hadith has been graded sound by more than one scholar. It is the Sunnah, then, that gave this scene the name *mīthāq*, borrowing an authentic Qurʾanic word used elsewhere, and not the verse itself that bears this name. The distinction is a fine methodological one and worth noting: a strong thematic link joins the two scenes, but it is not a direct verbal witness from the text of 7:172 itself.

### The Prophetic Witness

The hadith of Ibn ʿAbbās just cited, besides indicating the naming of the scene of the primordial offspring (*dharr*), carries a further significance that serves the central structure of this article. The wording “brought forth from his back every offspring He had created and scattered them before Him” depicts the covenant as a tangible, physical act (a bringing forth, a scattering, a face-to-face address) and not as a mere abstract divine decree. This accords with what was drawn from the linguistic root itself: even in its deepest unseen manifestations, the *mīthāq* is portrayed in the language of body and substance, not in the language of pure abstraction.

### A Reading in Light of the Higher Objectives (*Maqāṣid*)

From the twenty-five occurrences of *mīthāq* it may be concluded that the Qurʾan uses it especially in settings where breaking is to be feared: the covenant of the Children of Israel, which they did indeed break:

﴿فَبِمَا نَقْضِهِمْ مِيثَاقَهُمْ﴾ (4:155; 5:13)

*“So for their breaking of their covenant…”*

and the covenant of the Christians:

﴿وَمِنَ الَّذِينَ قَالُوا إِنَّا نَصَارَىٰ أَخَذْنَا مِيثَاقَهُمْ﴾ (5:14)

*“And from those who say, ‘We are Christians,’ We took their covenant.”*

and the covenant of marriage, which can come undone through divorce. It is as if the choice of this particular word, with its weight of rope and its suggestion of firm fastening, were an implicit warning that attends every place where it occurs: you stand before a binding, not a passing word, and to break it is not merely to fail in a promise but to sever a handhold that was meant never to break.

This reading meets another observation about how the word is distributed among those addressed. In the Qurʾan the *mīthāq* is taken only from one presumed capable of fulfilling it and of understanding it (the Children of Israel, the Christians, the prophets, the spouses), and is never used of a covenant imposed on one who has no will. Even in the scene of the primordial offspring that the Sunnah named *mīthāq*, the answer came as a free acknowledgment, “They said: Yes,” and not under compulsion. The weight of the word, though borrowed from the field of fetter and binding, thus does not contradict choice but rests upon it: a *mīthāq* is made fast only upon one who first chose to have it made fast upon him, by his acknowledgment or consent.

### The Contemporary Application

In an age abounding in quick promises, such as pledges made in a message, agreements concluded with a click, and contracts easy to withdraw from through a small clause in the margin, the word *mīthāq* reminds us that there are commitments to be treated with the weight of the mountain raised over the Children of Israel, not with the lightness of a passing word. Marriage, which the Qurʾan called a solemn covenant, is a living example: a decision sometimes taken with the speed of a word spoken, though in the scale of the Qurʾanic text it deserves a fastening equal to that of the greatest covenants. So too with professional and social covenants. Whoever makes an agreement with a partner, an employee or a neighbor would do well to recall that a word, once given, should be made fast in the heart as a rope is made fast, and not left hanging with the lightness of air.

The scene of Jacob with his sons adds another practical dimension: seeking assurance is not a sign of ill opinion, but a legitimate prudence when the matter is grave. Jacob, though aware of the apparent sincerity of his sons' intentions, did not rest content with their bare promise, having known from them in the earlier story of Joseph what he had known. He sought a pledge in which God is called as witness. This is a model for anyone today who wavers between blind trust and absolute doubt. The heart may remain trusting toward its family and loved ones, and yet seek for their great commitments a form that is witnessed and documented, without any diminishing of good opinion.

### Conclusion

For the greatest covenants it mentions (the covenant of the Children of Israel beneath the Ṭūr, the covenant of the prophets and the covenant of marriage) the Qurʾan chooses a word whose root knows no abstraction: a rope drawn taut, a fetter made fast, a handhold that does not break. And when it joins *ʿahd* and its *mīthāq* in a single verse, it reminds us that every real commitment needs two things: a word that is spoken, and a binding made fast in the heart before it is on the tongue. And God, the Exalted, knows best; He is the Guardian of success.
