*Qurʾanic tawakkul is the entrusting of one's affair to an all-sufficient Agent after every available means has been taken, never an excuse for idleness; it joins effort and consultation to a heart at rest in God.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

When the people of Abraham kindled the fire and cast him into it, he had no army, no stratagem, no visible way of escape. Ibn ʿAbbās (may God be pleased with them both) relates, in a report recorded by al-Bukhārī in his *Ṣaḥīḥ*, that the last words Abraham (peace be upon him) spoke as he was thrown into the fire were: «حَسْبِيَ اللَّهُ وَنِعْمَ الْوَكِيلُ»,[^1] "God is enough for me, and how excellent a Trustee (*wakīl*) He is." A single phrase chosen by the Intimate Friend of the All-Merciful in the most desperate moment a human being has known: not "I shall be saved," nor "someone will rescue me," but "God is my Trustee." It is as though he called his Lord to witness that he had delegated his whole affair to Him, so that, whether the fire consumed him or spared him, the Trustee had taken the matter in hand.

### The Root and Its Occurrences

The root w-k-l occurs in the Qurʾan seventy times, in four forms. The first is the intensive verb *wukkila*, "was made responsible for the matter," which occurs twice (6:89 and 32:11, as in the Angel of Death, who has been "charged" (*wukkila*) with taking souls). The second is the verb *tawakkala*, of the pattern *tafaʿʿala*, which occurs forty times; it is the most frequent form and forms the very core of the Qurʾanic theme of tawakkul. The third is the noun *wakīl*, which occurs twenty-four times, sometimes as a description of God (﴿وَكَفَىٰ بِاللَّهِ وَكِيلًا﴾, "God suffices as Trustee") and sometimes in a context denying any such trusteeship to the prophets themselves (﴿وَمَا أَنْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ بِوَكِيلٍ﴾, "You are not a guardian over them"). The fourth is the active participle *mutawakkilūn*, which occurs four times.

### The Linguistic Root: From Commercial Agency to the Agency of the Heart

In ordinary Arabic usage, the *wakīl* is the one to whom a matter is delegated, to carry it out on behalf of its owner. A man appoints another as his agent to sell his goods or manage his property. This does not mean that the owner leaves his property unattended; it means that he trusts the one he has chosen to act for the best, while he himself goes on with his other affairs.

When the Qurʾan uses the verb *tawakkala* (a pattern that commonly signals that the agent makes the act a quality of his own) to describe the relation of the servant to his Lord, it carries this very commercial sense over into the most intimate relationship possible: the servant takes God as Trustee over his entire affair. This does not mean abandoning the means; it means perfect confidence that the One to whom the matter has been entrusted, God alone, is fully sufficient for it.

### The Central Structure: Delegation, Not Passivity

This fine distinction, between delegation and passivity, is the key by which the Qurʾan and the Sunnah together resolve a widespread misunderstanding of tawakkul, one that sometimes reduces it to a neglect of means in reliance on destiny. A man once asked the Prophet ﷺ: «يَا رَسُولَ اللَّهِ، أُرْسِلُ نَاقَتِي وَأَتَوَكَّلُ، أَمْ أَعْقِلُهَا وَأَتَوَكَّلُ؟» "Messenger of God, shall I let my camel go and trust in God, or tie her and trust in God?" The answer was decisive: «بَلِ اعْقِلْهَا وَتَوَكَّلْ»,[^2] "Rather, tie her, and then trust in God."

The Prophet ﷺ did not tell the man to leave his camel untethered in reliance on God. He commanded him to take the evident means (to hobble the animal and secure her) and then to entrust the outcome to God. True tawakkul is therefore made of two elements, neither of which can stand alone: taking the means within reach, and then delegating the result wholly to the One who is better able to order it.

### When Tawakkul Is Mistaken for Tawākul

More than many of its sister concepts, this one has suffered from confusion with a word that resembles it in sound and differs from it in sense: *tawākul*, idle dependence. Tawakkul, as we have seen, is delegation followed by tranquility after the means have been taken. Tawākul is the abandoning of the means altogether, a passive leaning on destiny that serves as a pretext for sloth.

Shaykh al-Islām Ibn Taymiyyah draws the distinction in a precise phrase: "To turn toward the means is a fault against the doctrine of God's oneness, and to erase the means as means is a defect in reason."[^3] The heart may not lean on a means as though it were an equal partner, nor may the mind deny that means exist at all. Rather, a means is taken in its proper place within the ordering of affairs, while the heart clings to the Causer of causes. Imam al-Ghazālī describes the state of tawakkul as "obscure in knowledge, evident in action":[^4] its inner reality is subtle and hard to attain, but its mark on the limbs is plain, a striving that never ceases and a heart that is never anxious.

### ʿUmar and the People of Yemen: Do Not Hold Back from Seeking Provision

Few things embody this difference more eloquently than a report from ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb (may God be pleased with him). He saw a group from Yemen who had abandoned their work and settled in the mosque, pleading tawakkul. He said: "Let none of you hold back from seeking his livelihood and say, 'O God, provide for me,' when he knows that the sky does not rain gold or silver." Then he recited:

﴿فَإِذَا قُضِيَتِ الصَّلَاةُ فَانْتَشِرُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَابْتَغُوا مِنْ فَضْلِ اللَّهِ﴾ (62:10)[^5]

*"And when the prayer is ended, disperse throughout the land and seek the bounty of God."*

ʿUmar thus tied the verse that commands dispersing in the land to seek provision, immediately after the prayer, to the truth that God "provides for people only by means of one another," that is, through striving and dealing, not through sitting still and waiting. Although its setting differs from the story of Abraham in the fire, this report completes it from the other side. Abraham had no means to take against the fire, so he surrendered the whole matter; the seeker of provision does have a means (effort and work), and it is not permitted him to renounce it in the name of a tawakkul not enjoined upon him in this case.

### A Model Linking Tawakkul to Consultation

Among the most delicate occurrences of the root in the Qurʾan is its pairing with consultation in a single verse that describes the method of prophetic leadership:

﴿وَشَاوِرْهُمْ فِي الْأَمْرِ فَإِذَا عَزَمْتَ فَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ﴾ (3:159)

*"And consult them in the matter; then, when you have resolved, put your trust in God."*

The verse does not make tawakkul a substitute for consultation and deliberation, but its sequel: consult first, and once you have resolved upon a decision after consultation, entrust the final outcome to God. In this order (consultation, resolve, tawakkul) is a further confirmation that tawakkul does not excuse one from exhausting the rational and consultative means, but comes after them, to set the heart at rest about what lies beyond human control.

### Another Model: A Denial That Clarifies the Meaning

The Qurʾan's precision in using the word *wakīl* appears also in that it employs it to deny to the prophets themselves a trusteeship they do not hold. When people refuse to believe, the Qurʾan says on the Messenger's tongue:

﴿وَمَا أَنْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ بِوَكِيلٍ﴾ (6:107; likewise 10:108, 39:41, 42:6)

*"You are not a trustee over them."*

The Messenger's task is to convey the message, not to hold the hearts of people in trust and guide them; that is a trusteeship belonging to God alone. The recurrence of the word in a context of negation confirms that the Qurʾan uses *wakīl* in its exact sense: one who assumes full charge of a matter on behalf of another, which, in the matter of guidance and the governing of the cosmos, no one possesses but God.

### A Third Model: A Way Out and Provision Beyond Reckoning

In Sūrat al-Ṭalāq the Qurʾan joins tawakkul to two immediate fruits:

﴿وَمَنْ يَتَّقِ اللَّهَ يَجْعَلْ لَهُ مَخْرَجًا. وَيَرْزُقْهُ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَحْتَسِبُ وَمَنْ يَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ فَهُوَ حَسْبُهُ﴾ (65:2-3)

*"And whoever is mindful of God, He will make a way out for him, and will provide for him from where he does not reckon. And whoever puts his trust in God, He is sufficient for him."*

There is a gentle progression here: taqwā opens the way out, and tawakkul brings provision from a place that had not been counted upon. The word *ḥasbuhu*, "sufficient for him," repeats the very root Abraham uttered in the fire (*ḥasbiya Allāh*), as though the verse confirms, in the language of revelation, what the Intimate Friend of the All-Merciful did in his most extreme hour: whoever takes God as his Trustee, God suffices him in whatever concerns him.

### A Fourth Model: The Same Word Across History

The very words that Abraham spoke in the fire were spoken by the believers after the Battle of Uḥud, when word reached them that Quraysh was massing an army to return and destroy them:

﴿الَّذِينَ قَالَ لَهُمُ النَّاسُ إِنَّ النَّاسَ قَدْ جَمَعُوا لَكُمْ فَاخْشَوْهُمْ فَزَادَهُمْ إِيمَانًا وَقَالُوا حَسْبُنَا اللَّهُ وَنِعْمَ الْوَكِيلُ﴾ (3:173)

*"Those to whom the people said, 'The people have gathered against you, so fear them,' but it only increased them in faith, and they said, 'God is enough for us, and how excellent a Trustee He is.'"*

The prophetic report with which this article began makes clear that this very phrase was one that «قَالَهَا إِبْرَاهِيمُ عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ حِينَ أُلْقِيَ فِي النَّارِ، وَقَالَهَا مُحَمَّدٌ ﷺ»,[^1] "Abraham, peace be upon him, said it when he was thrown into the fire, and Muhammad ﷺ said it" when he was threatened with the gathering of his enemies. The two situations differ entirely in their details, a fire kindled once, an army mustered another time, yet the answer of the trusting heart is one and unchanging among the prophets and their followers: to entrust the matter to the best of Trustees. It is not a mere phrase spoken but a stand taken at the moment of utmost peril, when every visible human resource is gone.

### The Prophetic Witness

Al-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah relate, on the authority of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb (may God be pleased with him), that the Prophet ﷺ said:

«لَوْ أَنَّكُمْ تَتَوَكَّلُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ حَقَّ تَوَكُّلِهِ، لَرَزَقَكُمْ كَمَا يَرْزُقُ الطَّيْرَ: تَغْدُو خِمَاصًا وَتَرُوحُ بِطَانًا»[^6]

*"If you were to rely on God as He ought to be relied upon, He would provide for you as He provides for the birds: they go out in the morning hungry and return in the evening full."*

The birds set out at the beginning of the day with empty bellies and come back at its close, full and satisfied. The Prophet ﷺ did not say, "He would provide for you while you crouch in your nests." He said that the birds *go out* in the morning, that is, they strive and fly in search of their sustenance, and then return, and God has sufficed them. The very simile confirms the central structure: true tawakkul does not dispense with going out and striving, but accompanies it and quiets the heart in the midst of it.

### A Reading in Light of the Higher Objectives

Scholars observe that the Qurʾan makes tawakkul a quality inseparable from faith, not something added to it, when it says: ﴿وَعَلَى اللَّهِ فَتَوَكَّلُوا إِنْ كُنْتُمْ مُؤْمِنِينَ﴾ (5:23), "And put your trust in God, if you are believers." It ties the realization of faith to the realization of tawakkul, and not to inward assent alone. Some hold that the secret of this link is that tawakkul is the practical fruit of the certainty that the whole matter lies in God's hand. Whoever truly believes that there is no disposer but Him must entrust his affairs to Him; otherwise his inward faith would be cut off from his outward conduct.

Hence some of the early generations understood the words ﴿وَعَلَى اللَّهِ فَلْيَتَوَكَّلِ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ﴾, "In God let the believers put their trust" (recurring with the same sense, according to the Arabic text, in six places), not as a command added to faith, but as a spelling out of what faith itself requires in conduct.

It may also be noticed that most of the passages that command tawakkul follow the mention of a trial or confrontation in which the one addressed lacks the full means: battle, or being called a liar, or harm from enemies, or the fear of want in provision. This makes it likely that tawakkul in the Qurʾanic scheme is not a state equally present at all times, but is summoned most intensely at the very boundaries of human helplessness, where what a person can contrive comes to an end, and what lies beyond his power begins.

Because of this standing of tawakkul in the structure of the faith, Ibn al-Qayyim raises it to a lofty rank in *Madārij al-Sālikīn*: "Tawakkul is half of the religion, and the other half is *ināba* (turning back to God), for religion is seeking help and worship; tawakkul is the seeking of help, and ināba is the worship."[^7] On this division, religion is not completed by an act of worship whose outward form is obedience unless it is joined by a tawakkul whose inward essence is seeking help. For the words ﴿إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ وَإِيَّاكَ نَسْتَعِينُ﴾ (1:5), "You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help," the axis of Sūrat al-Fātiḥah, which is recited in every unit of prayer, bring the two halves together: a worship inseparable from seeking help, and a tawakkul inseparable from worship. Ibn al-Qayyim calls the station of tawakkul "the widest and most comprehensive of the stations," because the servant's need to seek his Lord's help attends him in every one of his affairs, and not in the great trials alone.

### Awe, Faith, and Tawakkul: A Single Portrait

If one wishes to see tawakkul as a quality embodied in a composite picture, Sūrat al-Anfāl draws for the true believers three connected features:

﴿إِنَّمَا الْمُؤْمِنُونَ الَّذِينَ إِذَا ذُكِرَ اللَّهُ وَجِلَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ وَإِذَا تُلِيَتْ عَلَيْهِمْ آيَاتُهُ زَادَتْهُمْ إِيمَانًا وَعَلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يَتَوَكَّلُونَ﴾ (8:2)

*"The believers are only those whose hearts tremble when God is mentioned, and when His verses are recited to them, it increases them in faith, and in their Lord they put their trust."*

The heart's trembling at remembrance, the growth of faith on hearing the verses, and trust in God: the three belong together in the portrait of the true believer. Tawakkul is not isolated from the effect that remembrance and the Qurʾan have upon the heart. The two verses that follow complete the picture:

﴿الَّذِينَ يُقِيمُونَ الصَّلَاةَ وَمِمَّا رَزَقْنَاهُمْ يُنْفِقُونَ. أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ حَقًّا﴾ (8:3-4)

*"Those who establish prayer and spend from what We have provided them. Those are the believers in truth."*

The description closes with a prayer established and a giving not withheld, two acts that only one who strives and works can perform, never one who sits and leans. The true man of tawakkul, in this composite portrait, is a heart in reverent awe, a faith that grows, a prayer upheld, and wealth spent: an integrated way of life, not a word spoken in the hour of hardship and forgotten in the hour of ease.

### The Contemporary Practical Dimension

Many confuse tawakkul with tawākul, imagining that neglect of the means (failing to plan for the future, carelessness in one's work, laxity in precaution) is a lofty species of tawakkul. The hadith "tie her, and trust in God" demolishes this notion at its root. A true trustee does not release the one who appointed him from his own duty; he takes upon himself what the principal cannot control, after the principal has done what lies within his power.

In the world of work and projects this offers a practical measure: a person should plan, consult, and take every available means, and then entrust the final outcome to God, without an anxiety that embitters him or a procrastination that cripples him. Here lies the difference between a tranquil heart that labors diligently and is not exhausted by the fear of failure, and a heart that is either idle in the name of tawakkul or fretful in the name of prudence. Both are far from the tawakkul the Qurʾan describes.

The words of Abraham and of the Companions of the Prophet ﷺ, "God is enough for us, and how excellent a Trustee He is," serve as a practical guide for the moments when every resource is exhausted: when a person faces a grave medical diagnosis, or a financial loss he has no means of recovering, or a fateful decision that has passed wholly out of his hands. In just such moments, when no means remains to be taken and no plan left to arrange, tawakkul appears in its clearest form: not a helplessness to act, but a calm that comes after every possible action has been spent.

### Conclusion

From the phrase Abraham spoke in the heart of the fire, to the camel whose owner was bidden to tie her before trusting in God, to the bird that goes out striving and returns provided for, to ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb bidding those who sat idle to disperse in the land, the Qurʾan and the Sunnah draw for tawakkul one unchanging meaning: that a person spend what means he has, and then entrust to God what he does not have, certain that the best Trustee for his affair is the One whom he has made his Trustee, and that the faith of his heart is complete only when awe, trust, and work are gathered in it together.

And God is enough for us, and how excellent a Trustee He is.
