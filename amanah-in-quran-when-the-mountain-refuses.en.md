*The Qurʾan opens the meaning of trust (amānah) with a cosmic scene in which heavens, earth, and mountains shrink from a burden that man alone takes up, and with it the possibility of betrayal. Amānah is thus a tested deed, the keeping of what is entrusted exactly as it was entrusted, and it stands as the very condition of faith and of a messenger's credibility.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

God offers a trust (amānah) to the heavens, the earth, and the mountains, the greatest things in the visible creation in breadth, steadfastness, and solidity. All of them refuse to bear it, and shrink from it in dread:

﴿إِنَّا عَرَضْنَا الْأَمَانَةَ عَلَى السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَالْجِبَالِ فَأَبَيْنَ أَنْ يَحْمِلْنَهَا وَأَشْفَقْنَ مِنْهَا وَحَمَلَهَا الْإِنْسَانُ إِنَّهُ كَانَ ظَلُومًا جَهُولًا﴾ (33:72)
*"Indeed, We offered the Trust to the heavens, the earth, and the mountains, but they refused to bear it and were afraid of it; and man bore it. Indeed, he was ever unjust and ignorant."*

It is an awe-inspiring scene. The deep-rooted mountains, the far-stretching earth, and the lofty heavens all know the measure of this burden and shy away from it, while man, the slightest of them in bulk, steps forward and takes it up, though the very same verse calls him unjust and ignorant. This paradox, the refusal of the greatest and the acceptance of the frailest, is the gateway to understanding amānah in the Qurʾan.

### Delimiting the Word and Counting Its Occurrences

The root ʾ-m-n occurs in the Qurʾan eight hundred and seventy-nine times (879), which makes it very nearly the most frequent root in the entire Book. This vast number, however, comprises three distinct semantic branches that must be carefully told apart. By far the largest is īmān and "the believers" (the verb āmana 537 times, the active participle muʾmin 202 times, the verbal noun īmān 45 times, besides their feminine forms), a concept in its own right, with an article of its own, and not confined within this study. The second branch is amn, in the sense of safety and security from fear (the verb amina 20 times, the noun amn five times, āminīn ten times, and others). The branch that concerns us here is amānah in the sense of a deposit and an entrusted confidence. It is represented by the noun amānah in the singular twice (33:72, 23:8); the plural amānāt four times (4:58, 8:27, 23:8, 70:32); the adjective amīn fourteen times; and, once, the passive verb uʾtumina, "was entrusted" (2:283). These are twenty-one places that are the direct verbal witness to the concept of amānah, out of 879 occurrences of the root as a whole.

### The Linguistic Root: Three from a Single Origin

This linguistic kinship among īmān, amn, and amānah is no coincidence; it reflects a single semantic structure, for the root's origin turns on stillness, tranquility, and the absence of fear from some quarter. Amn is the stillness of one who no longer fears an outward enemy; īmān is the stillness and repose of the heart in affirming what is unseen; and amānah is the stillness that the holder of a right finds when he knows that his right is kept safe with another and will not be betrayed. All three meet in one meaning: freedom from the fear of treachery, whether the treachery of fate, of the soul against the truth, or of the trustee against what was deposited with him.

### The Central Structure: The Scene of the Cosmic Offering

Verse 33:72 is the axis upon which the whole meaning of amānah turns. When God offers the Trust to the heavens, the earth, and the mountains, the verse does not state explicitly what this Trust is. Hence the commentators diverged: some say it is the charge of obedience coupled with freedom of choice; others, the obligations and rulings of the law; others, the intellect by which man is held accountable. What all these views share is that the Trust here is a burden which entails responsibility and choice, and is no mere innate attribute. The mountains, for all their solidity, and the heavens, for all their breadth, "were afraid" of it, that is, they dreaded the consequence of bearing it, because they are subjugated creatures without choice and are not called to account for what they were never charged to choose. Man, by accepting the Trust, accepted with it the possibility of betrayal. That is the price of the choice by which he was distinguished from the rest of creation.

### A Qurʾanic Model: Trustworthiness as a Condition in Every Messenger

In Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ a striking scene recurs. Five prophets, Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lot, and Shuʿayb, address their peoples in precisely the same words:

﴿إِنِّي لَكُمْ رَسُولٌ أَمِينٌ﴾ (26:107, 125, 143, 162, 178)
*"Indeed, I am to you a trustworthy messenger."*

Before any of them states the substance of his message or calls his people to the worship of God, he first introduces himself by one attribute: trustworthiness. It is as though the Qurʾan affirms that a message's credibility rests first on trust in its bearer, and not on the force of his argument alone. Joseph is described by the same attribute when he is summoned to interpret the king's dream:

﴿إِنَّكَ الْيَوْمَ لَدَيْنَا مَكِينٌ أَمِينٌ﴾ (12:54)
*"Indeed, you are today established with us, and trusted."*

And Gabriel, too, is so described in the Qurʾan's portrayal of revelation:

﴿ثَمَّ أَمِينٍ﴾ (81:21)
*"…trustworthy."*

### Another Model: The Practical Definition of Amānah

As it did with patience, the Qurʾan does not leave amānah an abstract concept standing alone, but defines it in operational terms in the setting of a loan:

﴿فَإِنْ أَمِنَ بَعْضُكُمْ بَعْضًا فَلْيُؤَدِّ الَّذِي اؤْتُمِنَ أَمَانَتَهُ وَلْيَتَّقِ اللَّهَ رَبَّهُ﴾ (2:283)
*"But if one of you entrusts another, let him who is entrusted discharge his trust, and let him fear God, his Lord."*

When two men deal with each other on mutual trust, without writing or witnesses, a common situation of daily life, the Qurʾan does not leave the matter to good intention alone. It joins the actual discharge ("let him discharge") to the God-fearing of the heart ("and let him fear God, his Lord"), affirming that amānah, like patience and gratitude before it in this series, is an outward act that is put to the test, and not an inward sentiment that may be satisfied by a mere claim.

The story of Joseph offers a further model of the attribute amīn, in the setting of a practical trial. When the king asked that Joseph be brought out of prison, he did not come out at once, but first asked that the accusation of slander against him be investigated. When his innocence was established, the ʿAzīz described him with full confidence:

﴿إِنَّكَ الْيَوْمَ لَدَيْنَا مَكِينٌ أَمِينٌ﴾ (12:54)
*"Indeed, you are today established with us, and trusted."*

The attribute of trustworthiness, then, was not bestowed on Joseph for nothing. It was confirmed after years of patience, chastity, and truthfulness, so that it became a standing won by deed, and not a title tossed about at random.

### The Prophetic Witness

The Prophet ﷺ gathers faith and trust into one decisive sentence: «لَا إِيمَانَ لِمَنْ لَا أَمَانَةَ لَهُ، وَلَا دِينَ لِمَنْ لَا عَهْدَ لَهُ» — "There is no faith for one who has no trustworthiness, and no religion for one who has no covenant."[^1] This hadith does not merely link amānah to īmān on the level of language, as the shared root showed. It makes the former an existential condition of the latter. One who cannot be trusted cannot rightly be described as a believer, however loudly he claims it with his tongue, since faith, by its very linguistic origin, is a repose of the heart that cannot coexist with open betrayal. The Prophet ﷺ himself was known among his people, before his mission, by the title "al-Amīn" (the Trustworthy), so that even the idolaters who rejected his message used to leave their deposits in his keeping, trusting in him.

### A Reading in Light of the Higher Objectives (Maqāṣid)

It will be observed that the Qurʾan does not confine amānah to material deposits alone, but widens it to embrace everything with which a person is entrusted. It includes judgments and testimonies:

﴿إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَأْمُرُكُمْ أَنْ تُؤَدُّوا الْأَمَانَاتِ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهَا وَإِذَا حَكَمْتُمْ بَيْنَ النَّاسِ أَنْ تَحْكُمُوا بِالْعَدْلِ﴾ (4:58)
*"Indeed, God commands you to render trusts to those entitled to them, and when you judge between people, to judge with justice."*

It includes the secrets of war and sensitive information:

﴿يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَخُونُوا اللَّهَ وَالرَّسُولَ وَتَخُونُوا أَمَانَاتِكُمْ﴾ (8:27)
*"O you who believe, do not betray God and the Messenger, nor betray your trusts."*

And it includes even marital rights:

﴿وَالَّذِينَ هُمْ لِأَمَانَاتِهِمْ وَعَهْدِهِمْ رَاعُونَ﴾ (23:8, 70:32)
*"And those who are watchful over their trusts and their covenant,"*

in a passage that comes directly after the verses on guarding chastity. This breadth shows that amānah, in the Qurʾanic scheme, is no narrow juristic chapter concerned with financial deposits, but a governing principle that covers every instance in which a person is relied upon for something that belongs to another.

The commentators differed over the nature of the Trust offered in 33:72, in a difference that enriches the concept instead of confusing it. Some held it to be the obligations of the law in their entirety; others, the intellect, which is the ground of legal responsibility; and a third group, trusts in their common sense of deposits and rights among people. This plurality is no contradiction, but reflects the breadth of the word itself, for all these meanings share in being a burden which only the possessor of choice accepts, and for whose faithful discharge or neglect he is held to account. This is the heart of the meaning, and over it no one differs.

### The Contemporary Application

In an age when the circles of entrustment widen by the day, with personal data, passwords, trade secrets, and exacting professional responsibilities, the meaning of Qurʾanic amānah widens with them, though its essence does not change: that what a person has been entrusted with remain preserved as it was, neither exploited, nor divulged, nor neglected. The scene of the mountains that shrank from bearing the Trust reminds us that accepting a responsibility ought to be preceded by an honest awareness of its weight, and not by the rashness of one who takes it up supposing it a pure honor that costs nothing. It is wise to learn from the dread of the mountains before learning from the daring of man. To accept a trust is a virtue, but to accept it without reckoning its size is the very thing for which the verse describes man as "unjust and ignorant."

In the workplace, the principle of Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ, which puts trustworthiness before every other claim, serves as a practical criterion for choosing those to whom a matter is committed. Before asking about competence, experience, or eloquence, the Qurʾan asks first about trust. Many institutional failures today stem not from a lack of technical competence but from the absence of trustworthiness in the use of that competence. This is what the Qurʾan pointed to when it made "trustworthy" the description that precedes every other in the prophets' introduction of themselves.

### Conclusion

From a cosmic scene in which the deep-rooted mountains refused to bear the Trust, to a prophetic hadith that makes it the condition of faith itself, the Qurʾan draws for amānah one meaning that runs through all its forms: a trust deposited and kept as it was deposited, and not as the one entrusted with it may wish. Since man has borne what the heavens, the earth, and the mountains refused, it is fitting that he bear it with an awareness equal to the greatness of the One who offered it to him. And God, Exalted is He, knows best; He is the Guardian of success.
