*In the Qurʾan, mercy is a single circle ever widening: from the mother's womb, through the ties of kinship, to the divine mercy that encompasses all things. Whoever severs the narrowest of these rings cuts himself off from a branch of the widest.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

Abū Hurayrah (may God be pleased with him) reported that the Prophet ﷺ said: «إِنَّ الرَّحِمَ شِجْنَةٌ مِنَ الرَّحْمَٰنِ، فَقَالَ اللَّهُ: مَنْ وَصَلَكِ وَصَلْتُهُ، وَمَنْ قَطَعَكِ قَطَعْتُهُ» — “The womb (*raḥim*) is a branch (*shijnah*) of the Most Merciful (*al-Raḥmān*); and God said: Whoever keeps you joined, I shall keep joined to Me, and whoever severs you, I shall sever from Me”[^1]. The word *shijnah* originally denotes the tangled, interwoven roots of a tree, which cannot be pulled apart without injury to the very trunk from which they spring. The mother's womb, from which every human being enters the world (the first place he knows and the first warmth that enfolds him), thus bears the same root as the greatest of God's names, *al-Raḥmān*. This is no passing verbal resemblance. As the hadith itself declares, it is a true interweaving: the bond of kinship is a branch attached to the mercy of al-Raḥmān, and whoever cuts it has cut himself off from something of His mercy.

This linguistic interweaving of *raḥim* (the mother's womb) and *al-Raḥmān* (the most exalted name of God) is the key to understanding the concept of mercy throughout the Qurʾan.

### The Word and Its Count

The root r-ḥ-m occurs in the Qurʾan 339 times, in nine forms: twenty-eight times as the verb *raḥima*; twelve times as the noun *arḥām* (in the sense of physical wombs); four times as the elative *arḥam*; once as the noun *ruḥm*; fifty-seven times as the name *al-Raḥmān*, which is almost the only name the Qurʾan uses as a direct substitute for the supreme name “Allāh” itself («قُلِ ادْعُوا اللَّهَ أَوِ ادْعُوا الرَّحْمَٰنَ» — “Say: Call upon God, or call upon al-Raḥmān,” 17:110); one hundred and fourteen times as the noun *raḥmah*; one hundred and sixteen times as the adjective *raḥīm*; once as *marḥamah*; and six times as the active participle *rāḥimīn*. The forms *raḥmah* and *raḥīm* alone account for two hundred and thirty occurrences, more than two-thirds of the whole, which makes this one of the most frequently recurring moral and doctrinal roots in the Qurʾan.

### The Linguistic Root: Womb and Mercy from a Single Source

It is striking that the very root which yields *raḥmah* and *al-Raḥmān* also yields *al-arḥām*, the physical wombs in which the child is formed: «هُوَ الَّذِي يُصَوِّرُكُمْ فِي الْأَرْحَامِ كَيْفَ يَشَاءُ» — “It is He who shapes you in the wombs as He wills” (3:6); and «اللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ مَا تَحْمِلُ كُلُّ أُنْثَىٰ وَمَا تَغِيضُ الْأَرْحَامُ» — “God knows what every female bears, and what the wombs diminish” (13:8). The bond between mercy and the womb is therefore no late exegetical invention but an original linguistic reality, confirmed by the Prophet ﷺ in the hadith cited above. Kinship takes its name from the name of God, al-Raḥmān, because in essence it is a vestige of His mercy, branching out from it, and not a social virtue standing apart from religion.

### The Central Structure: A Circle Widening from the Womb to All That Mercy Encompasses

The Qurʾan draws for mercy a circle of ever-increasing breadth, beginning at the narrowest compass and ending at the widest. The first ring is the mothers' wombs, where every human life begins, wrapped in a care over which the child has no power whatever. The second is the bond of kinship (*al-raḥim*), whose severance the Qurʾan forbids: «فَهَلْ عَسَيْتُمْ إِنْ تَوَلَّيْتُمْ أَنْ تُفْسِدُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَتُقَطِّعُوا أَرْحَامَكُمْ» — “Would you then, if you turned away, perhaps spread corruption in the land and sever your ties of kinship?” (47:22). The third is God's mercy toward His servants at large, which the Qurʾan presents as a self-imposed obligation and not an occasional favor: «كَتَبَ رَبُّكُمْ عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِ الرَّحْمَةَ» — “Your Lord has prescribed mercy upon Himself” (6:54). The verb *kataba* (“prescribed”) carries the force of an irrevocable legal obligation, as though God had made mercy binding upon Himself as a lawgiver binds others, in honor of His servants and not out of any need of them. The widest ring of all is what God affirms for Himself in His converse with the angels: «وَرَحْمَتِي وَسِعَتْ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ» — “And My mercy encompasses all things” (7:156). Nothing that exists lies beyond this last circle.

### A Qurʾanic Model: Mercy as the Very Character of the Message

The Qurʾan is not content to describe God as merciful. It describes the Prophet's mission itself by mercy, in the most concise verse imaginable: «وَمَا أَرْسَلْنَاكَ إِلَّا رَحْمَةً لِلْعَالَمِينَ» — “We have sent you only as a mercy to all the worlds” (21:107). Mercy here is not one attribute of the Messenger among others; it is the reason for his sending and its all-embracing purpose, extending to all the worlds and not to the believers alone. Elsewhere the Qurʾan describes itself in the same terms: «وَنُنَزِّلُ مِنَ الْقُرْآنِ مَا هُوَ شِفَاءٌ وَرَحْمَةٌ لِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ» — “We send down of the Qurʾan that which is a healing and a mercy for the believers” (17:82). The Book that carries injunctions and limits is described, first of all, as mercy, and not merely as legislation.

### Another Model: The Most Merciful of the Merciful on the Lips of the Afflicted

Three prophets, each in the hour of his sharpest anguish, turn specifically to the name *Arḥam al-Rāḥimīn* (“the Most Merciful of the merciful”): Jacob, on losing his sons («وَأَنْتَ أَرْحَمُ الرَّاحِمِينَ» — “and You are the Most Merciful of the merciful,” 7:151); Joseph, when judgment upon his brothers is left to him («وَهُوَ أَرْحَمُ الرَّاحِمِينَ» — “and He is the Most Merciful of the merciful,” 12:64); and Job, when affliction touches him («أَنِّي مَسَّنِيَ الضُّرُّ وَأَنْتَ أَرْحَمُ الرَّاحِمِينَ» — “Affliction has touched me, and You are the Most Merciful of the merciful,” 21:83). The superlative is not summoned in a moment of ease. It is summoned when every possible human mercy has failed, when the one who prays understands that none surpasses all the merciful but God alone. This accords with the widening circle drawn above: when the nearer rings of mercy (family, kin, friends) contract, the sufferer may still take refuge in the ring that never contracts.

Elsewhere the Qurʾan joins mercy directly to marriage, making it, beside affection, one of the pillars of conjugal repose: «وَجَعَلَ بَيْنَكُمْ مَوَدَّةً وَرَحْمَةً» — “And He placed between you affection and mercy” (30:21). Affection is a natural inclination that may arise without any cause. Mercy is the persistence in giving and forbearance even when that inclination dims or cools, and this explains why the Qurʾan pairs the two words rather than resting content with either alone.

### The Prophetic Witness

The hadith “the womb is a branch of the Most Merciful” is consonant with another scene the Prophet ﷺ related concerning God's vast mercy: that God divided mercy into a hundred parts, sent down one part among His creatures, by which they show mercy to one another (so that the mare lifts her hoof from her foal lest she trample it), and kept back ninety-nine parts, with which He will show mercy to His servants on the Day of Resurrection[^2]. This hadith makes plain that every mercy a person witnesses in this world (a mother's, a father's, even an animal's toward its young) is but one part in a hundred of a divine mercy far more vast, which agrees perfectly with the breadth of the last circle drawn by the verse «وَرَحْمَتِي وَسِعَتْ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ».

### A Further Scene: A Wall Whose Inner Side Is Mercy

The Qurʾan paints for the Day of Resurrection a scene that gives mercy an almost architectural form: a wall raised between the believers and the hypocrites, «لَهُ بَابٌ بَاطِنُهُ فِيهِ الرَّحْمَةُ وَظَاهِرُهُ مِنْ قِبَلِهِ الْعَذَابُ» — “with a gate, whose inner side holds mercy, while its outer side faces the punishment” (57:13). Mercy here is not an abstract state of feeling but a place that one truly enters, with an inner side in which one takes shelter from whoever stands outside. This embodiment parallels what was said in an earlier article in this series about the *sakīnah* that is “sent down”: the Qurʾan speaks of both realities in language that is nearly tangible, not as abstractions that remain confined to the heart.

### A Maqāṣidī Reading

It has been observed that *al-Raḥmān* is the only name coupled with *al-Raḥīm* at the opening of every sura of the Qurʾan but one. Many scholars have seen in this pairing a pointer to two kinds of mercy: a general *raḥmāniyyah*, which embraces all creation, believer and unbeliever alike, in this world (as in sustenance, air, and sunlight), and a special *raḥīmiyyah*, reserved for the believers in the Hereafter. Though the exegetes differ over its details, this distinction is consistent with the structure of the verses themselves. When God's mercy is said to have “encompassed all things,” the context is that of the general *raḥmāniyyah*; when mercy is named as the condition of entering Paradise («فَسَيُدْخِلُهُمْ رَبُّهُمْ فِي رَحْمَتِهِ» — “their Lord will admit them into His mercy,” 45:30), it belongs to the special *raḥīmiyyah*.

### The Contemporary Application

The linguistic bond between *raḥim* and *al-Raḥmān* reminds us that estrangement from one's relatives is no mere social failure but, as the hadith declares, a severing from a vestige of God's own mercy. In an age when families drift apart across continents and the ties of kinship are cut on the pretext of busyness or old quarrels, this bond is a reminder that upholding kinship is not an optional social courtesy but a living extension of one of the names of God. And whoever finds himself today in the place of Jacob or Job (in an anguish for which he finds no merciful helper among creatures) is reminded by the Qurʾan that the last circle of mercy, which encompasses all things, is never closed, however narrow the nearer circles may grow.

The breadth of the Qurʾanic circle of mercy, from the womb to the worlds, also summons the one who practices mercy within its narrowest rings (his own family and relatives) not to stop there, but to carry it outward to the wider circles the Qurʾan drew for the Message itself: a mercy to all the worlds, and not to one household alone. Finally, the fact that the Message and the Qurʾan are both described by mercy (and not by wisdom or knowledge alone) reminds everyone who bears the burden of teaching, calling to God, or leading that the first measure of his message's success is not merely the accuracy of what it conveys, but the degree of visible mercy it bears toward those it addresses, believers and unbelievers alike, just as the verse “a mercy to all the worlds” is restricted to no one group.

### Conclusion

From the womb of the mother, from which every human being is born, to a mercy God prescribed for Himself, to a mercy that encompasses all things, the Qurʾan draws mercy as one widening circle and not as separate circles. Whoever severs the narrowest of them, the bond of his kinship, has, by the very word of the hadith, cut himself off from a branch attached to the widest. And God Most High knows best; with Him is all success.
