*Tawbah is a return, not merely a sorrow, and in the Qurʾan it is a meeting of two turnings: the servant turns back to his Lord, and God has already turned toward the servant, so that His grace is what sets the heart in motion.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

A man was in a barren wilderness, with no companion and no water, and he lost his mount, on which lay all his food and drink. He searched for it until the search wore him out, and gave it up in utter despair. Then he went to a tree and lay down in its shade, waiting for death to come. And while he lay there, behold, his mount stood before him, with his food and drink upon it just as he had left them. He seized its halter, and his joy reached a pitch beyond all bearing, so that his tongue slipped in the violence of his delight, and he said: «اللَّهُمَّ أَنْتَ عَبْدِي وَأَنَا رَبُّكَ»,[^1] "O God, You are my servant, and I am Your Lord!"

Such is the joy, beyond all description, that the Messenger of God ﷺ portrays; and then he says: «لَلَّهُ أَشَدُّ فَرَحًا بِتَوْبَةِ عَبْدِهِ حِينَ يَتُوبُ إِلَيْهِ، مِنْ أَحَدِكُمْ كَانَ عَلَى رَاحِلَتِهِ...»,[^1] "God is more joyful at the repentance of His servant when he turns to Him than one of you who was upon his mount…" This vivid scene, related by Anas ibn Mālik, is the finest doorway into a Qurʾanic concept that is commonly thought to move in one direction, but is in truth a movement from two sides.

### The Linguistic Ground: A Return, Not Mere Remorse

In the language of the Arabs the root t-w-b denotes "returning," and this is its original sense, one that never leaves it in any of its derivatives. *Tāba* means "he returned," and *tawbah* is a return from one state to another. This differs from what springs to mind when the concept is rendered by the English word "repentance," which carries only shades of remorse and sorrow.

Remorse is, in Qurʾanic tawbah, one of its conditions (as the hadith has it: «النَّدَمُ تَوْبَةٌ», "Remorse is repentance"),[^2] but it is not tawbah itself. Tawbah is the actual movement of returning, and not the feeling that accompanies it. The penitent servant does not rest content with regretting what he has done; he truly turns back from what he was doing to what pleases his Lord.

### The Occurrences: A Single Structure That Does Not Branch

The root t-w-b occurs in the Qurʾan eighty-seven times, in seven forms: the verb *tāba* and its conjugations sixty-three times, by far the greater part; the feminine active participle *tāʾibāt* once (66:5); the verbal noun *tawb* once (40:3); the noun *tawbah* seven times; *matāb* twice; the plural participle *tāʾibūn* once (9:112); and the intensive form *tawwāb* twelve times, eleven of them as an attribute of God and once in the plural describing repentant servants: ﴿إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ التَّوَّابِينَ﴾ ("Indeed, God loves those who constantly repent," 2:222).

Unlike other roots in this series, such as *amānah*, which became entangled with a far wider root, the root t-w-b is clearly bounded here: every occurrence is a direct lexical witness to the concept of tawbah, with no need to separate it from a neighboring sense.

### The Central Structure: The Same Verb in Both Directions

Here lies the secret of this concept. The verb *tāba* is used not of the servant alone but of God as well, in the very same linguistic form. When the servant returns to his Lord, it is said, *tāba*; and when God turns back to His servant with acceptance and forgiveness, it is likewise said, *tāba ʿalayhi*, "He turned toward him."

Adam, the first from whom disobedience came and the first from whom repentance came, received words from his Lord and returned by them, and the Qurʾan tells what happened:

﴿فَتَلَقَّىٰ آدَمُ مِن رَّبِّهِ كَلِمَاتٍ فَتَابَ عَلَيْهِ إِنَّهُ هُوَ التَّوَّابُ الرَّحِيمُ﴾ (2:37)

*"Then Adam received words from his Lord, and He turned toward him. Truly, He is the Ever-Returning (al-Tawwāb), the Most Merciful."*

Notice that the verse does not say "and Adam repented," but "and He turned toward him": the one who turned, in the apparent form of the verb here, is God. In the Qurʾan, then, tawbah is not a single movement in one direction, but two turnings that meet: the servant's turning, and God's turning toward His servant.

For this reason "al-Tawwāb" is among the names of God that most often occur in connection with this root, eleven times. It is an intensive form that signifies repetition: God does not turn toward His servant once and then withdraw; each time the servant returns, God returns toward him, however often the sins are repeated.

### The Priority of Grace: When God Moves First

More wondrous still, in one particular place the Qurʾan discloses an order, both temporal and logical, between the two turnings which the reader does not expect. It occurs in the story of the three who were left behind from the expedition of Tabūk, Kaʿb ibn Mālik among them, when the earth, for all its vastness, closed in upon them, and their own souls closed in upon them. Of God's turning toward them and toward the Prophet ﷺ He says:

﴿لَقَدْ تَابَ اللَّهُ عَلَى النَّبِيِّ وَالْمُهَاجِرِينَ وَالْأَنصَارِ الَّذِينَ اتَّبَعُوهُ فِي سَاعَةِ الْعُسْرَةِ...﴾ (9:117) ﴿...ثُمَّ تَابَ عَلَيْهِمْ لِيَتُوبُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ هُوَ التَّوَّابُ الرَّحِيمُ﴾ (9:118)

*"God has turned in mercy toward the Prophet and the Emigrants and the Helpers who followed him in the hour of hardship… Then He turned toward them, that they might turn back. Truly, God is the Ever-Returning, the Most Merciful."*

Consider the particle *li-* in *li-yatūbū*, "that they might turn back." It is the *lām* of purpose and consequence, and it means that God's turning toward them was the cause that led to their own turning, and not the reverse. The verse does not say, "They turned, so God turned toward them"; it says, "God turned toward them, so that they turned."

This overturns a common notion, namely that the servant begins with remorse and return, and God answers him afterward. The verse shows the grace of God as prior: it is He who awakens in the servant's heart that first movement toward returning, as a gift of His guiding favor and not as something the servant has earned. And this is precisely what the linguistic fact conveys: one verb serves both directions, yet one direction, the grace of God, is what sets the other in motion.

### The Temporal Limit: A Repentance Whose Door Is Closed in Time

For all the breadth of this grace, the Qurʾan has not left the door open without bound; it has drawn for it a clear limit in time:

﴿إِنَّمَا التَّوْبَةُ عَلَى اللَّهِ لِلَّذِينَ يَعْمَلُونَ السُّوءَ بِجَهَالَةٍ ثُمَّ يَتُوبُونَ مِن قَرِيبٍ فَأُولَٰئِكَ يَتُوبُ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ...وَلَيْسَتِ التَّوْبَةُ لِلَّذِينَ يَعْمَلُونَ السَّيِّئَاتِ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا حَضَرَ أَحَدَهُمُ الْمَوْتُ قَالَ إِنِّي تُبْتُ الْآنَ﴾ (4:17-18)

*"Acceptance of repentance by God is only for those who do evil in ignorance and then repent soon after; those, God turns toward them… But repentance is not for those who go on doing evil deeds until, when death comes to one of them, he says, 'Now I repent.'"*

The words *min qarīb*, "soon after," do not necessarily mean that the interval between the sin and the repentance must be short, whether in hours or in days. The majority of the commentators took it to mean any time before one sees death face to face and before the angel of death descends, however long the span may be. Yet the verse does close the door for good at two moments: at the sight of death, and in persistence in unbelief until death. Tawbah is thus a return available so long as life lasts, and not an eternal opportunity without condition.

### From Evil Deeds to Good Deeds: An Effect That Does Not Stop at Erasure

The Qurʾan is not content to describe tawbah as the blotting out of sin; it goes further in speaking of "sincere repentance":

﴿يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا تُوبُوا إِلَى اللَّهِ تَوْبَةً نَّصُوحًا﴾ (66:8)

*"O you who believe, turn to God in sincere repentance."*

This is a repentance that resembles sound counsel (*naṣīḥah*) in its truthfulness and purity, and leaves the servant no room to go back to what he has turned from. In another place the Qurʾan describes its effect in terms that surpass mere "pardon" and reach "transformation":

﴿إِلَّا مَن تَابَ وَآمَنَ وَعَمِلَ عَمَلًا صَالِحًا فَأُولَٰئِكَ يُبَدِّلُ اللَّهُ سَيِّئَاتِهِمْ حَسَنَاتٍ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ غَفُورًا رَّحِيمًا﴾ (25:70)

*"Except for one who repents, believes, and does righteous work: for those, God will change their evil deeds into good deeds. And God is Ever-Forgiving, Most Merciful."*

Forgiveness erases, but transformation converts the very place of the evil deed into a good deed in the servant's record. This is among the most astonishing meanings of tawbah in the Qurʾan, for it makes the past of which the servant is ashamed a ground for his hope and not for his despair, once his repentance is sound.

### The Prophetic Witness

The scene with which this article opened, that of the lost mount in the desert, was related by Anas ibn Mālik and recorded by Imam Muslim in his *Ṣaḥīḥ* (no. 2747).[^1] It is among the most eloquent of hadiths in bringing God's joy at His servant's repentance within reach of immediate human feeling: a joy surpassing that of a man who had despaired of life itself and then found it suddenly restored to him, together with everything he held dear.

In another hadith, likewise related by Anas, recorded by al-Tirmidhī (no. 2499), Ibn Mājah (no. 4251) and Aḥmad in his *Musnad*, and graded ḥasan by al-Albānī:

«كُلُّ بَنِي آدَمَ خَطَّاءٌ، وَخَيْرُ الْخَطَّائِينَ التَّوَّابُونَ»[^3]

*"Every son of Adam is prone to error, and the best of those who err are those who repent often."*

This hadith connects directly with the Qurʾan's own wording, in its description of the believers' attributes: ﴿التَّائِبُونَ الْعَابِدُونَ الْحَامِدُونَ...﴾ (9:112), "Those who repent, who worship, who praise…" The Prophet ﷺ makes "the penitent" the best of those among the children of Adam who err. Among them the measure of excellence is not freedom from error, but swiftness of return after error.

### From the Sayings of the People of Knowledge

Ibn al-Qayyim (may God have mercy on him), in his book *Madārij al-Sālikīn*, classifies repentance according to the station of the one who repents. The repentance of the common believers is from outward sins; that of the elect is from heedlessness of God in every state, so that some of the gnostics even said, "The good deeds of the pious are the evil deeds of those brought near," on account of the shortfall they contain from the perfection of presence with God.

Imam al-Ghazālī, in *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn*, relates repentance to what he calls "knowledge," "state," and "deed." Knowledge of the harm of sin produces the "state," which is pain and remorse; and the state in turn impels to the "deed," which is leaving the sin, resolving, and making amends.

Ibn Rajab al-Ḥanbalī, in *Jāmiʿ al-ʿUlūm wa-l-Ḥikam*, brings out the comprehensive aspect of its higher purposes when he says that repentance combines three pillars, none of which is valid without the others: desisting from the sin at once, remorse for what is past, and resolve not to return to it in the future. If the sin concerns the right of another human being, a fourth pillar is added: restoring the right or seeking its owner's release.

### Contemporary Application

Many who are weighed down today by a sense of guilt are good at remorse but do not know how to return. They remain within the circle of self-reproach without taking the practical step toward change, so that the sin stays present in the conscience with no real repentance to end it.

The Qurʾanic lesson here is twofold. On the one hand, the servant should not wait until he feels fully worthy before he returns; for God's grace, as in the story of Kaʿb ibn Mālik and his two companions, is what first moves the heart toward returning. Let him therefore hasten the moment he finds that stirring in himself, for it comes from God and not from him. On the other hand, he should not procrastinate in reliance on the vastness of mercy, for the door has a limit in time which no one knows.

In the field of education and daʿwah, this concept offers an alternative to the culture of "perfection or nothing," which drives those who err to despair and impels them to persist in error out of hopelessness of being accepted. When a person is offered the choice of being "infallible" or "a sinner who repents," he finds in this Prophetic hadith room to breathe, one that brings him back to the road rather than driving him from it.

### Conclusion

From the journey of a man who lost his mount in a desolate wilderness, to Adam receiving words in the first repentance humanity ever knew, to Kaʿb ibn Mālik and his two companions in the earth that narrowed for all its breadth, the Qurʾan draws one picture. Repentance is not a movement the servant begins alone, in a single direction toward his Lord, but two turnings that meet, the priority of one of them a grace from God that no desert can earn.

And whoever knows that his return to his Lord is nothing but an answer to a return that preceded it, from his Lord toward him, finds it easy to return, and does not hold back from it for fear of being charged with a fault; for the best of those who err, as the Messenger of God ﷺ said, are those who repent. And success is by God, and God knows best.
