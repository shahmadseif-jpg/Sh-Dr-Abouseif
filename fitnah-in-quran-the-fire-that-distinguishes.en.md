*Fitnah, in the Qurʾan, is a single fire seen in many forms: the goldsmith's fire that separates pure metal from alloy, the fire into which the believers of the Trench were cast, and the fire of the Hereafter. Its purpose is never to create what is in a person but to disclose it, so that the truthful are made manifest and the false are exposed.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

Trenches were dug in the earth and fire was kindled in them. The believers of that town were brought forward and given a choice: either to renounce their religion or to be cast into that blazing pit. They did not renounce it. They were thrown in, watching the fire devour those who went before them, and they stood firm and unmoved. This is the story of the People of the Trench, which the Qurʾan relates in Sūrat al-Burūj, and the deed of those who kindled that fire it describes with one word and no other:

﴿إِنَّ الَّذِينَ فَتَنُوا الْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَالْمُؤْمِنَاتِ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَتُوبُوا فَلَهُمْ عَذَابُ جَهَنَّمَ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابُ الْحَرِيقِ﴾ (85:10)
*"Indeed, those who put the believing men and believing women to the trial (fatanū) and then did not repent will have the punishment of Hell, and they will have the punishment of the Burning."*

Here fatanū is a past-tense verb set in the place of the gravest of crimes, and we shall not search long before discovering that the choice of word is no accident. The root f-t-n, in its linguistic origin, is a real fire before it becomes a metaphor, and for the People of the Trench the fire was no metaphor at all, but a fact.

### Sixty Occurrences in Five Forms

Scholars count f-t-n among the Qurʾan's most widely attested words and the most varied in meaning. The root recurs sixty times in five forms: the verb fatana and its conjugations (twenty-three times); the noun fitnah (thirty-four times); the noun futūn (20:40); the active participle fātinīn (37:162); and the passive participle maftūn (68:6).[^1] This breadth in number is matched by a breadth in meaning. For that reason this article, unlike the two before it in this series, does not survey every occurrence one by one, but follows the single thread that gathers all this variety, citing the most prominent examples in each of its forms.

### The Goldsmith's Fire

Before following that thread through its sixty occurrences, we must pause at its first source. The people of the language say that the origin of fitnah is to put gold or silver into the fire so that its quality may be told from its dross; one says "fatantu al-dhahab" when one melts gold in the fire to separate the pure from the alloyed. The Arabs then widened the use of the word until it embraced everything by which a person is tried and by which his truthfulness is told from his falsehood. Ibn al-Athīr says in his definition: "Fitnah: the trial and the test… and its origin is taken from 'I assayed the silver and the gold,' when you melt them in the fire so that the inferior is distinguished from the good." He added that fitnah is the greatest degree of testing.[^2] The word, then, in its origin, describes neither bare evil nor bare good, but a process of disclosure: a fire that does not make the metal, but reveals what purity or adulteration was in it from the first.

Here it is fitting to distinguish fitnah from its two sisters, balāʾ (affliction) and imtiḥān (examination). The three are close in general meaning but are not entirely synonymous. Balāʾ is more general: it covers everything with which a person is afflicted, good or evil, without necessarily carrying the sense of disclosing what is hidden, as in the words

﴿وَنَبْلُوكُمْ بِالشَّرِّ وَالْخَيْرِ فِتْنَةً﴾ (21:35)
*"And We test you with evil and with good as a trial (fitnah)."*

Here fitnah is made the aim of the affliction, and not its synonym; that is, We afflict you in order to try you. Fitnah is more specific, since it always carries the sense of melting the outer to disclose the inner. That is why the word is used only where there is the possibility of a hidden reality being laid bare, whether truthfulness or falsehood, sincerity or hypocrisy. This is what makes it, as Ibn al-Athīr described it, the greatest degree of testing, and no mere passing examination.

### One Fire in Five Forms

This single metaphor, the goldsmith's fire, gathers the five forms among which fitnah is distributed in the Qurʾan. At first glance they seem scattered, but on reflection they are one fire taking five shapes.

The first is the fitnah of ease: a person is tried by what he loves, not by what he dislikes, such as wealth and children:

﴿وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّمَا أَمْوَالُكُمْ وَأَوْلَادُكُمْ فِتْنَةٌ﴾ (8:28)
*"And know that your wealth and your children are but a trial (fitnah)."* (The same words recur in 64:15.)

The second is the fitnah of hardship: a person is tried by what he dislikes, such as fear, poverty, and sickness, as in 21:35 above.

The third is the fitnah of persecution: a believer is forced to abandon his religion through harm and torture, as befell the People of the Trench, and as the emigrants are described:

﴿مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا فُتِنُوا﴾ (16:110)
*"…after they had been persecuted (futinū)."*

The fourth is the fitnah of division: a rift and strife among the Muslims themselves that threatens their unity. The Qurʾan calls it

﴿أَشَدُّ مِنَ الْقَتْلِ﴾ (2:191)
*"…more grievous than killing,"*

and indeed

﴿أَكْبَرُ مِنَ الْقَتْلِ﴾ (2:217)
*"…greater than killing."*

The fifth, which closes the circle, is the fitnah of the punishment itself. When the first four come to their outcome in the Hereafter, the word returns to its first origin, the real fire. It will be said to the people of the Fire on the Day of Resurrection:

﴿ذُوقُوا فِتْنَتَكُمْ﴾ (51:14)
*"Taste your trial (fitnah)."*

And the disbelievers are described as those

﴿يَوْمَ هُمْ عَلَى النَّارِ يُفْتَنُونَ﴾ (51:13)
*"…on the Day they will be tried (yuftanūn) over the Fire."*

The word that began as a metaphor drawn from the goldsmith's fire ends as a reality in the fire of Hell, and between these two ends pass all the kinds of trial that a person undergoes in his life.

### Where the Forms Intersect

Though the five forms are read separately in most places, other passages gather more than one of them, and even disclose a sixth aspect not named in the division above: fitnah as training, not punishment. Some passages present fitnah neither as punishment nor as mere disclosure, but as a discipline that prepares its bearer for a great mission to which these trials were the only road. When God addresses Moses after choosing him for the message, He reminds him of a chain of trials that came before the choice, not after it: the killing of a soul, flight in fear, and years of exile among the people of Madyan, all of it under a single word:

﴿وَقَتَلْتَ نَفْسًا فَنَجَّيْنَاكَ مِنَ الْغَمِّ وَفَتَنَّاكَ فُتُونًا فَلَبِثْتَ سِنِينَ فِي أَهْلِ مَدْيَنَ﴾ (20:40)
*"And you killed a soul, and We saved you from distress, and We tried you with a severe trial (futūnan). Then you remained for years among the people of Madyan."*

Moses was not chosen for the message in spite of those trials, but after they had refined and prepared him for it. In this lies a correction of a common assumption that sees fitnah as an obstacle to being chosen, when it is sometimes the only road to it.

Other passages join more than one form at once. The story of Solomon (peace be upon him):

﴿وَلَقَدْ فَتَنَّا سُلَيْمَانَ وَأَلْقَيْنَا عَلَىٰ كُرْسِيِّهِ جَسَدًا ثُمَّ أَنَابَ﴾ (38:34)
*"And We certainly tried Solomon and placed on his throne a body; then he turned back in repentance,"*

and before him that of David:

﴿وَظَنَّ دَاوُودُ أَنَّمَا فَتَنَّاهُ فَاسْتَغْفَرَ رَبَّهُ﴾ (38:24)
*"And David realized that We had tried him, so he asked forgiveness of his Lord,"*

show that fitnah does not strike the disobedient alone, but reaches the prophets themselves in a form befitting their rank, so that it becomes a cause of further turning to God and not of deviation. Against this stands a verse at the opening of Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt that lays down a general rule governing all fitnah:

﴿أَحَسِبَ النَّاسُ أَنْ يُتْرَكُوا أَنْ يَقُولُوا آمَنَّا وَهُمْ لَا يُفْتَنُونَ * وَلَقَدْ فَتَنَّا الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ فَلَيَعْلَمَنَّ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ صَدَقُوا وَلَيَعْلَمَنَّ الْكَاذِبِينَ﴾ (29:2-3)
*"Do the people think that they will be left to say, 'We believe,' and not be tried? We certainly tried those before them, and God will surely make evident those who are truthful, and He will surely make evident the liars."*

A mere claim is not enough. Fitnah is what shows, exactly as the goldsmith's fire does, who was truthful in his claim and who was false in it.

### The Fitnah of This Community

If the Qurʾan established that fitnah is a universal way from which no community escapes, the Prophet ﷺ singled out this community in particular with a warning that came for no other in such explicit terms. Among the most widely circulated hadiths on this subject is his saying ﷺ: «إِنَّ لِكُلِّ أُمَّةٍ فِتْنَةً، وَفِتْنَةُ أُمَّتِي الْمَالُ» — "Indeed, every community has a fitnah, and the fitnah of my community is wealth." Al-Tirmidhī narrated it and said: a good and sound hadith, and al-Albānī authenticated it.[^3]

In naming wealth specifically as this community's fitnah there is a direct link to the first form of Qurʾanic fitnah, the fitnah of ease, and a prophetic warning against supposing that fitnah exists only in hardship and affliction. The gravest danger this religion faces, once its domain widens and its blessings multiply, is the fitnah of riches, not the fitnah of poverty. The precision of the hadith lies in this: every community has its own fire by which it is tried, and not the fire of another. A community that came before may have been tried by hunger or oppression; this community, to whom the world was opened and whose wealth grew after scarcity, was given its greatest test in what it was given and not in what it was denied. This explains why warnings against wealth abound in texts after the conquests and expansion more than in the early texts of oppression in Mecca.

### Why Fitnah?

In his commentary on the opening verses of al-ʿAnkabūt, Ibn al-Qayyim makes clear that the wisdom of fitnah is disclosure, not punishment. When a person claims faith, God tries him to make manifest whether he is truthful in his claim or false. If he was false, he turns back at the first test, like one fleeing punishment; if he was truthful, he stands firm and even grows in faith.[^4] This is precisely the meaning of God's words elsewhere:

﴿وَإِنْ أَصَابَتْهُ فِتْنَةٌ انْقَلَبَ عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِ﴾ (22:11)
*"But if a trial (fitnah) befalls him, he turns back on his face."*

The turning back is the sign of one whose faith was adulterated gold, which only the fire exposed.

From another angle, that of the higher objectives, the exegetes established the meaning of the verse

﴿وَقَاتِلُوهُمْ حَتَّىٰ لَا تَكُونَ فِتْنَةٌ وَيَكُونَ الدِّينُ لِلَّهِ﴾ (2:193)
*"Fight them until there is no fitnah and the religion is for God,"*

as follows: the permission to fight in Islam was never for compelling anyone to enter it, but for lifting fitnah, in the sense of persecution and coercion to abandon the religion, from the believers. The aim of lawful fighting is to remove fitnah, not to impose it. This turns on its head a common charge against Islam: the very verse sometimes cited as proof that "Islam spread by the sword" is the one that establishes that the aim of fighting is to end religious coercion, not to impose it.

From a third angle, Ibn Taymiyyah spoke of fitnah in the context of the general law of affliction. God does not try His servants in vain or out of vengeance, he said, but for a wisdom followed by a reward and an elevation unattainable without it. Patience through fitnah is thus a cause of loftier rank, and not merely deliverance from its punishment. This explains the hadith in which Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ asked the Prophet ﷺ which people are most severely tried. He said: «الْأَنْبِيَاءُ، ثُمَّ الْأَمْثَلُ فَالْأَمْثَلُ، يُبْتَلَى الرَّجُلُ عَلَى حَسَبِ دِينِهِ» — "The prophets, then the next best, then the next best; a man is tried according to his religion."[^5] The intensity of trial upon the prophets is no punishment. The more severe the fitnah, the deeper the disclosure it brings about, and the greater the reward that follows.

### Which Fire Are We In?

This fivefold division gives us a lens for reading our own reality. Many who take notice of the fitnah of hardship, when struck by illness, loss, or constriction, are wholly heedless of the fitnah of ease against which the hadith specifically warned. They suppose that their safety from the first is proof of deliverance from fitnah altogether, while abundance of wealth and absorption in the world may be their real fitnah, unnoticed because it does not hurt as poverty hurts. Many who take notice of the fitnah of open persecution, plain harm on account of religion, are heedless of the fitnah of division that the Qurʾan described as graver than killing. They plunge into quarrels within a single rank, unaware that they are kindling the very fire they were warned of, though with a different hand. And the fitnah of belief, following the ambiguous verses in pursuit of their interpretation (3:7), takes today the shape of a swift stream of media and intellectual discourse that presents doubt in the garb of certainty, so that none can tell the pure from the adulterated save one who knew the root before the branch was set before him.

The question this article poses, then, is not "Am I in a fitnah?" The answer is always yes, since no one is without some fire by which he is tried. The question is: "Which fire am I in now, and do I know that I am in it?" The story of Moses adds a further dimension. Some of what we take today to be an obstacle on our path, a failure, an exile, or years that seemed lost, as Moses may have thought of his years in Madyan, may be the very training that prepares us for what we cannot yet imagine. The effect of fitnah is measured not by its severity alone, but by what it makes of its bearer when he looks back on it after years.

### Conclusion

From the goldsmith's fire that tells pure gold from alloyed, to the fire of the Trench into which the first believers were cast, to the fire of the Hereafter whose people are told "Taste your trial," the word fitnah passes through five forms of a single fire, and its aim in all of them is one: that the truthful be made manifest from the false, and the pure from the adulterated. God will not leave people to say "We believe" without being tried; He will surely make evident those who are truthful, and He will surely make evident the liars. And God, Exalted is He, knows best; He is the Guardian of success.
