*Qurʾanic yaqīn is not a single state seized all at once, but a journey by ascending degrees, from knowledge to sight to lived truth; its measure is not the heat of conviction but its correspondence to reality, and its fruit is steadfastness in the hour of trial.*

By Dr. Ahmed Muhammad Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy (AIA)

Yaqīn, certainty: a word that many take to be one indivisible state, in which the heart either comes to rest and is at peace, or fails to settle and hangs suspended between doubt and conjecture. Yet when the Qurʾan speaks of yaqīn, it does not describe a single state grasped in one stroke, but successive degrees, as though the road to certainty were itself a journey and not a halting place. In the distance between the common notion of certainty and the true Qurʾanic certainty lies most of our error in understanding a word we repeat constantly without realizing that it has a precise structure. Indeed, the Qurʾan itself warns against a counterfeit certainty, which its owner takes for knowledge when it is conjecture in disguise.

### The Word in the Qurʾan: Noun and Verb

Let us begin where every linguistic inquiry begins: with the count. The root y-q-n occurs in the Noble Qurʾan in twenty-eight places, fourteen in verbal form (*yūqinūn*, *yastayqinūn*, *mūqinīn*) and fourteen in nominal form (*al-yaqīn*), and in all these places its sense does not go beyond four meanings.

The first and most frequent is "assent": ﴿وَبِالْآخِرَةِ هُمْ يُوقِنُونَ﴾ (2:4), "and of the Hereafter they are certain," meaning that they affirm the reality of the Hereafter and the resurrection, with parallels in several places (27:3; 31:4; 32:24; 45:4, 20). The second is "death," limited to just two places: ﴿وَاعْبُدْ رَبَّكَ حَتَّىٰ يَأْتِيَكَ الْيَقِينُ﴾ (15:99), "and worship your Lord until certainty comes to you," and ﴿حَتَّىٰ أَتَانَا الْيَقِينُ﴾ (74:47), "until certainty came to us." The third is "seeing with one's own eyes and direct witnessing," in *ʿilm al-yaqīn*, "the knowledge of certainty," and *ʿayn al-yaqīn*, "the eye of certainty" (102:5, 7), together with the related *ḥaqq al-yaqīn*, "the truth of certainty" (56:95; 69:51). The fourth is pure "knowledge" as opposed to conjecture, which has a single striking witness, to be mentioned below.

Yaqīn has a fifth use, narrower but worth noting: "certain tidings" (*al-nabaʾ al-yaqīn*), as when the Qurʾan reports the hoopoe telling Solomon, peace be upon him, about Sheba: ﴿وَجِئْتُكَ مِن سَبَإٍ بِنَبَإٍ يَقِينٍ﴾ (27:22), "and I have come to you from Sheba with sure news," that is, a firmly established report that admits no doubt. This use is a reminder that yaqīn in the Qurʾan is not a quality confined to the great matters of belief; it extends to every report that must be verified before it is passed on. It is a methodological principle in which the Qurʾan anticipated what is known today as the verification of sources.

This distribution itself carries a meaning. In most of its occurrences yaqīn is a quality that is sought and acquired (﴿وَلِيَكُونَ مِنَ الْمُوقِنِينَ﴾, "and that he might be among those who have certainty"), and not a state a person is born with ready-made. That alone overturns the common notion of certainty as a gift bestowed or withheld without any striving.

### The Linguistic Root, and a False Certainty the Qurʾan Warns Against

If yaqīn is a quality sought and acquired, and not a ready-made gift, the reason is that its very linguistic root carries the sense of movement rather than initial rest. In the language, *yaqīn* is the opposite of doubt. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī said that it is "the stillness of understanding together with the fixity of judgment," and Ibn ʿĀshūr said that it is "decisive knowledge that admits no doubt." The difference between knowledge and certainty is that knowledge may be accompanied by inner hesitation despite its soundness, whereas certainty is knowledge that has reached such firmness that not doubt alone, but even disquiet, becomes impossible.

For this reason the Qurʾan chose this very word to name death in the two verses cited above. Death is the one event in which there is room for no doubt whatever, however much a person may doubt everything else. It is a compulsory certainty that comes to a person whether he wills it or not, in contrast to the voluntary certainty of faith, which the heart strives toward and which is never imposed upon it.

But the Qurʾan does not stop there. In one unique verse it declares that personal conviction alone is not called certainty unless it corresponds to the truth. God says of the killing of Jesus, peace be upon him:

﴿وَمَا قَتَلُوهُ وَمَا صَلَبُوهُ وَلَٰكِن شُبِّهَ لَهُمْ ۚ ... وَمَا قَتَلُوهُ يَقِينًا﴾ (4:157)

*"They did not kill him, nor did they crucify him, but it was made to appear so to them… and they did not kill him for certain."*

Ibn ʿAbbās, may God be pleased with him, explained that the pronoun in "they killed him" refers to conjecture and not to Jesus, so that the meaning is: they did not put their conjecture to death with certainty. That is, they were wholly convinced that they had killed him, took their conviction for certainty, and it was in truth only a deluded conjecture.

This verse alone demolishes the common confusion between the intensity of conviction and the soundness of certainty. Conjecture may reach such psychological firmness that its owner imagines it certainty, while the truth is the very opposite. True certainty, then, is not the intensity of a feeling, but the correspondence of knowledge to reality.

### The Three Degrees: From Knowledge to Sight to Truth

Once it is settled that true certainty is correspondence with reality and not intensity of feeling, the next question is how this correspondence is reached. The answer lies in a progression laid out consecutively in Sūrat al-Takāthur: ﴿كَلَّا لَوْ تَعْلَمُونَ عِلْمَ الْيَقِينِ﴾ (102:5), "No! If only you knew with the knowledge of certainty," then ﴿لَتَرَوُنَّهَا عَيْنَ الْيَقِينِ﴾ (102:7), "you will surely see it with the eye of certainty," and it is completed by the verses of al-Wāqiʿah and al-Ḥāqqah with "the truth of certainty."

These are not three synonyms for one thing, but three entirely different, ascending ways of knowing, none of which makes the others unnecessary. The commentators gave an illustration from fire. To know of its existence from seeing smoke rise is *ʿilm al-yaqīn*: a sound rational inference, but an indirect one. To see the fire with your own eye is *ʿayn al-yaqīn*: direct witnessing that dispenses with inference. To enter the fire and be burned by it is *ḥaqq al-yaqīn*: an immersion and lived experience that no viewing from afar can resemble.

This same progression applies exactly to the certainty of faith. The knowledge of certainty concerning the Hereafter is what the believer attains by examining evidence and proofs. The eye of certainty is a higher degree, when the heart brings the Hereafter to mind so vividly that it approaches witnessing, as in the definition of iḥsān: «أَنْ تَعْبُدَ اللَّهَ كَأَنَّكَ تَرَاهُ»,[^1] "to worship God as though you see Him." The truth of certainty is the highest degree, when knowledge so mingles with the heart that it becomes conduct and not information, so that nothing stands between what the servant knows and what he does. Whoever is content with the first degree and takes it for the goal of certainty has compressed a long road into a passing halt.

This progression has a practical consequence that goes beyond theory. One who knows the prohibition of usury by proof alone (knowledge of certainty) may fall into it when oversight slackens. One who brings God's displeasure with it to mind so vividly that it approaches sight (eye of certainty) becomes ever more careful. One whose knowledge has so mingled with him that it has become nature and not effort (truth of certainty) needs no reminder at every transaction. The difference, then, lies not in the soundness of the belief but in the depth of its rooting. This is why even a scholar who knows the ruling may sometimes disobey it: he may possess the knowledge of certainty without having reached its eye or its truth.

### Those Described as Being Among the Certain

These three degrees are not merely a theoretical abstraction; they are a road that a particular human being walked before the Qurʾan set it down as a rule. The active participle *al-mūqinīn* occurs in connection with one name in particular, Abraham, peace be upon him:

﴿وَكَذَٰلِكَ نُرِي إِبْرَاهِيمَ مَلَكُوتَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَلِيَكُونَ مِنَ الْمُوقِنِينَ﴾ (6:75)

*"And thus We showed Abraham the dominion of the heavens and the earth, that he might be among those with certainty."*

The context is his watching the stars, the moon, and the sun set one after another before his heart came to rest upon the Lord of the worlds. It is striking that certainty did not come to Abraham all at once, but after a journey of observation and repeated refutation, as though the Qurʾan were applying to his own story the three degrees we have described.

Though the verse in which Abraham asks to be shown how the dead are given life does not use the word "certainty" itself, its context is closely related:

﴿رَبِّ أَرِنِي كَيْفَ تُحْيِي الْمَوْتَىٰ ۖ ... وَلَٰكِن لِّيَطْمَئِنَّ قَلْبِي﴾ (2:260)

*"My Lord, show me how You give life to the dead… but that my heart may be at rest."*

It is a request to move from the knowledge of certainty, which he already held in faith, to the eye of certainty: the request of a prophet with no doubt in him, but with a longing for the higher rank.

The other occurrence of the participle describes a company and not an individual: the leaders of the Children of Israel, of whom God said:

﴿جَعَلْنَا مِنْهُمْ أَئِمَّةً يَهْدُونَ بِأَمْرِنَا لَمَّا صَبَرُوا وَكَانُوا بِآيَاتِنَا يُوقِنُونَ﴾ (32:24)

*"We made from among them leaders who guided by Our command when they were patient and held Our signs with certainty."*

The Qurʾan joins patience with certainty here in a striking way, as though certainty could not take root except where patience takes root, and as though patience were itself one of the fruits of certainty and not a separate virtue.

### The Witness of the Sunnah: Certainty as an Energy of Steadfastness, Not Inert Knowledge

If the Qurʾan paired certainty with patience in the example of the Children of Israel, the Prophetic Sunnah spells out this pairing in practice. It is not content to state certainty as a theoretical concept; it binds it to practical steadfastness under trial. Among its testimonies is his ﷺ counsel to Ibn ʿAbbās, which includes: «وَاعْلَمْ أَنَّ مَا أَخْطَأَكَ لَمْ يَكُنْ لِيُصِيبَكَ، وَمَا أَصَابَكَ لَمْ يَكُنْ لِيُخْطِئَكَ»,[^2] "And know that what has missed you was never going to befall you, and what has befallen you was never going to miss you." This counsel does not address the intellect with an abstract truth; it addresses the heart in the very moment of trial, to steady it.

Another is the supplication that the Prophet ﷺ used to make for his Companions:

«اللَّهُمَّ اقْسِمْ لَنَا مِنْ خَشْيَتِكَ مَا تَحُولُ بِهِ بَيْنَنَا وَبَيْنَ مَعَاصِيكَ، وَمِنْ طَاعَتِكَ مَا تُبَلِّغُنَا بِهِ جَنَّتَكَ، وَمِنَ الْيَقِينِ مَا تُهَوِّنُ بِهِ عَلَيْنَا مَصَائِبَ الدُّنْيَا»[^3]

*"O God, apportion to us such fear of You as will stand between us and disobedience to You, and such obedience to You as will bring us to Your Paradise, and such certainty as will make the calamities of this world light for us."*

Here it is stated outright that the practical function of certainty is to lighten calamities, and not merely to fix a creed in place. In the guidance of prophethood, certainty is an energy spent in the moment of testing, not knowledge stored up for debate.

### The Sayings of the Scholars: Certainty as a Station, Not a Halt

This bond between certainty and conduct, traced by the Sunnah in practice, is what the scholars afterward set out in theory. Ibn al-Qayyim, in *Madārij al-Sālikīn*, devoted a whole station to certainty, counting it the very goal of the travelers on the path and not a passing stage along the way, and he affirmed that the people of certainty differ in rank in it just as they do in knowledge and action together, and not in knowledge alone. This accords with the three degrees themselves: whoever is confined to the knowledge of certainty has not yet reached what the possessor of the truth of certainty has reached, though both share the root of assent. It has also been related of some of the verifying scholars that "certainty is to faith what the spirit is to the body," and that when patience and certainty come together they bring forth leadership in religion, just as the verse of al-Sajdah itself declares.

From another side, Imam al-Ghazālī, in *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn*, made certainty the root of all acts of worship, of the heart and of the body alike, for no outward act is sound unless it springs from inward certainty. The humility of prayer comes from the worshipper's certainty that he stands before One who sees him, and the generosity of almsgiving from the giver's certainty that God guarantees the replacement. In this al-Ghazālī meets Ibn al-Qayyim from a different angle: both make certainty a foundation and not a branch, though they differ in expression. One describes it as a station to which one ascends, the other as a root upon which all else is built.

### The Portrait of the Person of Certainty Today

If certainty is for these two Imams a foundation and not a branch, how much does the man of today need to reclaim that very foundation. Contemporary man lives through a crisis of methodical doubt such as he has never known before: a philosophical relativism that calls every fixed point into question, and a flood of information that bewilders more than it guides, until many doubt the very possibility of certainty, to say nothing of attaining it.

It is precisely in this setting that Qurʾanic certainty regains its worth. It is not a refusal of questions or a freezing of the mind, but a goal reached by the very progression that Sūrat al-Takāthur laid out: inference, then witnessing, then immersion. The person of certainty today is not one who holds ready answers to every question, but one who has a steadiness of heart that holds firm before questions without collapsing; who binds his certainty to his patience as the Qurʾan binds the two in the verse of al-Sajdah; who neither awaits a certainty without trial nor takes trial for a refutation of certainty.

Furthest from true certainty is the one who takes the strength of his own conviction as proof of its soundness, and the verse of al-Nisāʾ has already dispelled that illusion. The measure is not the warmth of feeling but the correspondence of the heart to the truth, even if that correspondence comes only after a long journey of questioning and not in one moment of enthusiasm.

### Conclusion

This progression brings us back to where we began: certainty is not a single state that is either seized all at once or not at all, but a journey from inference to witnessing to immersion, which the heart travels degree by degree, as Abraham, peace be upon him, traveled it, passing from a star that set, to a moon that set, to a sun that set, until he came to rest upon Him who never sets. And so long as a person remains in this world, the door to passing between the degrees stays open: what is lacking in knowledge is made good by witnessing, and what is lacking in witnessing is made good by immersion, until the final certainty comes, after which there is no further passage.

And whatever peace the heart has reached in this world remains short of that final certainty, after which there is no passage; and God knows best what has come to rest in every heart.
