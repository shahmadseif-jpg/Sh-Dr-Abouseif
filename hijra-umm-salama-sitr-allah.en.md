*Lessons from the Hijra on building a home on trust and on God's protective care, drawn from the family of Abū Salama and Umm Salama, may God be pleased with them. By Dr. Ahmed Mohamed Ali Abouseif, President of the American Imams Academy.*

We usually think of the Hijra as a story of sacrifice: people leaving behind their wealth and their homes, and risking their lives for their faith. That is all true. But one family's story from the Hijra opens up another lesson that is just as deep. It shows us that the Hijra was also a school for strong marriages, and a clear sign of how God protects His servants, sending whatever help He wills to guard a person's honor and dignity. This is the family of Abū Salama ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAbd al-Asad and his wife Umm Salama, Hind bint Abī Umayya.

Two things come together in this family's story that rarely appear side by side: a sacrifice that breaks your heart, and a trust between husband and wife that holds firm even when life tears the family apart and scatters it across the land. Through both, a larger truth comes into view: when a person is sincere with God, God takes it upon Himself to protect them — even if that protection comes through a man who did not yet believe.

## A Family Before It Was a Journey

Abū Salama was no minor figure among the Companions of the Prophet ﷺ. He was the Prophet's cousin through his aunt Barra bint ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, and also his foster brother, since both had been nursed by the same woman, Thuwayba. He was one of the very first people to accept Islam, one of the first to migrate to Abyssinia, and later among the first — some reports say the very first — to migrate to Medina.

Umm Salama came from one of the most respected families in Quraysh. Her father, Abū Umayya ibn al-Mughīra, was famous across Arabia for his generosity; people called him "the Traveler's Provision" because whoever traveled with him never had to bring his own food. She grew up with wealth and status, and she gave all of it up for her faith and her husband.

When we look at how this couple migrated, we don't see two people who happened to share a road. We see a home built on mutual trust: a husband who knew his wife's worth, and a wife who knew her husband's standing in the faith. That trust is the key to the whole story — and it was about to face a test that only a home with a solid foundation could survive.

It is worth noting that both husband and wife belonged to the same clan, Banū Makhzūm, one of the most powerful and wealthy clans in Quraysh. So they were not running away from weakness in search of power. They were walking away from real power and status for something that would last longer. They left behind their standing, their reputation, and the protection of their clan, and chose a life far from home with no clan and no status — carrying nothing on that road except their faith and their trust in each other. That alone tells you what this family was made of, before the journey even began.

## A Family Torn Apart in Front of Him

Abū Salama decided to migrate. He put his wife and young son on a camel and set out for Medina. But when the men of Banū al-Mughīra — Umm Salama's relatives — saw him, they blocked his way. "You can do what you like with yourself," they said, "but this woman is one of ours. Why should we let you take her off into the world?" They pulled the camel's reins out of his hand and took her away.

That made Abū Salama's own relatives, Banū ʿAbd al-Asad, furious. "If you're taking her from our man," they said, "then we're not leaving our boy with her." The two sides fought over little Salama, pulling him back and forth until they dislocated his arm, and Abū Salama's family took him. Abū Salama went on to Medina alone. In a single hour, the family was split three ways: the husband in one place, the child in another, and the mother torn between them.

Umm Salama later described those days: "Every morning I would go out and sit at al-Abṭaḥ, and I would cry until evening — for a year, or close to it." A whole year of tears, out in the open, with no control over her own situation — only a patience she had to find again every morning. We don't need to add anything to this scene; it speaks for itself. A woman from one of the most honored families in Quraysh, sitting outside for a year, waiting for a way out she could not see.

Then relief came from the same place the hardship had come from. A man from her own extended family passed by, felt sorry for her, and told her relatives: "Why don't you let this poor woman go? You've separated her from her husband and her son!" So they told her, "Go join your husband if you want." And Abū Salama's family gave her son back. This account is recorded in Ibn Isḥāq's biography of the Prophet as transmitted by Ibn Hishām, and Ibn Kathīr included it in *al-Bidāya wa-l-Nihāya*; a number of scholars have considered its chain of narration sound.

## A Husband Who Knew His Wife's Worth

A quick reader might blame Abū Salama: how could he go on to Medina and leave his wife and child behind with those people? But when we look at the situation more carefully, a deeper meaning appears. He was not abandoning them. He was obeying the command to migrate after every other door had been shut, and he was trusting a woman whose character he knew well.

A husband who is secure in his marriage knows his wife's worth. He knows she has the emotional strength and the good judgment to protect herself and his honor, even when she is surrounded by pressure and hardship. Abū Salama could not protect his wife in person; he had been physically kept away from her. But he was at peace knowing that she was protected by her own faith and her own good sense. That is a rare and high kind of trust. Only a husband who has built his home on respect for his partner — not on control over her — ever reaches it. He trusted her with his honor just as he trusted her with her faith.

And Umm Salama lived up to that trust completely. For a full year, alone and heartbroken, her faith never wavered, and having no one on her side never wore down her patience. When the chance finally came to join him, she didn't hesitate for a moment. She set out across the desert by herself with her little boy. A woman who can hold on for a year and then head into the unknown alone, with that kind of steadiness, proves by her actions that every bit of her husband's confidence was deserved.

This corrects a common idea that a woman is kept safe only by walls and guards. What protects a woman most is what she carries inside herself — her faith, her intelligence, her integrity — not the restrictions men place around her. Abū Salama understood this. His peace of mind did not depend on keeping hold of her; it depended on knowing who she was. That is the difference between a husband who watches his wife because he doesn't trust her, and a husband who is at ease because he knows she respects herself too much to compromise. The first may control her movements but lose her heart; the second has her heart, and that is all the protection he needs.

## The Escort God Sent Her

Umm Salama left Mecca on her camel with her son, just the two of them, headed for Medina — several days away, across a land where no traveler was safe, let alone a woman alone with a small child. When she reached al-Tanʿīm, just outside Mecca, she met ʿUthmān ibn Ṭalḥa, who at the time was still an idol-worshipper and had not yet accepted Islam. "Where are you going, daughter of Abū Umayya?" he asked. "To my husband in Medina," she said. "Is anyone with you?" She replied, "No, by God — no one but God and this little boy of mine." He said, "By God, I'm not going to leave you on your own."

He took her camel's reins and began leading her. Umm Salama later said: "By God, I never traveled with any Arab man more honorable than him. Whenever we reached a stop, he would have my camel kneel, then step away. Once I had gotten down, he would take the camel off, unload it, and tie it to a tree. Then he would go lie down under another tree, away from me. When it was time to move on, he would get up, bring my camel, and load it, then step back and say, 'Get on.' Once I was settled, he would come back, take the reins, and lead me on until the next stop." He kept doing this all the way to Qubāʾ, on the edge of Medina. Then he said, "Your husband is in this village. Go in, with God's blessing." And he turned around and headed back to Mecca.

Think about this scene in terms of God's protection. Here is a believing woman in the most vulnerable situation a person can face: alone, far from home, with no one to protect her. And what did God do? He sent her a protector from somewhere she never would have expected — from the very people who opposed her faith. He was a man of Quraysh who still worshipped idols, yet God placed a deep sense of decency in him and used it to protect her. He never came close, never asked prying questions, never watched her as she got on or off her camel. He led the camel, then stepped aside; had it kneel, then moved away — until he had brought her safely to her destination and gone home. This is the protection God promises those who are close to Him. A person doesn't need to have the means of protection in hand; it is enough to be sincere, and God will take care of the rest and send whatever help He wills:

> ﴿وَلِلَّهِ جُنُودُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ﴾ [الفتح: ٧]
>
> "To God belong the forces of the heavens and the earth." (al-Fatḥ 48:7)

And the story comes full circle. This man, who protected a believing woman's honor while he was still an idol-worshipper, was later honored by God with Islam. On the day Mecca was opened, the Prophet ﷺ gave him back the key to the Kaʿba and made his family its permanent keepers, saying to them:

> «خُذُوهَا خَالِدَةً تَالِدَةً، لَا يَنْزِعُهَا مِنْكُمْ إِلَّا ظَالِمٌ»
>
> "Take it, to keep forever. No one will take it from you except a wrongdoer."

The man who protected the dignity of one of God's servants was made a guardian of God's own House. God rewards a deed with something of the same kind.

## What This Means for Us Today

We badly need to bring this family's story out of the history books and into our own lives. We live in a time full of ways to keep people "safe" — phones, tracking apps, locked doors — and yet it is a time when trust between husbands and wives is surprisingly fragile. Many homes today fall apart not because of poverty, displacement, or persecution, but because of suspicion, constant checking up on each other, and a lack of confidence that one's spouse can be trusted.

This is where the example of the early Muslims speaks directly to us. Abū Salama's family was torn apart physically in ways most of our homes will never experience — a year apart, a child with a dislocated arm, a desert crossed alone — and yet the family came out of it stronger than before. Why? Because what held it together was not being physically close, but trust between hearts and sincere reliance on God. The husband trusted and did not belittle her. The wife protected herself and did not let him down. And God protected them both and did not let them be shamed. How different that is from homes that have every means of closeness but lack the most basic trust.

The story also holds a lesson for Muslim women, just as important as the lesson for men. Umm Salama was not a passive figure waiting for someone to save her. She acted, and she was patient. She held on when holding on was the only thing she could do; she moved the moment a door opened; and she headed into the unknown trusting her Lord — not a companion, not a guard. A woman who carries that kind of faith is exactly the woman a husband should trust, and exactly the one God takes under His protection. God's protection is earned through sincerity. It does not come through helplessness or simply waiting.

In the end, real protection is not a lock on a door. It is what grows out of being sincere with God. Whoever is sincere with God, He protects — even out in the open desert. And whoever betrays the basic trust they were given, He exposes — even behind seven locked doors.

## More Signs of This Family's Honor

The honor of this family was not a single moment on the road to Medina. It stayed with them for the rest of their lives.

When Abū Salama later died in Medina — from a wound he had received at the Battle of Uḥud that reopened — Umm Salama grieved deeply for the husband who had been among the very first to migrate. She then recalled something he had told her he heard from the Prophet ﷺ:

> «مَا مِنْ مُسْلِمٍ تُصِيبُهُ مُصِيبَةٌ فَيَقُولُ مَا أَمَرَهُ اللَّهُ: ﴿إِنَّا لِلَّهِ وَإِنَّا إِلَيْهِ رَاجِعُونَ﴾، اللَّهُمَّ أْجُرْنِي فِي مُصِيبَتِي، وَأَخْلِفْ لِي خَيْرًا مِنْهَا — إِلَّا أَخْلَفَ اللَّهُ لَهُ خَيْرًا مِنْهَا»
>
> "Whenever a Muslim is struck by a loss and says what God has told him to say — 'We belong to God, and to Him we will return. O God, reward me for my loss and give me something better in its place' — God will give him something better in its place."

This is recorded by Muslim in his *Ṣaḥīḥ* (918). Umm Salama said: "When Abū Salama died, I said it. But then I thought to myself, 'Who could be better than Abū Salama?' And then God gave me His Messenger ﷺ in his place." The Prophet ﷺ married her, and she became one of the Mothers of the Believers. What greater honor could a family receive than for God to replace its loss with the Messenger of God himself? It is the most complete fulfillment of the words "give me something better in its place."

Her wisdom — which shows just how right her first husband was to trust her — also appears clearly at al-Ḥudaybiya. When the Prophet ﷺ told his Companions to sacrifice their animals and shave their heads, they held back, overwhelmed by how hard the terms of the treaty had been on them. The Prophet ﷺ came to Umm Salama troubled. She gave him wise advice: go out, don't say a word to anyone, sacrifice your own animal and call for someone to shave your head — and the people will follow your example. It happened exactly as she said. The people got up and began sacrificing and shaving one another's heads. This is recorded by al-Bukhārī in his *Ṣaḥīḥ* (2731). The same woman whose judgment Abū Salama trusted to guard his honor was the woman whose advice the Prophet ﷺ trusted in one of the most difficult moments the Muslim community ever faced. Her husband's trust had been well placed all along.

## What We Take Away

There are two lessons here that no Muslim can do without. The first is that the Hijra was a sacrifice made for faith — people gave up their lives and their families for it — and that whoever is sincere in such a sacrifice never truly loses; God gives them something better than what they left behind. The second is that this family is a model of a stable marriage that neither time nor hardship can shake, because it was built on mutual trust: a confident husband who knew his wife's worth, a faithful wife who never let him down, and above them both a Lord who protects His servants and sends them help from wherever He wills — even turning an idol-worshipper into the protector of a believing woman's honor, and then rewarding him by making him a keeper of His House.

If you want God's protection, be sincere with Him. If you want a home that storms cannot knock down, build it on trust, not suspicion. No one can expose what God has chosen to protect, and no amount of time can tear down what has been built on reverence for God.

And God knows best. He alone grants success, and there is no lord but Him.
